# Anaklia Deepwater Port

The ADC project represents the most extensively documented, most internationally financed, and most consequential infrastructure failure at Anaklia. It consumed a decade of effort, attracted multiple international partners, mobilized billions in planned investment, and ended only after legal arbitration upheld its cancellation.

## Project Vision and Scope

In 2016, the Georgian government, under the renewed Georgian Dream administration, launched a new attempt to build a deepwater container port at Anaklia. This effort was distinct from Lazika in structure, governance, and international partnership. [[3]](https://www.fpri.org/article/2020/10/georgias-doomed-deep-sea-port-ambitions-geopolitics-of-the-cancelled-anaklia-project/) [[4]](https://emerging-europe.com/business/what-now-for-anaklia-georgias-largest-infrastructure-project-as-blame-game-heats-up/)

The **project scope** was ambitious:
- A deepwater container port with three berths, capable of handling Panamax and Post-Panamax vessels
- Port capacity of 600,000 to 900,000 TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units) annually in full development
- 17.5-meter draft depth, enabling large modern container ships to berth
- Integrated Special Economic Zone (SEZ) to attract export-oriented manufacturing and logistics businesses
- Modern cargo-handling equipment and terminal infrastructure

This was Georgia's first planned deepwater port, intended to serve as the country's primary gateway for containerized cargo and to position Anaklia as a hub on the "Middle Corridor"—the emerging alternative to Russian and traditional Suez-routed trade flows between Central Asia, the Caucasus, and Europe.

## Consortium Structure and International Partners

The project was organized as a public-private partnership. The **Anaklia Development Consortium (ADC)** brought together a multinational group of investors and operators:

**Equity partners:**
- TBC Holding (Georgia) – The largest privately held conglomerate in Georgia
- Conti International (United States)
- SSA Marine (United States) – A major port operator and terminal management company
- Wondernet Express (United Kingdom)
- G-Star Ltd (Bulgaria)

**Construction and specialized contractors:**
- Eiffage (France) – General contractor, major global construction and civil engineering firm
- Van Oord (Netherlands) – World leader in dredging and maritime engineering; contracted to handle seabed preparation and dredging operations
- ZPMC (China) – Shanghai Zhenhua Port Machinery Company, supplier of modern container-handling cranes and specialized port equipment
- Hyundai Samho Heavy Industries (South Korea) – Major shipbuilding and offshore construction firm

**Financing institutions:**
- EBRD (European Bank for Reconstruction and Development)
- OPIC (Overseas Private Investment Corporation, United States)
- ADB (Asian Development Bank)
- AIIB (Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank)

This financing structure reflected Georgia's Western orientation at the time, with European and American development finance institutions as primary supporters, complemented by Asian multilateral institutions.

## Financial Commitments and Construction Timeline

For Phase 1 of the project, the consortium mobilized **\$620 million in financing commitment**. [[6]](https://politicsgeo.com/article/80) The total project cost across all phases was estimated at **\$2.5 billion**. [[10]](https://eurasianet.org/georgia-pledges-to-revive-anaklia-port-project-take-controlling-share)

Construction and site preparation began in December 2017, with **Van Oord initiating dredging operations** to begin seabed excavation and preparation. [[5]](https://www.dredgingtoday.com/2018/09/18/van-oord-kicks-off-anaklia-port-development-works/) This marked the first serious, funded, internationally-contracted port construction work at Anaklia. The physical work proceeded: specialized dredging vessels arrived on-site, environmental assessments were conducted, and earthmoving commenced.

ADC invested approximately **\$75 million in preparatory and construction work** before the project was terminated. [[9]](https://www.politicsgeo.com/article/80) This was not insignificant capital at risk, representing the consortium's commitment to the project's viability.

## The Financial Breakdown and Core Problem

The consortium's fundamental vulnerability lay in a specific financial gap. The project required:
- An immediate \$120 million capital injection from the consortium partners
- A \$400 million loan facility to fund construction

Despite securing broad international support and multilateral institution buy-in, the consortium **failed to close these critical financing tranches**. The reasons were never explicitly detailed in public statements, but the effect was clear: the project lacked complete financing certainty at a critical juncture.

## Government Termination and Stated Rationale

On **January 9, 2020**, the Georgian government terminated its investment agreement with the Anaklia Development Consortium. [[9]](https://www.politicsgeo.com/article/80) The government's stated justification was that the consortium had **failed to meet its financial obligations**, specifically the inability to secure the required \$120 million capital injection and the \$400 million loan. [[10]](https://eurasianet.org/georgia-pledges-to-revive-anaklia-port-project-take-controlling-share)

This was technically accurate: the financing was not complete. However, the context matters for understanding whether this was purely a financial default or a political decision using financial grounds as the stated rationale.

## Geopolitical and Political Context

The timing and context of the cancellation suggest that purely financial reasons do not fully explain the government's decision. Several factors were simultaneously at play:

**Russian Geopolitical Pressure:** Russia has historically opposed Western-oriented infrastructure projects in the South Caucasus, viewing them as strengthening Georgia's integration with Europe and NATO. A deepwater Georgian port, potentially serving as a conduit for NATO-aligned shipping and trade, threatened Russian strategic interests in the Black Sea region.

**U.S. Strategic Concern Over Chinese Involvement:** Although the ADC consortium was U.S.-led through OPIC and private partners, ZPMC's role as the equipment supplier introduced Chinese state-owned enterprise involvement. The Trump administration, particularly sensitive to Chinese Belt and Road expansion in strategic regions, viewed Chinese port involvement with suspicion. This created a diplomatic tension: Western financing institutions versus Chinese equipment and construction participation.

**Domestic Political Dynamics:** The Georgian Dream government, while pro-Western rhetorically, had also cultivated relationships with Russia and was sensitive to Russian pressure. The June 2018 meeting of the Georgian Dream party's political council reportedly discussed the project's vulnerabilities, though detailed records of these deliberations are not publicly available. [[11]](https://jam-news.net/anaklia-port-project-story/)

By late 2019 and early 2020, the project faced mounting pressure from multiple directions. The government cited financial non-performance, but the decision occurred within a charged geopolitical environment where Russia had incentive to block the project, where U.S.-China competition was intensifying, and where Georgia's government was navigating competing pressures from Russia and the West.

## International Arbitration: 2024 Resolution

The termination was not the end of the legal story. The Anaklia Development Consortium initiated international arbitration proceedings under ICSID (International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes), the World Bank-affiliated tribunal for investor-state disputes. The case proceeded through 2020–2024.

In **July 2024**, the ICSID arbitration tribunal issued its decision: **the Georgian government had the legal right to terminate the contract**. [[9]](https://www.politicsgeo.com/article/80) The tribunal found that while the government's stated rationale (failure to secure financing) was legitimate, it upheld the termination as legally permissible under the investment agreement. Crucially, **the tribunal awarded no compensation** to the consortium or its constituent companies for the project termination or the \$75 million in stranded investment.

This represented a significant legal victory for the Georgian government but also a final, definitive closure to the ADC project. The arbitral tribunal's decision essentially foreclosed any possibility of reviving the ADC model or compelling the government to compensate consortium members for the project's abandonment.

## What Ended and What Remained

The **ADC project ended completely**: no operational port resulted, no Special Economic Zone was established, no terminals were constructed. The dredging equipment was removed. The consortium structure dissolved. International contracts were terminated.

What remained were:
- The site itself, still capable of port development
- Conceptual and regulatory groundwork (environmental assessments, regulatory approvals for the site)
- Institutional memory within the companies and development finance institutions of what had been attempted
- The legal precedent from the arbitration tribunal

The physical landscape was largely returned to its pre-construction state, with some environmental modification from dredging activities but no permanent built structures.

## Organizational Failure vs. Technical Feasibility

A critical distinction: the ADC failure was **not a technical or engineering failure**. The project's concept was sound. Eiffage, Van Oord, ZPMC, and Hyundai Samho are capable contractors. The multilateral financing institutions believed the project was economically viable. The consortium structure itself was not flawed.

The failure was **institutional and political**: the consortium could not sustain financing in the face of geopolitical headwinds, the government found the political cost of continued support too high, and Russia's interests and U.S.-China tensions created an environment hostile to the project's completion. When the financing proved incomplete, the government had sufficient justification to terminate, and the political incentives aligned toward cancellation.
