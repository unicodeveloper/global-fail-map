# Vioxx

## What Was Attempted

Merck initiated the APPROVe trial (Adenomatous Polyp Prevention on VIOXX) in approximately 2000 as a Phase III prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical study [[33]](https://info.groupbenefits.org/docs/OGBforms/News/2004/MerckAnnouncement.pdf). The trial enrolled 2,600 patients with a documented history of colorectal adenomas (precancerous polyps). The treatment protocol assigned patients to either rofecoxib 25 mg daily or placebo for 156 weeks (approximately 3 years), with the primary objective of measuring efficacy in preventing recurrence of colorectal adenomas [[33]](https://info.groupbenefits.org/docs/OGBforms/News/2004/MerckAnnouncement.pdf).

VIOXX (rofecoxib) was a selective cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitor, a novel class of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug designed to reduce gastrointestinal toxicity compared to traditional NSAIDs while preserving anti-inflammatory efficacy. The compound had achieved worldwide commercial success: VIOXX generated \$2.5 billion in sales in 2003 and was marketed in over 80 countries [[33]](https://info.groupbenefits.org/docs/OGBforms/News/2004/MerckAnnouncement.pdf). The polyp prevention indication would have expanded the addressable market and extended VIOXX's patent life through combination indication exclusivity.

## Why It Mattered

VIOXX represented Merck's flagship pain and inflammation franchise during the late 1990s and early 2000s. The drug was marketed to millions of patients with osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and acute pain conditions. Establishing efficacy in colorectal polyp prevention would have positioned the drug as a dual-benefit therapy for patients at risk for both inflammatory disease and colorectal cancer. The financial stakes were substantial.

## Clinical Outcome and Termination

The trial was terminated in September 2004 after interim analysis revealed cardiovascular safety signals that contradicted the trial's primary efficacy objective. After 18 months of treatment, patients randomized to VIOXX 25 mg demonstrated a statistically significant increase in confirmed cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction or stroke) compared to placebo: 3.5% in the VIOXX arm versus 1.9% in the placebo arm (p < 0.001) [[33]](https://info.groupbenefits.org/docs/OGBforms/News/2004/MerckAnnouncement.pdf). Notably, the first 18 months of the trial showed no increased cardiovascular risk; the signal emerged after the 18-month timepoint, suggesting a dose-duration relationship [[33]](https://info.groupbenefits.org/docs/OGBforms/News/2004/MerckAnnouncement.pdf).

## Geographic Connection to Whitehouse Station

Merck announced the voluntary worldwide withdrawal of VIOXX in an official press release dated September 30, 2004, issued from the company's headquarters in Whitehouse Station, New Jersey [[33]](https://info.groupbenefits.org/docs/OGBforms/News/2004/MerckAnnouncement.pdf). The announcement was signed by Raymond V. Gilmartin (Chairman and CEO) and Peter S. Kim (President of Merck Research Laboratories), both acting from corporate headquarters [[33]](https://info.groupbenefits.org/docs/OGBforms/News/2004/MerckAnnouncement.pdf).

## What Ended and Why

VIOXX was completely withdrawn from worldwide markets. Importantly, the decision was not mandated by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration; rather, Merck stated in the press release that "a voluntary withdrawal is the responsible course to take" given the cardiovascular data and the availability of alternative pain management options [[33]](https://info.groupbenefits.org/docs/OGBforms/News/2004/MerckAnnouncement.pdf). The company emphasized that this decision reflected "the highest regard for the safety of our patients" despite VIOXX's established efficacy in treating pain and inflammation [[33]](https://info.groupbenefits.org/docs/OGBforms/News/2004/MerckAnnouncement.pdf).

The withdrawal had far-reaching consequences. CEO Raymond V. Gilmartin resigned in May 2005, less than nine months after the withdrawal announcement [[27]](https://www.corp-research.org/merck). The legal aftermath was substantial: a jury in Texas rendered a \$253 million verdict in the first civil lawsuit (November 2005), and Merck subsequently settled approximately 27,000 remaining VIOXX-related lawsuits for a total of \$4.85 billion by 2007 [[27]](https://www.corp-research.org/merck).

## What Survived

The cardiovascular safety signal identified in APPROVe prompted retrospective analysis of prior VIOXX clinical trials. The VIGOR trial (2000), which evaluated VIOXX in rheumatoid arthritis patients, was re-analyzed and similarly showed increased myocardial infarction risk in the VIOXX group (0.5%) compared to naproxen (0.1%; p < 0.05) [[34]](https://www.ccjm.org/content/ccjom/71/12/933.full.pdf). This finding, not evident at initial trial completion, became visible only upon systematic reassessment following APPROVe.

The VIOXX withdrawal and subsequent litigation became a watershed moment in pharmaceutical industry governance, clinical trial safety monitoring, and the relationship between regulators and manufacturers. While VIOXX itself was eliminated, the incident influenced industry practices for cardiovascular safety monitoring in drug development and clinical trial design oversight for decades afterward.
