# Sadat City

## Nasr City (Announced 1958): The Capital That Was Never Moved

Egypt's first explicit attempt to relocate its capital in the modern era occurred in 1958, when government planners proposed moving the seat of power to a new city designated Nasr City (named after President Gamal Abdel Nasser). [[8]](https://thewestendmuseum.org/history/topic/politics-law/urban-renewal-in-egypt/) [[10]](https://www.economist.com/interactive/1843/2026/04/02/egypts-new-pyramid-scheme) The project was constructed, but the government never actually relocated. [[8]](https://thewestendmuseum.org/history/topic/politics-law/urban-renewal-in-egypt/) [[10]](https://www.economist.com/interactive/1843/2026/04/02/egypts-new-pyramid-scheme) Cairo remained the capital, and Nasr City evolved instead into a residential neighborhood for middle-class families.

The capital relocation objective—distinct from the settlement itself—was abandoned. [[8]](https://thewestendmuseum.org/history/topic/politics-law/urban-renewal-in-egypt/) [[10]](https://www.economist.com/interactive/1843/2026/04/02/egypts-new-pyramid-scheme) Nasr City survives as a functioning residential area within greater Cairo, but it never achieved the administrative purpose for which it was designed. [[8]](https://thewestendmuseum.org/history/topic/politics-law/urban-renewal-in-egypt/) What survived is a city that serves a different function than originally conceived, illustrating a pattern in which purpose-built administrative settlements become residential or commercial communities when the original mandate is withdrawn.

## Sadat City (Founded 1978): The Largest Failed Capital Project

Anwar Sadat's vision for a new capital was vastly more ambitious than his predecessor's. In 1978, Sadat established Sadat City as an explicit attempt to relocate the capital and decentralize Egypt's government. [[28]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Sadat_City) [[29]](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/Onlinepubs/trcircular/199/199-002.pdf) The location was 93–94 kilometers northwest of Cairo in Menoufia Governorate (coordinates 30°24′N, 30°34′E). [[28]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Sadat_City) [[29]](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/Onlinepubs/trcircular/199/199-002.pdf)

The original vision encompassed approximately 500 square kilometers of development, including planned population exceeding one million residents, five industrial zones, and a green belt of 126 square kilometers. [[28]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Sadat_City) [[29]](https://onlinepubs.trb.org/Onlinepubs/trcircular/199/199-002.pdf) The scope represented one of the largest planned urban relocations of the late twentieth century. Unlike earlier projects that engaged architectural utopianism, Sadat City was conceived primarily as an industrial and administrative center—a pragmatic strategy for Egypt's economic development.

Between 1970 and 1981, Sadat explicitly moved government ministries at a cost of 25 million pounds—a significant state investment signaling serious intent. [[7]](https://owagik.com/en/2024/03/17/new-capital/) However, the relocation immediately encountered a fundamental operational problem: transportation between Sadat City and Cairo was cumbersome and time-consuming. Government functionaries found it impractical to manage the nation's business from a city 94 kilometers distant from the existing power structure, communications infrastructure, and residential concentration of the political elite. [[7]](https://owagik.com/en/2024/03/17/new-capital/)

After Sadat's assassination in 1981, momentum for the capital relocation ceased. The relocated government ministries were subsequently transferred to the University of Menoufia, a remarkable administrative downgrade that symbolized the project's failure. [[7]](https://owagik.com/en/2024/03/17/new-capital/) The relocated ministries, despite infrastructure investment, had produced "no economic, traffic or security benefits." [[7]](https://owagik.com/en/2024/03/17/new-capital/) Rather, they had created operational friction and disconnection from the capital's existing systems.

Sadat City did not fail as a city. It survived and evolved into a functioning industrial hub with approximately 180 enterprises by recent accounting. [[28]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Sadat_City) Population grew from initial settlement to approximately 48,666 residents in 2006, and estimates of 150,000 by 2015, though one source reported 74,177 in 2023. [[28]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Sadat_City) A rail link connecting Sadat City to Cairo was completed in 2025, improving connectivity that had been the original barrier to capital relocation. [[28]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Sadat_City) The city serves as a regional industrial center and residential community, but it fulfilled this purpose as a secondary development, not as the primary capital relocation objective that had driven its initial conception.

What survived Sadat City is a fully functioning city serving different purposes than originally envisioned. The distinction is crucial: capital relocation as an objective was abandoned (discontinued), but the urban settlement itself succeeded as an industrial and residential community. This represents a partial success—the place endured, but the purpose was superseded.

## Mubarak-Era Capital Relocation Proposal (Pre-2015): The Feasibility Study That Decided Against

Before the current New Capital project, the Mubarak administration undertook a feasibility study for capital relocation. [[7]](https://owagik.com/en/2024/03/17/new-capital/) [[9]](https://www.middleeasteye.net/news/doubts-raised-over-achievability-egypts-new-capital) A proposal emerged to move multiple government ministries from Cairo, similar in logic to Sadat's attempt. [[7]](https://owagik.com/en/2024/03/17/new-capital/) However, the feasibility study determined that the plan was "too expensive and unrealistic." [[9]](https://www.middleeasteye.net/news/doubts-raised-over-achievability-egypts-new-capital) Without economic justification, the proposal was abandoned at the planning stage, never advancing to land acquisition or construction. [[7]](https://owagik.com/en/2024/03/17/new-capital/) [[9]](https://www.middleeasteye.net/news/doubts-raised-over-achievability-egypts-new-capital) What survived the proposal was documentation only—no physical structures, no displacement of populations, but evidence of the enduring logic that drove repeated capital-relocation attempts across four decades.
