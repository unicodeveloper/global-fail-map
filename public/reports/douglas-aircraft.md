# Douglas Aircraft

## What Was Built and Why It Mattered

The Douglas Aircraft Company, founded July 22, 1921, by Donald Wills Douglas Sr., became one of the twentieth century's premier aerospace manufacturers. [[23]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Douglas_Aircraft_Company) By the 1940s, Douglas was Santa Monica's largest employer. [[22]](https://employeelawca.com/vintagela/donald-wills-douglas-santa-monicas-largest-employer-by-the-1940s/) The company's DC-3, introduced in the 1930s, revolutionized commercial aviation and established Douglas as a dominant force in aircraft design and production. Decades later, the company possessed a backlog of over \$3 billion in orders—a position that should have secured its future indefinitely.

In 1963, Douglas launched the DC-9, a twin-engine commercial jet designed to compete in the medium-range airliner segment. The aircraft represented an ambitious technological gamble: advanced avionics, two large turbofan engines, and a production schedule compressed to meet market demand. [[24]](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/pnp/habshaer/ca/ca3100/ca3187/data/ca3187data.pdf) By 1966, the company had secured orders for hundreds of aircraft from airlines worldwide, positioning Douglas as a rising force in the jet age.

## The Crisis Unfolds (1963–1966)

What followed was one of the most dramatic operational failures in American manufacturing history, one that appeared to contradict the company's commercial success. According to research by the Haas School of Business at UC Berkeley, Douglas entered a production crisis around 1963 driven by structural misalignment between its design philosophy and market demands. [[21]](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/leonard/Active%20Papers/What%20Killed%20Douglas%20Aircraft.pdf) The company built each DC-9 aircraft as a near-custom product, incorporating airline-specific modifications that inflated assembly costs and extended production timelines. While competitors like Boeing adopted modular, production-line methodologies, Douglas maintained craft-oriented assembly practices inherited from the pre-jet era.

By fiscal year 1966, the consequences were catastrophic. [[21]](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/leonard/Active%20Papers/What%20Killed%20Douglas%20Aircraft.pdf) Assembly costs overran by \$27 million in that single year alone. The company's stock, projected to reach \$19.2 million in market capitalization in February 1966, instead fell to a loss of \$5.23 per share. [[21]](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/leonard/Active%20Papers/What%20Killed%20Douglas%20Aircraft.pdf) Internal projections calculated that Douglas was one year away from insolvency—despite commanding a \$3 billion order backlog that any rational observer would have assumed guaranteed solvency. The fundamental problem was structural: each additional aircraft sold generated losses rather than profit.

## Dissolution and Merger (April 28, 1967)

Facing existential crisis, Douglas Aircraft was forced into a merger with McDonnell Aircraft, announced April 28, 1967. [[21]](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/leonard/Active%20Papers/What%20Killed%20Douglas%20Aircraft.pdf) The merger created McDonnell Douglas, fundamentally altering Douglas's operational autonomy. Douglas Aircraft as an independent entity ceased to exist, though the McDonnell Douglas entity retained the Douglas name in second position—a symbolic demotion of the historic brand.

## Geographic Specificity

Douglas Aircraft operated two primary facilities in the Los Angeles area: the original Santa Monica headquarters and engineering center (established 1921), and the Long Beach plant at 3855 Lakewood Boulevard in Long Beach, which opened in 1941 as the company's primary production facility. [[24]](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/pnp/habshaer/ca/ca3100/ca3187/data/ca3187data.pdf) Santa Monica remained the core design and engineering headquarters; Long Beach became the manufacturing hub for the DC-9 family. By 1968, Santa Monica operations were consolidated into Long Beach, and the Santa Monica facility closed. [[20]](https://crankyflier.com/2012/05/31/touring-the-ghosts-of-douglas-aircraft-companys-past/)

## What Ended

The independent Douglas Aircraft Company ceased to exist as a separate entity in 1967. The Santa Monica headquarters facility, which had anchored Southern California's aerospace industry for 47 years, closed in 1968. DC-9 production continued at Long Beach under McDonnell Douglas management, where the aircraft was progressively refined into the MD-80 and MD-90 families. By November 1997, Boeing (which had acquired McDonnell Douglas in 1997) announced discontinuation of the MD-80 and MD-90 production lines. [[19]](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1997-nov-04-mn-50053-story.html) The final commercial aircraft rolled off the Long Beach production line on May 4, 2006, ending 65 years of continuous aircraft manufacturing at the facility. [[20]](https://crankyflier.com/2012/05/31/touring-the-ghosts-of-douglas-aircraft-companys-past/)

## What Survived

The DC-9 family aircraft achieved tremendous commercial success despite Douglas's manufacturing crisis. The original DC-9 sold over 1,000 units. The subsequent MD-80 and MD-90 variants, built under McDonnell Douglas and then Boeing, added thousands more. The final avatar of the DC-9 family, the Boeing 717 (itself descended from the MD-95), remained in production and service into the 2020s. [[19]](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1997-nov-04-mn-50053-story.html) While the Douglas Aircraft Company as an independent enterprise was liquidated, the aircraft designs that nearly bankrupted it became among the most commercially successful jet transports ever built.

Additionally, the Douglas division that managed Project RAND (the postwar research operations that evolved into strategic analysis) survived and was spun off as an independent nonprofit research institution, the RAND Corporation. [[21]](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/leonard/Active%20Papers/What%20Killed%20Douglas%20Aircraft.pdf) RAND continues operation as one of the United States' most influential policy research organizations, severed entirely from Douglas Aircraft's manufacturing operations but born from Douglas's early commitment to systematic defense research.
