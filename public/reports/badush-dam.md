# The Badush Dam

## Design and Purpose

The Badush Dam is an unfinished multi-purpose structure located on the Tigris River, 16 kilometers northwest of Mosul in Ninawa Governorate. [[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Badush_Dam) Unlike the marshes drainage project (which was deliberately destructive), Badush Dam was designed as a protective infrastructure—a critical piece of regional water security intended to mitigate catastrophic risk from the already-constructed Mosul Dam immediately upstream.

The Mosul Dam, completed in 1986, represents a chronic structural hazard for Iraq. Its foundation is composed of gypsum—a highly soluble mineral that erodes when exposed to flowing water. Engineers have documented ongoing seepage and subsidence beneath the dam for decades, creating a persistent threat of catastrophic failure. In 2016, after structural assessments revealed worsening conditions, it was described as "the most dangerous dam in the world." [[28]](https://practical.engineering/blog/2026/5/19/fixing-the-most-dangerous-dam-in-the-world) A complete failure of Mosul Dam would release a destructive flood that would devastate Mosul, surrounding communities, and downstream areas as far as Basra, near Al-Faw. The Badush Dam was conceived as a protective dam downstream of Mosul—designed to capture and mitigate the impact of a potential Mosul Dam failure. [[25]](https://journal.uod.ac/index.php/uodjournal/article/view/906) [[26]](https://www.academia.edu/36397540/Badush_Dam_NW_Iraq_A_Geological_Study) [[27]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348408104_Badush_Dam_Planned_And_Designed_As_A_Protection_Dam_Nw_Iraq)

## Construction and Abandonment

Construction of Badush Dam began in 1988, during the final year of the Iran-Iraq War. The project proceeded into the early 1990s but was halted abruptly in 1991 following the Gulf War and the imposition of comprehensive international economic sanctions on Iraq. [[24]](https://www.academia.edu/105574762/Badush_Dam_Planned_And_Designed_As_A_Protection_Dam_Nw_Iraq) The dam reached approximately 30 to 40 percent completion before work ceased. [[24]](https://www.academia.edu/105574762/Badush_Dam_Planned_And_Designed_As_A_Protection_Dam_Nw_Iraq) The exact status of completion varies slightly in sources—some cite 30 percent, others 40 percent—but all agree that a substantial structural skeleton remains incomplete.

The suspension was directly caused by three converging factors:

1. **Economic sanctions**: The international embargo made it impossible to secure financing and imported materials for continued construction.

2. **Geotechnical hazards**: During construction, workers encountered "lethal gas exhalation from decomposing sediment," releasing carbon dioxide and methane that threatened worker safety. [[28]](https://practical.engineering/blog/2026/5/19/fixing-the-most-dangerous-dam-in-the-world) This unexpected hazard complicated construction protocols and increased perceived risk.

3. **Institutional reluctance to restart**: Even after sanctions were partially eased and Iraq attempted to resume major infrastructure projects in the 2000s, government financing remained contested. A December 2005 Ministry of Water Resources estimate calculated that **\$300 million** would be required to complete the dam—a sum Iraq was reluctant to commit during the post-2003 instability and competing demands on state resources. [[28]](https://practical.engineering/blog/2026/5/19/fixing-the-most-dangerous-dam-in-the-world)

## Cost and Completion Scenarios

The original cost envelope for Badush Dam is not consistently documented in accessible sources, but completion estimates have escalated substantially. A 2018 engineering assessment identified an alternative approach—a deep-cutoff wall solution designed to seal the gypsum foundation beneath Mosul Dam and reduce the need for Badush as a supplementary containment structure. This alternative was estimated at **\$3 to 5 billion**, a figure that reflects the scale of Iraq's reluctance to commit resources. [[28]](https://practical.engineering/blog/2026/5/19/fixing-the-most-dangerous-dam-in-the-world)

As of 2026, no substantial restart of Badush Dam construction has been documented in accessible sources. The partial infrastructure remains visible on satellite imagery and in engineering literature as an incomplete monument to water-resource ambition in an unstable region. The dam was not formally cancelled; rather, it entered a state of indefinite suspension, where restart remains theoretically possible but practically deferred.

## What Survived and Current Status

The half-finished Badush Dam remains "still on the table if an opportunity for funding and stability emerges." [[28]](https://practical.engineering/blog/2026/5/19/fixing-the-most-dangerous-dam-in-the-world) The foundation work, partial spillway structures, and survey documentation survive. The geotechnical knowledge gained from construction—including documentation of the subsidence and seepage conditions beneath Mosul Dam—survived and informs ongoing discussions of alternatives to complete construction.

The critical protective function that Badush was designed to serve—mitigation of Mosul Dam failure risk—remains unresolved. Iraq's water-resource engineers continue to recommend either (a) completing Badush Dam, (b) constructing the alternative deep-cutoff wall beneath Mosul, or (c) undertaking a major rehabilitation of Mosul Dam itself. None of these solutions has been funded or initiated as of 2026. The Mosul Dam remains structurally compromised, and the protective infrastructure designed to mitigate that risk remains incomplete.

## Geographic Connection to Al-Faw

Badush Dam is located on the Tigris River in northern Iraq, upstream of Mosul. While not directly at Al-Faw's location, it is part of the integrated Tigris water-management system that determines flow rates and sediment loads reaching Al-Faw and the Persian Gulf. A catastrophic failure of Mosul Dam—the risk that Badush was designed to prevent—would have ramifications extending all the way downstream to Al-Faw, affecting delta hydraulics, salinity, and navigation corridors.

## Status Summary

**Project Status**: Abandoned/indefinitely suspended at ~30–40% completion.
**What Ended**: Active construction (1988–1991); restart attempts failed due to financing and political obstacles.
**Why**: Economic sanctions; geotechnical complications (subsidence, gas hazards); insufficient government financing commitment.
**What Survived**: Partial structural elements (foundation, spillway infrastructure); geotechnical survey data; engineering knowledge.
**Confidence**: MEDIUM. Construction timeline and halt date are consistent across sources, but detailed post-1991 status relies heavily on Wikipedia and academic abstracts rather than Tier 1 primary documents. Geotechnical specifications are documented in academic literature but remain unverified against primary Iraqi government records.
