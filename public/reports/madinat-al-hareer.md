# Madinat Al-Hareer

## Conception and Early Momentum (2004–2007)

In 2004, seeking to revitalize the long-dormant Subiya development concept, Kuwait's government formally announced Madinat Al-Hareer—literally "Silk City"—as a comprehensive urban development initiative [[4]](https://fanack.com/kuwait/geography-of-kuwait/). The project was initially approved by the Kuwait Municipality in 2005 and formally endorsed by the Council of Ministers in November 2007 [[3]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13563475.2024.2381443). The envisioned scope was extraordinary: a 250 square kilometer (96.5 square mile) mixed-use urban development spanning residential neighborhoods, industrial zones, commercial districts, and protected nature reserves, with a target resident population of approximately 700,000 by 2030 [[6]](https://www.arabianindustry.com/construction/news/2014/aug/17/kuwait-aiming-to-resurrect-stalled-mega-projects-4790266/) [[7]](https://www.constructionweekonline.com/article-29603-kuwait-aiming-to-resurrect-stalled-mega-projects) [[9]](https://www.middleeastarchitect.com/insight/42263-10-completed-and-under-construction-buildings-from-kuwait) [[10]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Madinat_al-Hareer).

The financial commitment was equally ambitious. Early estimates placed the total project cost at \$100 billion to \$132 billion, planned over a 25-year execution horizon (completion estimated 2030–2035) [[4]](https://fanack.com/kuwait/geography-of-kuwait/) [[7]](https://www.constructionweekonline.com/article-29603-kuwait-aiming-to-resurrect-stalled-mega-projects) [[10]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Madinat_al-Hareer) [[15]](https://militarybudget.org/kuwaits-burj-mubarak-al-kabir-will-it-actually-happen-1xuz). The centerpiece was the Burj Mubarak Al Kabir, a proposed 1,001-meter supertall tower with 234 floors, designed to rival or exceed the Burj Khalifa in Dubai [[6]](https://www.arabianindustry.com/construction/news/2014/aug/17/kuwait-aiming-to-resurrect-stalled-mega-projects-4790266/) [[8]](https://gulfconstructiononline.com/Article/386621/prospects-brighter) [[17]](https://www.skyscrapercity.com/threads/burj-mubarak-al-kabir-kuwait-city-1001m-234-fl.339039/page-2).

## The Institutional Paralysis (2006–2012)

Within two years of the formal November 2007 approval, the project had stalled. In 2006, responsibility was transferred from Kuwait Municipality to the Kuwait Investment Authority (KIA) and the Supreme Council for Planning and Development (SCPD) [[3]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13563475.2024.2381443). Rather than accelerating progress, this transfer created institutional fragmentation. Multiple sources indicate that the project became entangled in disputes between government agencies over financing mechanisms, land ownership rights, private-sector concessions, and decision-making authority [[3]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13563475.2024.2381443) [[5]](https://timeskuwait.com/northern-economic-zone-vital-to-realizing-vision-2035/).

A critical gap emerged: Kuwait's legal and governance frameworks lacked adequate instruments to facilitate the land titles, environmental permits, and public-private partnerships required for a project of this magnitude. Parliamentary debate over the appropriate financing model—whether to rely on government capital, foreign direct investment, or mixed public-private structures—created political uncertainty that deterred private-sector commitment [[3]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13563475.2024.2381443).

By 2012, it became evident that KIA and SCPD had failed to advance the project beyond preliminary studies. The government acknowledged this failure by creating the Subiya Land Administrative Authority (ABDA—or possibly another transliteration) to consolidate authority and restructure governance [[3]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13563475.2024.2381443) [[5]](https://timeskuwait.com/northern-economic-zone-vital-to-realizing-vision-2035/). This reorganization itself required time, legislative action, and appointment of leadership, causing further delay.

## Restructuring and Rebranding (2012–2017)

The 2012 creation of ABDA represented a tacit admission of institutional failure in the existing governance model. However, the restructuring did not precipitate rapid construction. Instead, between 2012 and 2017, the project was reframed and reintegrated into Kuwait's broader Vision 2035 strategic framework [[3]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13563475.2024.2381443). It was rebranded as part of the Northern Economic Zone initiative, expanding its scope beyond Subiya to encompass multiple planned economic zones including Al-Naayem, Al-Abdali, and Al-Wafra [[12]](https://kdipa.gov.kw/invest-in-kuwait/economic-zones/).

In 2019, the government assigned Deputy Prime Minister Nasser Sabah to oversee the Northern Economic Zone portfolio and presented renewed proposals to Parliament [[3]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13563475.2024.2381443) [[11]](https://kuwaittimes.com/article/29243/kuwait/kuwait-renews-push-for-northern-economic-zone/). However, by this point, more than a decade had been lost, and the project remained in preliminary or planning phases with no operational construction on the Silk City itself.

## Analysis: Why Madinat Al-Hareer Stalled (2004–2012)

The research findings, synthesized from news media and planning analyses, identify several reinforcing causes:

1. **Municipal-Private Sector Disputes**: Local government authorities and private developers disagreed over land acquisition methods, compensation structures, and operational control. Without a clear contractual framework, neither party was willing to commit major capital.

2. **Fragmented Agency Authority**: Transfer of responsibility to KIA and SCPD created coordination gaps rather than streamlining decisions. The two agencies' differing mandates—KIA focused on investment returns, SCPD on national planning—generated conflicting priorities.

3. **Absent Legal Frameworks**: Kuwait lacked adequate statutory instruments for land title transfer, environmental assessment, and concession agreements at the scale required. Legislative amendments that might have enabled these mechanisms faced parliamentary delay.

4. **Capital Constraints and Oil-Price Volatility**: While Kuwait's sovereign wealth funds were substantial, the government prioritized capital preservation and spending restraint during periods of lower oil prices. Committing \$130 billion to a single development project, phased over 25 years, was fiscally controversial when competing claims (education, healthcare, defense, and reconstruction of earlier war damage) demanded resources.

5. **Political Opposition in Parliament**: Some parliamentary members questioned the feasibility, financial prudence, and environmental impact of the megaproject, creating political risk for government officials championing the initiative [[3]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13563475.2024.2381443).

No single decision document cancelling or formally suspending Madinat Al-Hareer appears in accessible sources. Instead, the project was repeatedly reorganized, repackaged, and rebranded—a form of institutional avoidance rather than explicit termination. This pattern of restructuring without progress became characteristic of Kuwait's megaproject governance.
