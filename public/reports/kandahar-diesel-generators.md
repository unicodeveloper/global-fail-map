# The Kandahar Diesel Generators

As the 2010s began, the Kajaki Dam's Unit 2 turbine project was severely delayed by security conditions and construction challenges. The project, which would have added a third turbine and substantially increased output, was years behind schedule. To bridge the gap until Kajaki could be completed and transmission lines extended to Kandahar city—110 kilometers to the south—the U.S. military and USAID financed the construction of two temporary 10-megawatt diesel-fueled power generators in Kandahar. These generators were explicitly conceived as a temporary solution to provide electricity to Kandahar's population and industrial base while permanent hydroelectric capacity came online. [[18]](https://www.reuters.com/article/afghanistan-electricity-taliban-idUSL4N0QB5EE20140805/) [[19]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-afghanistan-electricity-taliban/lights-set-to-go-out-in-afghanistans-kandahar-after-u-s-aid-winds-down-idUSKBN0G51AU20140806/) [[20]](https://www.vice.com/en/article/8qx8ja/afghanistans-kandahar-province-is-about-to-lose-most-of-its-electricity)

The diesel plants cost approximately \$143.5 million to construct, and an additional \$140 million was budgeted for fuel costs over several years—a combined commitment of roughly \$283.5 million for infrastructure explicitly labeled as temporary. [[11]](https://www.propublica.org/article/afghanistan-waste-kajaki-dam-more-than-300-million-spent-still-not-done) [[15]](https://www.voanews.com/a/lights-set-to-go-out-in-kandahar-after-us-aid-winds-down/1971948.html) [[16]](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2014/aug/5/obamas-lack-of-plan-for-electricity-in-kandahar-co/) [[17]](https://www.dawn.com/news/1123476/lights-to-go-out-in-kandahar-after-us-aid-winds-down) The generators were commissioned in 2011 and began supplying electricity to approximately 130 factories, hospital facilities, and residential areas in Kandahar. [[15]](https://www.voanews.com/a/lights-set-to-go-out-in-kandahar-after-us-aid-winds-down/1971948.html)

The structural failure of this arrangement became apparent by 2014. The plan's underlying assumption—that Kajaki Unit 2 would be completed and operational, transmission lines would be built from Helmand to Kandahar, and the Afghan government utility company (Da Afghanistan Breshna Sherkat, or DABS) would have sufficient revenue collection capacity to sustain the diesel plant operations—proved fundamentally flawed on multiple fronts.

First, Kajaki Unit 2 remained incomplete, delayed by persistent Taliban activity in the region that made construction and security hazardous. [[20]](https://www.vice.com/en/article/8qx8ja/afghanistans-kandahar-province-is-about-to-lose-most-of-its-electricity) Second, even the existing Kajaki capacity could not be reliably transmitted to Kandahar because transmission lines remained incomplete or were subject to Taliban sabotage in areas they controlled. Third, the Afghan government lacked the financial capacity to maintain fuel purchases. In mid-2014, as the U.S. military withdrawal accelerated, the U.S. government signaled that it would end subsidies for the diesel plants' fuel. The Afghan utility company publicly stated it could not afford to purchase diesel fuel at market prices. [[16]](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2014/aug/5/obamas-lack-of-plan-for-electricity-in-kandahar-co/)

A Reuters report from August 2014 quoted Afghan Ministry of Power officials and local leaders stating the generators would shut down that month and remain non-operational unless international funding continued. [[18]](https://www.reuters.com/article/afghanistan-electricity-taliban-idUSL4N0QB5EE20140805/) [[19]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-afghanistan-electricity-taliban/lights-set-to-go-out-in-afghanistans-kandahar-after-u-s-aid-winds-down-idUSKBN0G51AU20140806/) The Washington Times reported in August 2014 that "the Afghan government says it cannot afford to maintain Kandahar's power generators or pay for the fuel. Diesel supplies in the city are already being rationed, and power outages will expand significantly when the generators lose fuel next month." [[16]](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2014/aug/5/obamas-lack-of-plan-for-electricity-in-kandahar-co/) Power cutoffs began in September 2014, with the generators expected to cease all operations by late 2014 or early 2015. [[15]](https://www.voanews.com/a/lights-set-to-go-out-in-kandahar-after-us-aid-winds-down/1971948.html) [[16]](https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2014/aug/5/obamas-lack-of-plan-for-electricity-in-kandahar-co/) [[17]](https://www.dawn.com/news/1123476/lights-to-go-out-in-kandahar-after-us-aid-winds-down) [[20]](https://www.vice.com/en/article/8qx8ja/afghanistans-kandahar-province-is-about-to-lose-most-of-its-electricity)

Additional complications hampered continuity. Taliban control of rural transmission infrastructure prevented DABS from collecting revenues from customers in insurgent-held areas—meaning that even in Kandahar city, the utility's ability to sustain fuel payments from electricity sales was constrained. Powerful local leaders and tribal figures refused to pay their electricity bills. [[18]](https://www.reuters.com/article/afghanistan-electricity-taliban-idUSL4N0QB5EE20140805/) [[19]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-afghanistan-electricity-taliban/lights-set-to-go-out-in-afghanistans-kandahar-after-u-s-aid-winds-down-idUSKBN0G51AU20140806/) The Vice report from August 2014 noted: "with persistent insurgent attacks in the Kandahar region, plans for the Kajaki Dam Unit 2 Project have been delayed, which would eventually relieve the stop-gap system"—but relief never came. [[20]](https://www.vice.com/en/article/8qx8ja/afghanistans-kandahar-province-is-about-to-lose-most-of-its-electricity)

**Geographic Connection:** The two diesel generators were located in Kandahar city, approximately 110 kilometers south of the Kajaki Dam. They were positioned to supply electricity to Kandahar's urban and industrial areas and represented the intended service territory for Kajaki's future power expansion.

**What Ended and Why:** The diesel generators ceased receiving fuel subsidies in September 2014 when the U.S. ended economic support. DABS proved unable to sustain fuel purchases from revenue collection. Taliban control of transmission areas prevented revenue collection from rural customers. The fundamental structural failure was the assumption that a permanent hydroelectric supply would be in place and Afghan government revenue capacity would exist to sustain temporary diesel infrastructure.

**What Survived:** The physical generators remained in place but were non-operational as of the 2014–2015 cutoff reported by multiple sources. No sources in the research dataset confirm restoration of the Kandahar generators to operation after 2015. The facility exists as non-functional infrastructure, representing approximately \$283.5 million in sunk costs.

**Confidence Assessment:** HIGH. Multiple independent news sources (Reuters, VOA News, Washington Times, Vice) corroborated the September 2014 cutoff and the underlying financial and political causes.
