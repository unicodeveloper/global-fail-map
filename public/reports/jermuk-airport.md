# Jermuk Airport

## Attempted Project and Geographic Setting

The Jermuk Airport project represents one of Armenia's longest-running unfinished infrastructure ambitions. The proposed facility was intended to establish commercial aviation service to Jermuk, a town of approximately 10,000 residents situated at 2,070 meters elevation in the Vayots Dzor mountains, 170 kilometers south of Yerevan. The project aimed to create a modern tourism hub accessible by air, which would theoretically enable the direct transfer of international visitors arriving in the country to the region's mineral-water spas without transit through the capital. Jermuk itself had operated a military airfield from the Soviet era, though its viability for scheduled civilian service remained contentious.

## Timeline and Political Commitment

The project timeline reveals a pattern of announcement and abandonment spanning nearly two decades. In July 2008, the Armenian government formally designated Jermuk as a state-priority tourist development center—a decision reinforced in September 2008 when the government declared the "Program of Measures on Development of Jermuk as an International Resort." [[1]](https://www.mining.com/armenia-approves-restart-of-amulsar-gold-mine/) The commitment appeared concrete: a territorial plan was approved in February 2010, and private investors began serious planning around the 2008–2010 timeframe. The planning incorporated high expectations. According to one government assessment cited in contemporary planning documents, the development zone was projected to attract approximately 100,000 international visitors annually and generate \$100 million in annual tourism revenue, with the potential to create 4,000 new jobs.

However, the momentum broke almost as soon as it formed. In April 2009, less than a year after the government's initial declaration, Armenia's Deputy Minister of Urban Development, Narek Sargsyan, made a public statement that directly contradicted the government's own policy. Sargsyan stated that Jermuk "is a cul-de-sac, outside the main air corridor" and that Armenia's aviation development priorities should instead focus on Goris airport, located in a different region with better connectivity to main flight paths. [[4]](https://forarmenians.com/news/7709/) This official contradiction—with a ranking government official publicly undermining the government's own stated policy—was not accompanied by a formal policy reversal, leaving the project in bureaucratic limbo.

The project would not die, but it would also not advance. Between 2016 and 2018, under Prime Minister Karen Karapetyan's government, the airport project resurfaced in political discourse and planning documents, only to fade again. Most recently, in December 2025, the current Mayor of Jermuk, Vahagn Arsenyan, announced plans to construct a new airport by 2028. [[4]](https://forarmenians.com/news/7709/) This announcement carried familiar language to earlier iterations but no new funding mechanism or construction contract.

## Geographic and Physical Obstacles

The failure of the Jermuk Airport project was rooted in structural geographic constraints that transcended political cycles. Jermuk's location as a narrow valley terminus—what Deputy Minister Sargsyan called "a cul-de-sac"—meant that aircraft arriving from major international routes would be forced into a dead-end approach pattern, complicating operations and raising costs relative to alternative destinations. The narrow valley also constrained runway length and aircraft approach angles, limiting the size and type of aircraft that could reliably operate from the site.

An additional obstacle was the proximity of Saint Gayane Church to the proposed airport site. The church, situated approximately 350 meters from the planned runway, created what planning documents characterized as an "artificial obstacle"—a cultural and potentially regulatory constraint on flight operations due to noise, safety, and heritage preservation concerns. The church's position was non-negotiable from a cultural heritage perspective, making runway reconfiguration necessary but geometrically difficult within the existing valley topography.

The site's high elevation (2,070 meters) also presented operational challenges for civilian aviation, particularly during winter weather conditions when visibility and approach difficulty increased. Armenia's existing major civilian airport (Zvartnots, near Yerevan) operated at a much lower elevation, and pilot training and aircraft certification for the high-altitude Jermuk site would have been more complex and costly.

## Current Status and Abandoned Infrastructure

As of September 2026, the Jermuk Airport project remains unbuilt. The old Soviet-era airfield from the 1950s continues to exist at the site, with some runway and taxiway infrastructure visible, but no documentation confirms that scheduled commercial air service operates from Jermuk as of 2026. The December 2025 announcement by Mayor Arsenyan of a 2028 completion target has not been accompanied by published funding allocation, construction contracts, or contractor selection—indicators that would normally precede serious airport development.

What survived the two decades of failed planning is the institutional artifact of the airport project itself: it persists as a recurring element in Vayots Dzor regional development strategy, re-announced at intervals, but having accumulated no tangible progress. The geography remains unchanged, and Sargsyan's 2009 assessment of the location's fundamental unsuitability for regional aviation remains valid. The project represents not a single discrete failure but an extended exercise in governmental announcement divorced from implementation capacity.

## Why the Project Failed

The Jermuk Airport project failed for three interlocking reasons: **geographic unsuitability** (narrow valley, high elevation, church proximity), **lack of economic justification** (the revenue projections from 100,000 annual visitors were aspirational rather than market-tested, and alternative routes via Yerevan offered better economics), and **institutional inconsistency** (the Deputy Minister's 2009 contradiction was never resolved through formal policy reversal, leaving the project orphaned between competing government priorities).

The recurring re-announcements in 2016–2018 and December 2025 suggest that the project persists in regional political rhetoric as a symbolic aspiration rather than as a serious infrastructure development target. Tourism development for Jermuk has proceeded through alternative mechanisms, including World Bank investment in regional tourism infrastructure.
