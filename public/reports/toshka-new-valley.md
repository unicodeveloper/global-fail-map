# The Toshka Project

The launch of the Toshka Project in 1997 under President Hosni Mubarak marked an attempt to learn from, and vastly exceed, the earlier New Valley scheme. Rather than relying on groundwater extraction, Toshka would pump water 310 kilometers from Lake Nasser—the massive reservoir created by the Aswan High Dam—through a concrete-lined canal system into the Toshka Depression in the southwestern desert [[4]](https://eros.usgs.gov/earthshots/toshka-project-egypt). The project was conceived in two phases: Phase 1 would reclaim 550,000 feddans and establish agricultural infrastructure; Phase 2 would expand to 2 million feddans and include construction of entirely new urban settlements to house millions of Egyptians [[1]](https://www.egyptindependent.com/toshka-new-valleys-mega-failure-slideshow/).

The centerpiece infrastructure was the Mubarak Pumping Station, an engineering achievement recognized by the American Society of Civil Engineers. Completed in March 2005, the station cost approximately \$400 million and featured 24 high-capacity pumps capable of lifting 1.2 million cubic meters of water per hour from Lake Nasser, the highest elevation pumping station in the world at that time [[21]](https://www.brighthubengineering.com/hydraulics-civil-engineering/59663-toshka-project-mubarak-pumping-station/). The Sheikh Zayed Canal system, named after the UAE leader, was constructed as the main conveyance structure, designed to carry this water across the desert in a concrete-lined channel stretching 310 kilometers from the lake toward the oases of the Western Desert, including intended extensions to Baris Oasis [[6]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/New-Valley-project).

Investment poured in from the government and, initially, from private sector investors. Large areas of desert were transformed: pivot irrigation systems appeared on satellite imagery, water pumps operated continuously, and crop trials began. Yet from the beginning, technical problems accumulated.

**Soil and hydrogeological challenges emerged quickly.** The Toshka Depression and surrounding areas had shallow clay layers that impeded water infiltration in some zones and caused waterlogging in others [[5]](https://www.egypt-business.com/web/details/1218-xg-Toshka-New-Valley-Project-A-Failure-of-Planning-or-a-Failure-of-Implementation/5141). Soil salinity poisoned crops in other areas; the desert surface and groundwater carried dissolved minerals that accumulated as water evaporated [[2]](https://www.npr.org/2012/07/10/155027725/mubaraks-dream-remains-just-that-in-egypts-desert). Evaporation losses from the open canal system consumed an estimated 10 percent of Lake Nasser's annual inflow, a staggering waste of a finite water supply upon which Egypt's entire agriculture depends [[10]](https://www.weaspire.info/the-toshka-story-and-why-the-gerd-needs-to-come-online/). The underground aquifers in the region proved less productive than earlier surveys suggested, and connecting the pumped surface water to deep storage proved problematic.

**Private sector investment collapsed before Phase 1 was complete.** The government had assumed the private sector would finance 75–80 percent of Toshka's expansion—a bet that proved wildly optimistic [[1]](https://www.egyptindependent.com/toshka-new-valleys-mega-failure-slideshow/). Private investors saw technical risks, market uncertainty (who would buy produce grown at such high transportation and production cost?), and political instability (Mubarak's regime was increasingly fragile). Investment fell far short, leaving the government to finance the gap. Government budgets stretched thin attempting to complete Phase 1 and maintain operations.

**In 2005, the Egyptian government formally abandoned Phase 2 and extended the original completion deadline to 2022, tacitly admitting the project could not deliver as planned [[1]](https://www.egyptindependent.com/toshka-new-valleys-mega-failure-slideshow/).** No official decree was published in Western media (and primary government archives remain inaccessible in this research), but the decision was announced and widely reported. Phase 2's vision of urban centers and mass population relocation was shelved indefinitely.

**What remained as of 2012:** approximately 54,000 feddans had been brought under irrigation by the Toshka Project, less than 10 percent of Phase 1's 550,000 feddan target, and a tiny fraction of Phase 2's 2 million feddan ambition [[1]](https://www.egyptindependent.com/toshka-new-valleys-mega-failure-slideshow/). The Mubarak Pumping Station stood as a technical monument to engineering ambition, continuing to operate and pump water, but at far below its design capacity and serving a footprint of cultivated land much smaller than envisioned [[21]](https://www.brighthubengineering.com/hydraulics-civil-engineering/59663-toshka-project-mubarak-pumping-station/). The Sheikh Zayed Canal remained incomplete: as of April 2012, approximately 60 kilometers of the intended 310-kilometer extension to Baris Oasis remained unbuilt, halted by funding constraints and declining priority [[1]](https://www.egyptindependent.com/toshka-new-valleys-mega-failure-slideshow/). The unfinished canal became a visible symbol of the project's stall—a concrete channel leading into the desert and simply ending.

Crops that did establish in Toshka included wheat, grapes, and tomatoes, cultivated using modern drip-irrigation technology to minimize water loss [[19]](https://nileresearch.org/cool_timeline/the-toshka-project/). These agricultural areas survived as functioning, if struggling, enterprises. The Ministry of Irrigation continued allocating annual budgets for the region—allocating 325 million Egyptian pounds annually for water resource projects in the New Valley as recently as late 2024—a sign that the project, though dramatically scaled back, persists in attenuated form [[13]](https://www.egypttoday.com/Article/1/136371/Egypt-allocates-LE-325-M-annually-for-water-resource-projects). The infrastructure itself (pumping stations, canals, irrigation pipes, power installations) remained in place and operational, serving a much smaller agricultural operation than any original timeline had promised.
