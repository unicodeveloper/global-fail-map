# New Gourna

## Hassan Fathy's Visionary Communities: New Gourna and New Baris

Hassan Fathy, an Egyptian architect of international renown, conceived two ambitious settlements designed to synthesize vernacular desert architecture with modernist environmental principles. Both projects attempted to create sustainable communities that would improve residents' lives while respecting regional building traditions and responding to extreme climate challenges. Both were abandoned or left incomplete.

**New Gourna (1945–1954):** Fathy designed the first settlement at Thebes (modern Luxor) on the west bank of the Nile. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) The project was conceptualized between 1945 and 1947 and constructed from 1946 to 1954. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) Its purpose was to relocate the residents of the village of Gourna, who had subsisted partly through archaeological looting of the nearby Theban necropolis, to a new sustainable community organized around agriculture, crafts, and trade. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins)

Fathy's design incorporated passive environmental systems drawn from Nubian and Roman architectural traditions. Structures employed mud-brick (adobe) construction, domed roofing that trapped and cooled air without mechanical systems, and passive water-cooling mechanisms. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) The settlement included an open-air theatre for community gatherings, a covered market for commerce, mosques, and schools—infrastructure intended to establish a complete, culturally rooted community. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins)

However, the settlement never achieved social integration. Residents, who had been relocated through involuntary relocation processes, "felt forced to adapt to a new prescribed existence." [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) The imposed design, regardless of its technical sophistication, did not match residents' social practices or preferences. Following Egypt's 2011 revolution, the settlement fell into neglect. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) Buildings deteriorated; passive cooling systems failed without maintenance, and structures that had been designed to remain 10 degrees Celsius cooler in summer and 10 degrees warmer in winter than external temperatures became significantly hotter and colder through degradation. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) The community has been described as "slowly disappearing into decaying concrete outskirts of Luxor," a dystopian outcome for a utopian vision. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins)

What survived New Gourna is architectural documentation and physical structures (though deteriorating). Fathy's design principles, synthesizing vernacular techniques with climate-responsive modernism, remain recognized as significant contributions to Arab architectural theory. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) The project's failure, however, illuminates the gap between technical architectural achievement and social acceptance: buildings constructed according to rational environmental principles cannot substitute for communities organized by residents' own agency.

**New Baris (1965–1967):** Fathy's second project, conceived for the Kharga Oasis in Upper Egypt approximately three hours' drive from Luxor, addressed a different problem: the possibility of sustainable development in one of Egypt's most remote desert regions. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) The settlement was designed between 1965 and 1967 and intended to utilize newly discovered underground water reserves to support fruit and vegetable export production. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins)

The technical innovation at New Baris was sophisticated climate control architecture. Fathy designed a system of "incrementally reduced airshafts and secondary towers" that would circulate cooled air throughout the settlement, reducing external desert temperatures by more than 15 degrees Celsius through passive ventilation. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) The architecture represented an advanced application of traditional Persian windcatcher (badgir) and Egyptian hypocaust principles to modern desert urbanism.

New Baris was never completed. Construction began in 1965–1967 but halted when Egypt's military mobilization for the 1967 Six-Day War demanded resources and attention. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins) The project was never resumed after the war. The incomplete structures remain as evidence of interrupted ambition; construction documentation and design records survive but the settlement was abandoned before habitability. [[1]](https://www.metalocus.es/en/news/great-utopias-hassan-fathy-badly-managed-governments-renovated-under-lens-hannah-collins)

What survived New Baris is design documentation and engineering principles. The passive cooling methodologies developed for the settlement—the "incrementally reduced airshaft" system—represent a technical contribution to climate-responsive architecture, even though the social implementation was cut short by geopolitical crisis.
