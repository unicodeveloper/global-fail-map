# NOHAB

## Founding and Industrial Evolution (1847–1950s)

NOHAB (Nydqvist & Holm AB) was founded in 1847 in Trollhättan by entrepreneurs Antenor Nydqvist, Johan Magnus Lidström, and Carl Olof Holm as Trollhättans Mekaniska Verkstad. [[6]](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/NOHAB) The company's initial business was the manufacture of water turbines, exploiting the hydroelectric potential of the Göta Älv falls. In 1865, NOHAB pivoted to steam locomotive manufacturing and built its 1,000th locomotive by 1912. [[5]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/1214801) [[6]](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/NOHAB)

NOHAB became a major supplier of motive power to railways across Northern Europe and Asia. The company secured a significant contract to manufacture 500 steam locomotives for the Soviet Union between 1921 and 1924, a deal that was notably paid in precious metals—56 tons of gold and 230 million Swedish kronor. [[3]](https://grokipedia.com/page/NOHAB) This Soviet order represented one of the largest single contracts in NOHAB's history and underscored the company's international reputation for quality engineering.

NOHAB expanded into diesel-electric locomotive manufacturing after World War II, positioned to benefit from the global railway electrification and modernization programs of the 1950s and 1960s. The company supplied 59 MY-class diesel locomotives to the Danish State Railways between 1954 and 1965, 35 Di3-class locomotives to Norwegian NSB (Norwegian State Railways) between 1957 and 1965, and 20 M61-class locomotives to Hungarian MÁV (Hungarian State Railways) in 1963–1964. [[3]](https://grokipedia.com/page/NOHAB) Under licensing agreements with General Motors EMD (Electro-Motive Division), NOHAB manufactured over 50 AA16-type locomotives for Swedish railways. [[3]](https://grokipedia.com/page/NOHAB)

## Diversification and Organizational Integration

Beyond locomotives, NOHAB diversified into aircraft engine manufacturing. In the 1930s, the company established Nohab Flygmotorfabriken AB (NOHAB Aircraft Engine Factory) and began manufacturing Bristol Jupiter aircraft engines under license. [[6]](https://www.gracesguide.co.uk/NOHAB) This division evolved into Svenska Flygmotor AB, which later became a core business segment. In 1941, Volvo acquired a majority stake in Svenska Flygmotor, and by 1970, Volvo owned 100 percent of the company. [[44]](https://www.crunchbase.com/organization/volvo-aero) This aerospace heritage ultimately survived NOHAB's collapse and continued through Volvo Aero, which was acquired by GKN in 2012, creating GKN Aerospace Sweden—an operation that remains fully operational in Trollhättan as of 2024.

In the 1960s, NOHAB also manufactured tank hull components for the S-tank (Stridsfordon 103), a Swedish main battle tank designed during the Cold War. The company also produced printing machinery and marine diesel engines under the Polar brand, with models such as the F216V delivering up to 3,520 brake horsepower. [[11]](https://shipcalculators.com/wiki/nohab-polar-marine-engines)

## Strategic Acquisition and Ownership Complications

In 1936, NOHAB was acquired by Bofors, the Swedish armaments manufacturer. This acquisition placed NOHAB within a much larger industrial conglomerate but also increasingly tied the company's fortunes to defense contracting and heavy machinery markets subject to international political volatility and trade restrictions.

## Market Collapse and Bankruptcy (1970s–1986)

Beginning in the 1970s, NOHAB faced a structural market decline that proved insurmountable. The global railway industry was transitioning from diesel-electric locomotives to electric traction systems in developed nations. Simultaneously, the 1973 oil crisis and the secondary 1979 energy crisis reduced diesel locomotive demand as railways and governments prioritized electrification to reduce fossil fuel dependence. [[18]](https://companies.jrank.org/pages/4684/Vattenfall-Ab.html)

The company's primary revenue stream—diesel locomotive manufacturing—contracted sharply. The final Rc-class electric locomotives for Swedish State Railways were completed in late 1979, marking the end of locomotive production for Swedish domestic market. [[18]](https://companies.jrank.org/pages/4684/Vattenfall-Ab.html) In 1978, NOHAB sold 51 percent of its marine diesel engine business to Wärtsilä, a Finnish marine engineering company. [[11]](https://shipcalculators.com/wiki/nohab-polar-marine-engines) The remaining stake was divested to Wärtsilä by 1984, eliminating another major revenue source. The marine engine business continued under Wärtsilä's ownership, but NOHAB lost the production and profit participation.

NOHAB declared bankruptcy in 1979. [[9]](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q1073100) Manufacturing operations ceased in the early 1980s. By 1986, NOHAB's production facilities were fully dismantled and the company was liquidated. [[9]](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q1073100)

## Geographic Location and Facility

NOHAB occupied manufacturing facilities in Trollhättan, including a historic smithy that dated to the company's 1847 founding. The NOHAB Smithy, an original forge structure from the 19th century, has been preserved as a historical artifact and is currently accessible within the Innovatum District, Trollhättan's science center and industrial heritage museum. [[12]](https://www.vastsverige.com/en/trollhattan-vanersborg/produkter/nohab-smithy/) The main manufacturing campus (location details not precisely documented in available sources) covered several hectares and included foundries, machine shops, assembly halls, and testing facilities necessary for locomotive and diesel engine production.

## What Survived: Locomotive Heritage and Aerospace Continuity

While NOHAB itself dissolved, the technologies and products associated with the company achieved partial survival:

**Locomotive Heritage**: NOHAB-built locomotives remain operational in heritage railway services across Europe. Hungarian MÁV operates nostalgic steam excursion trains using preserved NOHAB-manufactured locomotives. [[3]](https://grokipedia.com/page/NOHAB) Danish railway museums maintain MY-class diesels. Norwegian heritage railway lines operate preserved Di3-class and other NOHAB-designed units. [[3]](https://grokipedia.com/page/NOHAB) These locomotives, built in the 1950s–1960s, have exceeded their original design life by 50+ years and continue to operate as functional transportation assets and heritage tourism attractions.

**Marine Diesel Engine Continuity**: The Polar diesel engine line designed and manufactured by NOHAB continued under Wärtsilä ownership. Numerous vessels built in the 1960s–1980s with Polar engines remain in service in commercial shipping, fishing fleets, and pleasure cruising, extending the operational life of NOHAB's engineering decades beyond the company's dissolution. [[11]](https://shipcalculators.com/wiki/nohab-polar-marine-engines)

**Aerospace Heritage**: The aircraft engine division evolved into Svenska Flygmotor (Volvo majority stake 1941, full Volvo ownership 1970), which later became Volvo Aero (acquired by GKN in 2012). GKN Aerospace Sweden now operates as a world-class aero-engine component and systems manufacturer headquartered in Trollhättan, with 2,000 employees and a \$64 million new additive manufacturing facility completed in 2024. [[40]](https://pitchbook.com/profiles/company/65632-96) [[42]](https://www.gknaerospace.com/locations/europe/sweden/) [[43]](https://www.gknaerospace.com/news-insights/news/gkn-aerospace-expands-industry-leading-manufacturing-facility-in-sweden/) This operation represents the most significant surviving business derived from NOHAB's historical technical foundation, though through a chain of organizational transformations.

**NOHAB-GM Foundation**: A heritage preservation organization, the NOHAB-GM Foundation, maintains historical documentation and archives related to NOHAB's locomotive manufacturing. [[8]](https://www.nohab-gm.hu/en/en05_5.html)

## Why NOHAB Failed: Market Saturation and Energy Transitions

NOHAB's bankruptcy resulted from structural changes in transportation technology and energy policy that were beyond the company's control:

1. **Railway Electrification Preference**: Post-1945 European railway policy favored electrified mainlines over diesel-electric locomotives, particularly in developed economies. Electrification required high capital investment but eliminated ongoing fuel costs.

2. **Energy Crises and Oil Economics**: The 1973 and 1979 oil crises made diesel-powered transportation economically disadvantageous and prompted governments to prioritize electrification to reduce petroleum import dependence.

3. **Market Saturation**: By the 1960s, most developed-nation railways had completed their core diesel-electric locomotive inventory. Replacement demand became the primary market, but replacement cycles allowed long intervals between purchases. NOHAB could not sustain manufacturing volume on replacement demand alone.

4. **Global Consolidation**: Locomotive manufacturing consolidated globally, with larger manufacturers (GM EMD in the United States, Alstom in France, Siemens in Germany) achieving economies of scale that smaller national producers like NOHAB could not match.

5. **Loss of Diversification Revenue**: When marine diesel engines (sold to Wärtsilä 1978–1984) and defense contracts declined, NOHAB lacked sufficient alternative revenue to sustain the organization.
