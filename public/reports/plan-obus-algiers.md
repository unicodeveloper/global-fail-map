# Plan Obus

## What Was Attempted and Why It Mattered

Between 1931 and 1942, Swiss-French modernist architect Le Corbusier proposed an urban redesign of central and western Algiers that would have fundamentally restructured the city's spatial organization and social composition. The Plan Obus (named for its artillery-shell profile) combined three distinct zones: a business district with elevated structures at Cape of Algiers, a residential zone elevated into the city's heights, and a bridge-connected "roadtown"—a 14-level elevated highway structure with integrated residential levels—capable of accommodating approximately 180,000 residents. [[7]](https://www.bidoun.org/articles/le-corbusier-s-algerian-fantasy) [[8]](https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/projects-urban-planning-projects-abch-algiers-algeria-1930/) [[15]](https://arquitecturaviva.com/articles/algiers-city-infrastructure-and-landscape)

The project emerged from formal colonial planning initiatives. France, commemorating a century of rule in Algeria (1830-1930), commissioned Henri Prost and Le Corbusier to unveil a modernized city plan. [[7]](https://www.bidoun.org/articles/le-corbusier-s-algerian-fantasy) Le Corbusier spent four consecutive years (between 1930-1934) proposing increasingly detailed solutions to Algerian municipal authorities. [[8]](https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/projects-urban-planning-projects-abch-algiers-algeria-1930/) The design represented the pinnacle of the Congrès Internationaux d'Architecture Moderne (CIAM) aesthetic: monumental scale, functional separation, and technological utopianism. [[15]](https://arquitecturaviva.com/articles/algiers-city-infrastructure-and-landscape)

## Geographic Connection and Urban Scope

Le Corbusier's design specifically targeted central Algiers and the elevated terrain overlooking the Casbah (medina), the historic Arab quarter. The Plan Obus would have demolished or rendered invisible the Casbah's street pattern and social organization—arguably its central aim from a colonial perspective. [[7]](https://www.bidoun.org/articles/le-corbusier-s-algerian-fantasy) The geographic scope encompassed what is now downtown Algiers and the slopes above it, fundamentally altering the relationship between the European and Arab sectors of the city.

## Why It Was Abandoned

The project faced two categories of opposition. First, it was ideologically incompatible with Algerian social and religious traditions. The megastructural segregation of work, residence, and commerce conflicted with organic urban practices built over centuries. Second, it explicitly imposed a colonial vision of urban order—separating European from Arab populations through vertical stacking and spatial design. [[7]](https://www.bidoun.org/articles/le-corbusier-s-algerian-fantasy) The design was rejected because it prioritized European modernist aesthetics over local social cohesion.

## Historical Context of Rejection

The formal abandonment occurred in 1942, during World War II, when municipal governance disputes and economic constraints made large-scale construction infeasible. However, the deeper rejection came from Algerian society itself. Just twelve years after Le Corbusier's final proposal was dismissed, the Algerian Revolution erupted in 1954, originating in the Casbah district that the Plan Obus would have obliterated. [[7]](https://www.bidoun.org/articles/le-corbusier-s-algerian-fantasy) By 1962, when Algeria achieved independence, the political logic supporting such European-imposed urban restructuring had evaporated entirely.

## What Survived

The Plan Obus had profound intellectual legacy but zero physical realization. No structures were built according to Le Corbusier's designs in Algiers. What survived was the architect's professional evolution: the rejection prompted Le Corbusier to re-examine his assumptions about universal modernism and contributed to his later stylistic shift toward primitivism and more culturally grounded design approaches. The episode influenced architectural discourse worldwide but left Algiers's urban fabric fundamentally unchanged.
