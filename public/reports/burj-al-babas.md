# Burj Al Babas

Between Istanbul and Ankara in the town of Mudurnu, in Bolu Province in northwestern Turkey, one of the early 21st century's most spectacular property failures took concrete form and then stopped abruptly. Burj Al Babas was conceived as a luxury resort community of castle-themed villas marketed to wealthy Gulf tourists seeking secondary residences [[6]](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2019/jan/28/fate-of-castles-in-the-air-in-turkeys-151m-ghost-town) [[2]](https://www.abandonedspaces.com/public/burj-al-babas.html).

The developer, a Turkish company partnered with foreign investors, began construction in 2014 with enormous ambitions. The plan called for 732 individually designed Gothic-style château villas, each resembling European castle architecture [[2]](https://www.abandonedspaces.com/public/burj-al-babas.html). The vision was to create an exclusive community combining architectural fantasy with resort amenities.

By 2018, approximately 587 structures—roughly 80 percent of the planned villas—had been erected [[2]](https://www.abandonedspaces.com/public/burj-al-babas.html). The scale of construction was substantial; the project invested approximately \$200 million (£151 million) in development costs [[6]](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2019/jan/28/fate-of-castles-in-the-air-in-turkeys-151m-ghost-town).

Then, abruptly, the project collapsed. Turkish currency crisis struck in summer 2018 as the Turkish lira depreciated sharply [[1]](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/world/middle-east/turkey-financial-crisis-erdogan-contruction-projects-stall-latest-news-a8527281.html). Developers had financed the project partly in foreign currency—their debt accumulated to approximately £20 million—and the lira's value collapse made debt service impossible [[6]](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2019/jan/28/fate-of-castles-in-the-air-in-turkeys-151m-ghost-town). International buyer interest evaporated as the Turkish economy deteriorated. Purchasers from Gulf states withdrew from pre-purchase agreements and cancelled orders. The developer filed for bankruptcy in 2018.

Workers simply abandoned the site. The half-finished villas—walls erected but interiors completely hollow, many lacking roofs, none containing plumbing or electrical systems—remain frozen mid-construction. The site resembles an eerie architectural graveyard: hundreds of identical château structures in concrete skeleton form, standing on a Turkish hillside like "something out of a Disney horror film," as one journalist described it [[2]](https://www.abandonedspaces.com/public/burj-al-babas.html).

**What survived:** Nothing functional. The structures remain, but they serve no purpose. The site became a monument to overextension and the vulnerability of large construction projects to currency fluctuations. As of 2023, the structures remained abandoned on the landscape, a visible testament to the Turkish financial crisis's destructive power [[2]](https://www.abandonedspaces.com/public/burj-al-babas.html).

The Burj Al Babas collapse illustrates a recurrent pattern: ambitious real estate development in Turkey, dependent on foreign financing or foreign currency, shatters when macroeconomic crises strike. The developers were not incompetent—they erected 587 buildings on schedule—but they were exposed to currency risk they could not manage.
