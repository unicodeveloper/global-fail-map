# The Daugavpils dam

## The Project and Its Strategic Context

The Daugavpils Hydroelectric Power Station represented the fourth and largest planned component of the Daugava River hydroelectric cascade, a Soviet vision for transforming Latvia's primary river system into a series of controlled reservoirs and power-generating dams. The Daugavpils station was designed to produce 300 megawatts of capacity through 10 turbines, each rated at 30 megawatts [[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daugavpils_hydroelectric_power_station). The design was entrusted to the USSR's Hydroproject Institute, the premier Soviet hydroelectric engineering organization. Construction began in 1979 and proceeded through the 1980s, consuming vast expenditures and substantial material resources [[8]](https://eng.lsm.lv/article/features/video/documentary-tells-tale-of-failed-daugavpils-hydroelectric-project.a379471/).

The project's completion was projected for 1990, consistent with Soviet five-year planning cycles and the broader agenda to electrify and industrialize Soviet Latvia. The dam would have been located near Daugavpils, approximately 260 kilometers southeast of Riga, in the region where the Daugava River flows toward Belarus—making it not merely a Riga project but a centerpiece of Soviet Latvia's energy infrastructure and a transboundary system affecting neighbor states [[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daugavpils_hydroelectric_power_station).

## The Turning Point: October 17, 1986

The project's fate reversed abruptly following the publication of a single opinion piece. On October 17, 1986, the newspaper *Literatūra un Māksla* (Literature and Art) published an essay titled "Thinking About the Fate of the Daugava" (*Domāšana par Daugavas Likteni*), authored by Dainis Īvāns, a prominent journalist, and Artūrs Snips, an engineer [[11]](https://eng.lsm.lv/article/culture/history/how-a-press-story-undid-the-plan-for-another-power-plant-on-the-river-daugava.a354625/). The article circulated widely despite Soviet censorship apparatus and catalyzed immediate public mobilization. Within weeks, approximately 30,000 citizens signed petitions opposing the dam [[10]](https://meduza.io/en/feature/2023/08/29/the-fate-of-the-daugava). The campaign crossed the Baltic republics: Estonia, Lithuania, Slovakia, and Hungary mobilized solidarity movements; the response exceeded typical Soviet-era infrastructure debate and assumed the character of a transnational environmental and nationalist awakening [[10]](https://meduza.io/en/feature/2023/08/29/the-fate-of-the-daugava).

The Soviet authorities responded with a counterintelligence reflex: they attempted to suppress discourse about the Daugava by censoring even indirect references. In an act that highlighted the regime's desperation, the state banned the word "Daugava" from publications—a ban so comprehensive that it extended to consumer packaging, with the inscription removed from waffle boxes [[10]](https://meduza.io/en/feature/2023/08/29/the-fate-of-the-daugava). The prohibition created an Orwellian inversion: the more the authorities tried to erase the name of the river, the more the river became the symbolic center of Latvian resistance.

## Scientific and Political Reversals

The opposition secured intellectual legitimacy in the summer of 1987 when the Soviet Academy of Sciences convened a conference to evaluate the Daugavpils project and the broader Daugava cascade strategy. The Academy's conclusion was damning: the project was unacceptable on both environmental and economic grounds [[8]](https://eng.lsm.lv/article/features/video/documentary-tells-tale-of-failed-daugavpils-hydroelectric-project.a379471/) [[10]](https://meduza.io/en/feature/2023/08/29/the-fate-of-the-daugava). This endorsement from the Soviet Union's highest scientific body provided political cover for cancellation and acknowledged the ecological damage that would have been irreversible.

On November 5, 1987, the Council of Ministers of the Latvian Soviet Socialist Republic formally issued the decision to abandon the Daugavpils Hydroelectric Power Station [[8]](https://eng.lsm.lv/article/features/video/documentary-tells-tale-of-failed-daugavpils-hydroelectric-project.a379471/). The decision was historically consequential for reasons extending far beyond energy policy. The campaign to save the Daugava and halt the dam became recognized as the opening chapter of Latvia's independence movement. The newspaper editor and environmental activist Dainis Īvāns, author of the October 1986 article, became the founding leader and chairman of the Popular Front of Latvia in 1988, an organization that would grow to 110,000 members and serve as the institutional vehicle for Latvia's transition to sovereignty [[10]](https://meduza.io/en/feature/2023/08/29/the-fate-of-the-daugava). The river had become a nation.

## What Would Have Been Lost

The Daugavpils reservoir would have extended approximately 65 kilometers, stretching from the dam site northward into Belarus (threatening the industrial complex at Navapolatsk) and covering approximately 200 kilometers of the Upper Daugava valley—a landscape renowned for its ecological uniqueness and cultural significance [[11]](https://eng.lsm.lv/article/culture/history/how-a-press-story-undid-the-plan-for-another-power-plant-on-the-river-daugava.a354625/) [[12]](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/providers/122652/records/64746a8a79cbb2c2c1af3a4a). The most spectacular feature was the Daugavas loki (Daugava meanders), a series of tight, scenic loops carved by the river through limestone cliffs, creating one of the most visually distinctive fluvial systems in the Baltic region [[10]](https://meduza.io/en/feature/2023/08/29/the-fate-of-the-daugava). The meanders would have been permanently submerged beneath a monotonous, static reservoir, converting a dynamic living landscape into a utilitarian water storage facility.

The inundation would have displaced dairy farms, small towns, dacha communities, and agricultural settlements that had existed along the Daugava for centuries. Historical cemeteries would have been relocated (a process already begun before the cancellation decision), severing communities from their ancestral burial grounds. The forest ecosystems of the Daugava valley, which had recovered during decades of Soviet protection from agricultural expansion, would have been destroyed. Transboundary impacts would have been severe: Belarusian territories and the possibility of flooding the Navapolatsk chemical complex created international diplomatic complications that Soviet engineers had not adequately addressed [[11]](https://eng.lsm.lv/article/culture/history/how-a-press-story-undid-the-plan-for-another-power-plant-on-the-river-daugava.a354625/).

## What Survived

In contrast to the Riga Metro, the Daugavpils project left physical residue because construction had proceeded substantially. Excavated basin foundations, embankments, and site preparation remain visible in the landscape near Daugavpils, serving as archaeological evidence of Soviet ambition and abandoned engineering [[8]](https://eng.lsm.lv/article/features/video/documentary-tells-tale-of-failed-daugavpils-hydroelectric-project.a379471/). The relocated cemeteries persist as a permanent testimony to the project's partial implementation—Latvian communities were displaced and their dead were relocated, then the dam was cancelled, leaving only the displacement as a historical artifact.

The Upper Daugava valley itself—unsubmerged and intact—is what survived most consequentially. The meanders remain, the limestone cliffs stand, the ecological communities continue. This preservation became the legacy of the successful opposition movement. In 1988, the same year as the formal cancellation, the Gauja National Park (the oldest national park in the Soviet Union, established 1937) was expanded in the Daugava region to provide statutory protection against future dam proposals [[12]](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/providers/122652/records/64746a8a79cbb2c2c1af3a4a). The river that the Soviet regime tried to silence through censorship became the symbol of Latvian ecological and political restoration.
