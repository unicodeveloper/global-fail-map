# The London Ringways

London in the 1960s faced a paradox: car ownership tripled from 1945 to 1965 (reaching three million cars in the metropolitan area), yet the city remained a Victorian street network hostile to automotive traffic. Traffic congestion was severe and worsening. The Greater London Council, formed in 1965 as a unified metropolitan authority, inherited planning documents sketching a radical solution: four concentric motorway rings (Ringways 1–4) that would encircle London with 478 miles of motorway, radial spokes feeding into and out of the rings.

The concept dated to 1937 and was revived in the postwar Abercrombie County of London Plan (1943) [[27]](https://grokipedia.com/page/London_Ringways) [[1]](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/londons-lost-ringways/). By 1966, the GLC published Ringway 1 (the inner "Motorway Box"), followed by Ringway 2 in 1967 [[27]](https://grokipedia.com/page/London_Ringways).

The scale was extraordinary: Ringway 1 alone would require demolition of 7,585 houses over 1,048 acres, displacing 15,000 to 80,000 Londoners [[23]](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2019/oct/22/concrete-bungle-how-public-fury-stopped-the-1970s-plan-to-turn-london-into-a-motorway) [[28]](https://www.mylondon.news/news/nostalgia/plan-turn-london-massive-motorway-26782831). The project budget for Ringway 1 reached £480 million (\~£6.58 billion in 2025 values), with full system costs projected at \$1.7 billion [[27]](https://grokipedia.com/page/London_Ringways) [[28]](https://www.mylondon.news/news/nostalgia/plan-turn-london-massive-motorway-26782831). The Westway (A40 elevated section, Paddington to Shepherds Bush, 2.5 miles) was authorized first and construction began in 1964 by contractor Laing, opening 27–28 July 1970 [[25]](https://london.fandom.com/wiki/Westway) [[29]](https://flashbak.com/pictures-of-the-protests-at-the-westway-in-1970-27205/).

Westway's opening proved to be a political turning point. Residents beneath and around the elevated structure hung banners reading "Get us out of this hell, rehouse us now" [[29]](https://flashbak.com/pictures-of-the-protests-at-the-westway-in-1970-27205/). Noise, air pollution, vibration from 200,000+ daily vehicles, and the visual dominance of the concrete viaduct made the theoretical costs tangible. The Westway became a symbol of what the Ringways would do at vastly larger scale: sever communities, destroy neighborhoods, and ruin air quality for the sake of moving private cars.

By 1970, the London Motorway Action Group had mobilized over 100 resident associations across London's boroughs [[1]](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/londons-lost-ringways/) [[28]](https://www.mylondon.news/news/nostalgia/plan-turn-london-massive-motorway-26782831). Activists including the Covent Garden squatting coalition, led by vicar Austen Williams and renegade GLC official Brian Anson, escalated protests to council occupation and street action [[23]](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2019/oct/22/concrete-bungle-how-public-fury-stopped-the-1970s-plan-to-turn-london-into-a-motorway). Economist J. Michael Thomson (London Amenity and Transport Association) published technical critiques of the cost estimates and cited induced-demand theory: new roads generate equivalent additional traffic, not congestion relief [[1]](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/londons-lost-ringways/).

The Frank Layfield Public Inquiry (1970–1973) into the Greater London Development Plan received 30,000 objections; 75 percent targeted the motorway schemes [[24]](https://www.roads.org.uk/ringways/the-end). The Transport Minister and Treasury declared the costs "from remote to ridiculous" [[24]](https://www.roads.org.uk/ringways/the-end). In a strategic reversal, Conservative GLC opposition leader Reg Goodwin withdrew party support in June 1972, pledging to abandon the "disastrous plans." When Labour won GLC elections in April 1973, their first act was to **cancel Ringways 1 and 2 funding** [[24]](https://www.roads.org.uk/ringways/the-end) [[25]](https://london.fandom.com/wiki/Westway).

What was built: The Westway (2.5-mile elevated A40, opened 1970, later downgraded from A40(M) to A40 in 2000 when TfL assumed authority). The West Cross Route (0.7-mile ground-level M41, later A3220, Westway to Holland Park Roundabout). The East Cross Route (fragmented sections 1969–1979, connecting A102 Blackwall tunnel). Stub motorway connections at Brent Cross, M11 terminus, M23, and a hidden motorway-scale road buried beneath Westfield shopping center remained as monuments to unfulfilled plans [[27]](https://grokipedia.com/page/London_Ringways) [[26]](https://grokipedia.com/page/west_cross_route) [[25]](https://london.fandom.com/wiki/Westway).

Ringways 3 and 4, representing outer-ring sections, were partially incorporated into the M25 orbital motorway (completed 1986) and the South Circular Road, but never as comprehensive inner rings [[27]](https://grokipedia.com/page/London_Ringways).

What did not happen: No "Motorway Box" was built. The London ringway concept was abandoned entirely after 1973. Subsequent GLC and TfL administrations never revived inner-motorway schemes despite decades of traffic growth. The Margaret Thatcher era (1980s) attempted fragmented motorway fragments (Western Environmental Improvement Route, South Circular tunnel under Chiswick–Wandsworth), but electoral vulnerability derailed these when local opposition replicated 1970s campaigns [[1]](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/londons-lost-ringways/). Instead, London evolved: congestion charging (introduced 2003), emissions standards, public transport investment, and cycling infrastructure became the city's infrastructure strategy.

Geographic connection: Inner ring (Ringway 1) would have run through Fulham, Earl's Court, Kensington, Islington, Camden, Clapham, Greenwich. Westway runs 51.52°N, -0.21°W (Paddington) to 51.51°N, -0.24°W (Shepherds Bush). East and West Cross Routes through north and southeast London respectively.

Status: **Cancelled 13 April 1973. Westway and fragments remain as operational infrastructure; the grand scheme was definitively abandoned.**
