# The TIPNIS highway

The Trans-TIPNIS Highway—a 300-kilometer road cutting through the Isiboro Sécure National Park and Indigenous Territory (TIPNIS) in central Bolivia—sits geographically distant from Puerto Suárez. Yet it exemplifies the political constraints that have limited all large infrastructure in eastern Bolivia, including projects near Puerto Suárez.

## The Project and Its Strategic Rationale

The TIPNIS highway was designed to connect the Beni agricultural region directly to the commercial hub of Cochabamba, reducing travel time by 300 kilometers and creating a shortcut for agricultural exports to interior markets. The project was a centerpiece of President Evo Morales's 2008–2011 infrastructure development agenda, with funding from the Brazilian development bank BNDES (\$330 million of an estimated \$415 million project cost). [[19]](https://www.cnn.com/2011/10/22/world/americas/bolivia-highway-conflict) [[21]](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-latin-america-15409447) [[22]](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2011/9/27/bolivia-president-pauses-highway-construction)

TIPNIS is not simply a national park—it is legally constituted as an indigenous autonomous territory for approximately 12,000 residents, primarily Moxo, Yuracaré, and Tsimane peoples. The highway would bisect their territory, fragmenting land-use patterns, enabling external commercial access, and imposing environmental change at a scale the indigenous communities had not consented to.

## The Confrontation and Cancellation

June 2011: Morales publicly stated the road would be built "whether they like it or not"—an inflammatory signal of government intent to override indigenous opposition. [[24]](https://www.cnn.com/2011/09/27/world/americas/bolivia-indigenous-protest)

August 15, 2011: Indigenous leaders and their supporters organized a 500-kilometer march from Trinidad to La Paz, a two-month journey with 1,500 participants, to oppose the project.

September 26, 2011: Near Yucumo, approximately 250 kilometers short of La Paz, roughly 500 police officers with truncheons and tear gas forcibly dispersed the marchers. [[25]](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-latin-america-15057406) Many demonstrators were bused away without consent; protesters alleged four deaths, though officials denied fatalities and promised investigation. [[20]](https://www.survivalinternational.org/news/7735) [[26]](https://www.cnn.com/2011/09/29/world/americas/bolivia-protests/index.html)

September 27, 2011: Defence Minister Cecilia Chacón and Interior Minister Sacha Llorenti resigned in protest against the police action. [[22]](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2011/9/27/bolivia-president-pauses-highway-construction) Morales issued a public apology for the "imperdonable" use of force. [[26]](https://www.cnn.com/2011/09/29/world/americas/bolivia-protests/index.html)

October 1, 2011: Roughly 1,000 protesters resumed their march to complete the remaining 250 kilometers to La Paz, now moving forward unobstructed.

October 21, 2011: Morales formally cancelled the highway plan and requested Congressional modification of TIPNIS's protected status. [[8]](https://www.survivalinternational.org/news/7827) [[19]](https://www.cnn.com/2011/10/22/world/americas/bolivia-highway-conflict) [[21]](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-latin-america-15409447)

## Causes and Consequences

The TIPNIS cancellation reveals how indigenous political mobilization, combined with ministerial defection and international reputational cost, can override executive commitment to infrastructure development. Morales, Bolivia's first indigenous president, faced a contradiction: his political identity rested on indigenous empowerment, yet his development agenda required imposing infrastructure on indigenous territory against indigenous opposition. The police violence broke the contradiction in favor of indigenous rights—at significant political cost.

The broader consequence extends to Puerto Suárez and other regional infrastructure. The TIPNIS episode demonstrated that large-scale infrastructure projects affecting indigenous and protected territories would face organized, sophisticated opposition capable of deterring government completion. This calculus has influenced subsequent projects: Puerto Busch's crossing of protected wetlands became more controversial after TIPNIS; other regional development initiatives faced heightened scrutiny. [[9]](https://www.business-humanrights.org/en/latest-news/bolivia-world-bank-finds-chinese-state-owned-cscec-violated-labor-environmental-standards-in-road-project-locals-allege-insufficient-consultation-compensation/)

The Tamengo Canal and TIPNIS demonstrate two distinct political failure modes: one transnational (Brazilian governance constraints), one subnational (indigenous rights and domestic political coalition management). Yet both resulted in the same outcome—infrastructure left incomplete because the political cost of completion exceeded its perceived benefit.
