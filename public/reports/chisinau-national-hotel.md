# The National Hotel

The National Hotel stands on Ștefan cel Mare Boulevard, the capital's main thoroughfare, as the single most visible symbol of Chisinau's prolonged post-Soviet decline. Built between 1974 and 1978, this 17-story structure was designed by Soviet architects A. Gorbuntsov and V. Shalaginov as an Intourist flagship property—a luxury hotel intended to accommodate international guests visiting the Soviet Union. The building maintained operation through the Soviet period and into the transition decade, serving as one of Chisinau's most prominent hotels. [[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Hotel_(Chi%C8%99in%C4%83u))

The hotel closed around 2004 after nearly three decades of operation. The closure directly followed the 1992 Transnistria war, which devastated Chisinau's tourism sector. Although the war ended in a ceasefire agreement, international tourism to the capital never recovered to pre-1992 levels. The hotel's economic viability deteriorated through the 1990s and early 2000s, and by the mid-2000s, it ceased operations entirely. [[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Hotel_(Chi%C8%99in%C4%83u))

The National Hotel was privatized in 2006, transferred to private ownership through Alfa-Engineering, which held 83.24% of shares. Subsequent transfers changed the property's ownership multiple times, but each successive owner proved unable or unwilling to either renovate the building or undertake demolition. The hotel remained standing, deteriorating. [[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Hotel_(Chi%C8%99in%C4%83u))

As of September 2026, the National Hotel remained standing but completely derelict, with interior spaces stripped of valuable materials—wiring, windows, and marble tiles removed by salvagers. In August 2024, the *New York Times* characterized the building as "a study in the post-Soviet dysfunctions of one of Europe's least visited capitals," describing it as "reduced to a ruin in the more than 30 years since Moldova gained independence." [[2]](https://www.nytimes.com/2024/08/04/world/europe/moldova-national.html) Contemporary urban exploration documentation from 2024 shows the building's interior gutted, exterior weathered and graffitied, its iconic position on the capital's main boulevard creating a persistent visual reminder of administrative paralysis. [[15]](https://exploringwithkatie.com/abandoned-hotel-national-chisinau-moldova/) [[18]](https://www.flickr.com/photos/100954775@N03/54138890406/)

The property became trapped in legal complications. In 2021, Chisinau's city government granted demolition permission, but the decision was reversed the following year after a court placed a hold on the building as part of money-laundering proceedings against its then-owner, Vladimir Andronachi. The hold meant the building could not be demolished without judicial approval, yet no renovation program materialized. [[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Hotel_(Chi%C8%99in%C4%83u))

The situation escalated in November 2025 when a 13-year-old girl was found dead after falling from the building—evidence of its dangerous, accessible condition. A second incident in late November 2025 involved another minor found unconscious inside the structure. [[17]](https://radiomoldova.md/p/63691/former-national-hotel-will-be-fenced-by-chisinau-city-hall-owner-to-cover-costs) These tragedies prompted Mayor Ion Ceban to announce plans for the city government to install a fence around the abandoned building, with the owner required to cover costs of securing the property. [[17]](https://radiomoldova.md/p/63691/former-national-hotel-will-be-fenced-by-chisinau-city-hall-owner-to-cover-costs) In November 2025, the Moldovan parliament began analyzing new regulatory frameworks to mandate security measures for neglected buildings, responding in part to the National Hotel situation. [[16]](https://www.moldpres.md/eng/society/national-hotel-other-abandoned-buildings-might-be-mandatorily-secured-moldovan-parliament-analyzes-new-rules-for-neglected-buildings)

The National Hotel thus persists as a standing but uninhabitable ruin—its shell structurally intact but entirely non-functional. The building represents three distinct failures: the investor's inability to sustain the tourism economy post-1992; the city government's incapacity to execute either renovation or demolition despite multiple attempts; and the legal system's inability to resolve ownership disputes and criminal holds that have kept the property in limbo for five years.
