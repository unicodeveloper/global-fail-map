# Juraguá nuclear plant

What was attempted was ambitious in scope and driven by Cuba's acute energy shortage. In 1976, as the Soviet Union deepened its commitment to the Cuban Revolution, officials signed an agreement to construct two Soviet-design VVER-440 Model V-318 pressurized water reactors at a coastal site near the town of Juraguá in Cienfuegos Province [[15]](https://www.gem.wiki/Juragua_nuclear_power_plant). Each reactor would generate 440 megawatts of electricity; together, the pair would supply approximately 15 percent of Cuba's total electricity demand. This was not a peripheral project. In the context of the 1970s and 1980s, when Cuban power generation relied heavily on aging U.S.-vintage thermoelectric plants (many dating to the pre-1959 Republican era) and inefficient sugar-mill-linked power generation, a modern nuclear facility represented the most advanced technological option available to the island [[20]](https://2001-2009.state.gov/p/wha/rt/cuba/commission/2004/32252.htm).

Construction began formally in March 1983 on Unit 1, with Unit 2 following in 1985 [[17]](https://www.nytimes.com/2000/12/18/world/cuba-and-russia-abandon-nuclear-plant-an-unfinished-vestige-of-the-soviet-era.html) [[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juragua_Nuclear_Power_Plant). The Soviet Union provided not merely financing but expertise, materials, and direct engineering support. Soviet specialists numbering approximately 450 arrived to supervise construction and train Cuban workers and engineers [[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juragua_Nuclear_Power_Plant). The Soviets invested what would become over $3 billion by the late 1980s in what was called "the project of the century" in Cuban discourse [[18]](https://read.uolpress.co.uk/read/the-social-and-political-life-of-latin-american-infrastructures-eab4760e-3328-4053-8029-9ecae2dc08c2/section/66636b25-739e-45fa-8237-b3dde8b18fc3). By the early 1990s, Unit 1 had achieved approximately 90 percent completion of its civil construction (the concrete structures, containment systems, and basic reactor vessel installation), though the proportion of electrical and mechanical equipment installed remained contentious—U.S. assessments suggested only 30–37 percent equipment completion, while Cuban claims ranged toward 75–90 percent [[16]](https://en.clickpetroleoegas.com.br/o-sonho-nuclear-de-fidel-castro-como-uma-usina-nuclear-em-cuba-se-tornou-um-cemiterio-de-bilhoes-de-dolares/) [[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juragua_Nuclear_Power_Plant). Unit 2 remained only 20–30 percent complete at the point of suspension [[16]](https://en.clickpetroleoegas.com.br/o-sonho-nuclear-de-fidel-castro-como-uma-usina-nuclear-em-cuba-se-tornou-um-cemiterio-de-bilhoes-de-dolares/).

But the project harbored technical vulnerabilities that Soviet audits themselves were documenting. Quality control failures became apparent as construction proceeded. Welding standards—critical for reactor safety in a marine environment subject to salt corrosion—proved inadequate. U.S. government assessments documented that approximately 10–15 percent of examined welded joints failed initial inspection standards [[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juragua_Nuclear_Power_Plant). Cuban defectors with technical backgrounds, including engineer Vladimir Cervera Cruz, reported figures as high as 50 percent defective welds at certain examined sites [[9]](https://www.thecubareader.com/blog/a-failed-nuclear-power-project-in-cuba). Coastal corrosion of components, inexperienced Cuban welders accelerated into roles beyond their training, and rapid rotation of Soviet personnel further compromised quality assurance. The reactor complex sat on a humid coastal plain across from Cienfuegos, exposed to constant salt spray that tested materials and maintenance protocols [[12]](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna3340708).

## The Moment of Rupture

The project did not fail gradually. It died abruptly on December 25, 1991, when the Soviet Union formally dissolved. This was not hyperbole in Cuban planning documents—Soviet subsidies to Cuba, estimated at approximately \$4–6 billion annually during the 1980s, represented roughly 20 percent of Cuban gross domestic product [[17]](https://www.nytimes.com/2000/12/18/world/cuba-and-russia-abandon-nuclear-plant-an-unfinished-vestige-of-the-soviet-era.html). The preferential pricing Cuba received for sugar exports to the Soviet Union, the free or heavily subsidized supply of Soviet oil and machinery, and the direct capital transfers that funded projects like Juraguá vanished within weeks [[18]](https://read.uolpress.co.uk/read/the-social-and-political-life-of-latin-american-infrastructures-eab4760e-3328-4053-8029-9ecae2dc08c2/section/66636b25-739e-45fa-8237-b3dde8b18fc3).

By September 5, 1992, Cuba and the Soviet Union formally suspended construction on the Juraguá plant [[17]](https://www.nytimes.com/2000/12/18/world/cuba-and-russia-abandon-nuclear-plant-an-unfinished-vestige-of-the-soviet-era.html). The suspension announcement acknowledged what was becoming apparent: there was no mechanism to complete the facility. Cuba possessed no domestic capacity to finance the project independently. Russia, itself economically devastated by the dissolution and struggling to absorb the costs of its own post-communist transition, was no longer in a position to subsidize Caribbean projects. By the mid-1990s, Cuba entered what became known officially as the "Special Period in Time of Peace"—a euphemism for economic freefall characterized by rolling blackouts, severe fuel shortages, and acute scarcity of foreign exchange [[20]](https://2001-2009.state.gov/p/wha/rt/cuba/commission/2004/32252.htm).

Attempts were made to attract alternative financing. Between 1993 and 1996, Cuban officials made repeated appeals to Canada, Mexico, Argentina, and European firms to supply the estimated \$750 million required to complete at least one reactor [[28]](https://vancepuchalski.com/2025/06/16/juragua-nuclear-power-plant/). Siemens (West Germany), Ansaldo (Italy), and Électricité de France all withdrew interest, discouraged both by the technical and financial risks and by the Helms-Burton Act of 1996, which threatened sanctions against any foreign company investing substantially in Cuba [[12]](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna3340708) [[17]](https://www.nytimes.com/2000/12/18/world/cuba-and-russia-abandon-nuclear-plant-an-unfinished-vestige-of-the-soviet-era.html). In December 2000, Russian President Vladimir Putin made a final offer: Russia would fund \$800 million to complete one of the two reactors, transforming Juraguá into a single-unit facility. Fidel Castro declined. Official Cuban policy shifted away from nuclear energy, citing safety concerns and the unviable economics of completing the project [[10]](https://www.abandonedspaces.com/industry/soviet-nuclear-plant.html) [[12]](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna3340708).

## Current Status and Abandonment

As of September 2026, the Juraguá Nuclear Power Plant remains in precisely the condition it reached in September 1992: unfinished, defueled (no nuclear fuel was ever loaded into the reactors), and officially abandoned [[15]](https://www.gem.wiki/Juragua_nuclear_power_plant). The twin concrete containment structures stand on the coastal plain, partially dismantled. In 2004, as Cuba's thermal power generation crisis deepened, the main turbine from Juraguá was removed and relocated to the Antonio Guiteras Thermoelectric Plant in Matanzas Province, where it replaced a damaged unit critical to the facility's operation [[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juragua_Nuclear_Power_Plant). This cannibalization was symbolic: the partially built nuclear station became a source of spare parts for the aging coal and fuel-oil plants struggling to supply Cuba's electricity grid.

No International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) decommissioning program has been initiated [[15]](https://www.gem.wiki/Juragua_nuclear_power_plant). The site remains guarded but accessible to researchers and photographers; the reactor shells are exposed to coastal salt spray and undergo gradual material degradation. The facility consumes minimal maintenance resources but generates no electricity and likely never will [[11]](https://www.slate.com/blogs/atlas_obscura/2013/09/11/the_unfinished_cuban_nuclear_power_plant_abandoned_when_the_ussr_collapsed.html).

## What the Project Left Behind

The nuclear facility itself represents sunk cost and unrealized potential. But the Juraguá nuclear program had created a human and institutional infrastructure around itself. Thousands of Cuban engineers, technicians, and laborers were trained during the 1980s—some through direct Soviet instruction on-site, others through programs at Soviet technical institutes. The Higher Institute for Nuclear Science and Technology was established to train Cuban nuclear specialists; the Juragua Nuclear Polytechnical Centre created an institutional home for nuclear expertise [[18]](https://read.uolpress.co.uk/read/the-social-and-political-life-of-latin-american-infrastructures-eab4760e-3328-4053-8029-9ecae2dc08c2/section/66636b25-739e-45fa-8237-b3dde8b18fc3). When the project suspended, these trained specialists were abruptly unemployed in their field. Some absorbed into teaching positions; many emigrated or adapted to entirely different professions. The institutional structures survived but functioned at greatly reduced capacity [[18]](https://read.uolpress.co.uk/read/the-social-and-political-life-of-latin-american-infrastructures-eab4760e-3328-4053-8029-9ecae2dc08c2/section/66636b25-739e-45fa-8237-b3dde8b18fc3).

The suspension also signified an end to Cold War technology transfer in this sector. No substitute reactor technology was proposed by Cuba or any other state actor after 1992. Cuba's power generation strategy became instead dependent on volatile fuel-oil imports, aging thermoelectric plants, and later (from the 2000s onward) renewable energy projects of modest scale. The non-completion of Juraguá meant that Cuba's electricity supply would never transition away from the combustion-dependent, import-vulnerable model that characterized it in 1976.
