# The Olympic Stadium roof

The Montreal Olympic Stadium, built for the 1976 Summer Olympics, was designed by French architect Roger Taillibert as a distinctive structure with a retractable Kevlar roof. The stadium opened for the Games on schedule in July 1976 [[8]](https://www.nytimes.com/1976/07/18/archives/opening-of-olympics-is-hailed-in-montreal-opening-of-olympics-is.html). However, the retractable roof system—the structure's defining feature—was not installed until April 1987, more than a decade after the stadium opened [[16]](https://stadiumdb.com/designs/can/montreal_olympic_stadium). The inclined tower that supported the roof mechanism was only completed at that same time, over 11 years after the Olympic events concluded [[14]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/montreal-olympic-stadium-problems-1.4397761).

The retractable Kevlar roof arrived with severe operational constraints that made it impractical for regular use. The system could not operate in wind speeds exceeding 25 kilometres per hour. Once deployed or retracted, the roof required approximately one week for the Kevlar material to acclimate before being moved again—the design effectively limited the roof to monthly deployment cycles. Yet the system arrived with promotional promises and civic pride attached to the notion of a roof that could open for summer events. The public expected, and demanded, frequent demonstrations.

Between 1987 and 1998, the roof was opened and closed only 80 to 88 times in 11 years—roughly 7 to 8 openings per year [[14]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/montreal-olympic-stadium-problems-1.4397761). Despite this modest use, the Kevlar material tore repeatedly. In June 1989, approximately 8,000 spectators were evacuated when the roof tore during an event [[14]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/montreal-olympic-stadium-problems-1.4397761). A major 120-metre tear occurred in June 1991, and the system continued to experience deterioration [[14]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/montreal-olympic-stadium-problems-1.4397761). The operational constraints and repeated failures made clear that the retractable roof design, while ambitious and visually distinctive, did not match the practical needs of a sports facility requiring reliable, weather-independent operation.

In 1998, after 11 years of operation plagued by tears and weather constraints, the retractable system was abandoned and removed [[14]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/montreal-olympic-stadium-problems-1.4397761). A fixed roof structure was installed to replace it. This second roof, while more reliable than the retractable system, developed its own failure pattern. Over its 26-year operational span (1998–2024), approximately 7,453 documented rips occurred in the fixed roof material, requiring continual repair and eventual replacement [[15]](https://canada.constructconnect.com/dcn/news/projects/2026/01/fifty-years-after-the-games-quebecers-still-spending-on-montreals-olympic-stadium).

As of January 2026, Montreal's Olympic Stadium began 2026 the same way it began the 1976 Games: under construction and without a roof [[15]](https://canada.constructconnect.com/dcn/news/projects/2026/01/fifty-years-after-the-games-quebecers-still-spending-on-montreals-olympic-stadium). In February 2024, Quebec's government announced a third roof replacement project at an estimated cost of \$870 million, with completion targeted for 2028 [[17]](https://www.costar.com/article/1657859168/quebec-to-spend-870-million-on-new-roof-for-montreals-olympic-stadium). The stadium remains operational for events but continues to be a construction site 50 years after the Olympics. The retractable roof concept—the distinguishing engineering innovation that defined the stadium—was superseded by fixed-roof solutions within a decade of installation. The technology failed but the building persists as an operational venue that generates ongoing public expenditure.

The Olympic Stadium roof case demonstrates how engineering ambition can exceed operational reality. A technically innovative system designed to be distinctive can prove impractical at the scale and frequency of actual use. Unlike Mirabel or the REM de l'Est, the Olympic Stadium was not abandoned; it continues to serve its primary function. However, the core engineered feature that defined the project—the retractable roof system—failed and was abandoned within its first decade of operation, requiring replacement with simpler fixed-roof systems that, themselves, have proven problematic.
