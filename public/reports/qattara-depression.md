# The Qattara Depression Project

Beyond the immediate Toshka and New Valley area, but within the same broader Western Desert region of Egypt, lies another major abandoned project: the Qattara Depression Project. Though not directly in Toshka, this macro-engineering scheme represents the apex of abandoned infrastructure ambitions in Egypt's desert, and its cancellation in 2026 marks a final closure on a vision pursued for nearly a century.

The Qattara Depression is a vast natural basin in the Western Desert, situated approximately 60 meters below sea level [[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qattara_Depression_Project). The depression was initially surveyed by the British in the 1920s, and in 1927, engineers proposed a visionary scheme: construct a canal or tunnel from the Mediterranean Sea, 55–80 kilometers to the north of Qattara, to bring seawater down into the depression [[26]](https://www.nytimes.com/1981/03/28/business/egypt-planning-vast-desert-hydroelectric-project.html). The water would descend from sea level into the sub-sea-level basin, creating a massive head of water pressure. This pressure would drive turbines generating hydroelectric power—potentially enormous quantities of electricity—while the seawater would fill the depression, creating an inland sea [[23]](https://alchetron.com/Qattara-Depression-Project) [[25]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Qattara_Depression_Project).

The concept captured imaginations across generations and geographies. During the Cold War era, in 1957, the U.S. Central Intelligence Agency examined the project's feasibility as part of broader Middle East engineering schemes [[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qattara_Depression_Project). In the 1970s, German engineering firms, particularly Bassler, produced detailed technical assessments [[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qattara_Depression_Project). In 1981, The New York Times reported on Egyptian engineers under President Sadat who were seriously considering the project as a potential massive hydroelectric development [[26]](https://www.nytimes.com/1981/03/28/business/egypt-planning-vast-desert-hydroelectric-project.html).

Remarkably, in April 2023—nearly 100 years after the original British proposal—the Egyptian government commissioned a new feasibility study for the Qattara Depression Project, signaling a renewed interest in the scheme [[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qattara_Depression_Project). However, this revival proved brief.

**In May 2026, the Egyptian government formally abandoned the Qattara Depression Project**, determining after reviewing the feasibility study that the economic benefits would not justify the enormous capital cost, that environmental and ecological consequences were unacceptable, and that technical obstacles were more severe than earlier optimists had believed [[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qattara_Depression_Project). The concerns included massive groundwater salinization if seawater mixed with aquifers, destruction of the desert ecosystem, interference with oil and natural gas operations in the region, and the astronomical cost of excavating a 55–80 kilometer canal or tunnel through bedrock to sea level [[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qattara_Depression_Project). The project, which had been revived in proposals involving nuclear explosions (some Cold War-era planners had proposed using nuclear charges to excavate the canal quickly), was finally and explicitly abandoned.

**What survived:** only the concept in historical records, technical papers, and popular imagination. No physical infrastructure was ever constructed. The proposal remains an archive of engineering ambition—an idea pursued repeatedly across multiple eras by multiple nations, none of which ultimately found the vision economically justified or environmentally acceptable.
