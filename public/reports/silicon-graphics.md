# Silicon Graphics

## What Was Built

Silicon Graphics Incorporated (SGI) emerged as one of Mountain View's most consequential technology companies and a pioneering force in 3D graphics computing. Founded in November 1981 in Mountain View, California, SGI specialized in manufacturing high-performance graphics workstations and supercomputers. The company's headquarters and manufacturing campus was located at **1600 Amphitheatre Parkway in Mountain View**, a facility that would eventually become an architectural landmark. The Amphitheatre Parkway campus was completed in 1997 and designed as an unconventional corporate campus that broke from traditional office layouts, reflecting SGI's innovative culture at its peak. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Graphics) [[11]](https://pcad.lib.washington.edu/building/3578/)

SGI dominated the visualization and high-performance computing market throughout the 1980s and 1990s, providing essential tools for scientific research, film production (including Pixar's pioneering work), and industrial design. The company's graphics technology became synonymous with professional-grade 3D visualization.

## Failed Platforms and Discontinued Initiatives

By the mid-1990s and into the 2000s, SGI's market position began to deteriorate as the company attempted multiple platform transitions that ultimately failed to achieve commercial viability:

**Intel Itanium Platform Transition (mid-1990s–2000s):** SGI invested heavily in transitioning its product line from MIPS processors to Intel's Itanium architecture. This strategic pivot was intended to capitalize on Intel's processor development roadmap and reduce manufacturing costs. The initiative failed to gain traction in the market. Despite Itanium's technical capabilities, the platform never achieved the broad industry adoption that Intel and its partners projected, and SGI's investment in this transition diverted resources from core business operations.

**Linux and Intel IA-32 Systems (1990s–2000s):** As commodity Linux-based systems became increasingly competitive and cost-effective, SGI attempted to develop and market Linux Intel IA-32 systems to compete with lower-cost alternatives. This effort failed to penetrate the market meaningfully, as SGI could not overcome the price competition from pure-commodity manufacturers and open-source alternatives.

**Supercomputer Repositioning (mid-2000s):** In the mid-2000s, as high-performance computing markets shifted, SGI attempted to reposition itself in supercomputing and enterprise data centers. This initiative was undertaken too late and with insufficient resources to establish a viable competitive position.

## The Path to Dissolution

SGI's financial position deteriorated steadily. By 2006, the company relocated its headquarters from Mountain View to Sunnyvale, marking a symbolic end to the company's identity as a Mountain View anchor. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Graphics) The company filed for **Chapter 11 bankruptcy on April 1, 2009**, and its assets were sold to **Rackable Systems on May 11, 2009**. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Graphics)

## Geographic Connection and Facility Disposition

The Amphitheatre Parkway campus in Mountain View represents a direct geographic marker for SGI's presence and failure. The facility was constructed during the company's market dominance era. After SGI's decline, the property was acquired by Google, which initially leased the campus in 2003 before completing a purchase in 2006. The site now serves as the **Googleplex**, Google's international corporate headquarters, a transformation that symbolizes the regional transition from one computing era to another. [[11]](https://pcad.lib.washington.edu/building/3578/) [[10]](https://www.dilwortharch.com/works/sgi-google-headquarters)

## What Survived

Critically, SGI's organizational dissolution did not represent a complete technological failure. **OpenGL**, SGI's open graphics standard, was released as open source in 1999 and continues as a foundational technology in computer graphics. The open standard outlived the corporation, becoming adopted across industries and remaining relevant decades after SGI's bankruptcy. SGI's intellectual property was later acquired by Silicon Graphics International Corp., though the original company's market leadership and independent operations ceased. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon_Graphics)

## Status and Legacy

**Status:** Dissolved through bankruptcy; assets acquired and reorganized; brand and technology disseminated through open-source standards and acquisition. SGI represents a case where organizational failure occurred despite ownership of valuable intellectual property, due primarily to failed platform transitions, inability to compete on price in commodity markets, and strategic decision-making that diverted focus from core business strengths.
