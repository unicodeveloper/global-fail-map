# Metrolima

## The Vision: A Five-Line Underground Network

Between 1972 and 1973, a Swedish consulting firm, in partnership with Peruvian planners under the "Metrolima" consortium, designed an ambitious underground rapid transit network for the Lima-Callao metropolitan area. The proposal encompassed five integrated metro lines covering the entire urban region, estimated to cost **US\$317 million**—a substantial sum for Peru at the time. The design was formally approved by the Peruvian government in 1974, representing official commitment to the concept [[22]](https://metroeasy.com/peru/lima-metro/).

## Why It Mattered: Addressing Urban Mobility

In the early 1970s, Lima's population was growing rapidly while surface transportation remained chaotic. An underground metro system would have provided integrated, high-capacity transit serving workers, students, and residents across the metropolitan area. The planned network represented Peru's most comprehensive urban transit vision of that era, anticipating metropolitan growth patterns that would intensify over subsequent decades.

## The Failure: Three Convergent Crises

The project never progressed beyond the design phase. Three interconnected factors prevented implementation:

**Political Instability:** In 1973, President Juan Velasco Alvarado suffered a health crisis that triggered succession uncertainty and institutional instability. The political environment became inhospitable for launching a massive new infrastructure commitment [[7]](https://www.railway-technology.com/projects/lima-metro/).

**Technical Complexity:** Lima's location in one of the world's most seismically active regions posed extraordinary engineering challenges for underground construction. The geology of the Lima basin, combined with earthquake risk, made underground transit construction technically demanding and risk-laden compared to surface alternatives [[22]](https://metroeasy.com/peru/lima-metro/). Seismic design requirements would have substantially increased project costs.

**International Financial Crisis:** The global economic crisis of the mid-1970s, triggered by oil price shocks and currency instability, made securing international financing for the \$317 million project effectively impossible [[7]](https://www.railway-technology.com/projects/lima-metro/). Peru's foreign exchange situation deteriorated sharply, eliminating the possibility of external funding.

## What Survived: Conceptual Foundations

Although the physical metro system was never built, the design work and feasibility studies created intellectual capital that influenced subsequent transit planning. The five-line concept reappeared in later government proposals, including the 2010 Metro Network Plan approved by Peru's government, which adopted conceptual elements from the original Metrolima design. The idea of integrated underground rapid transit for Lima remained alive in planning discourse, even though the 1970s attempt never materialized [[7]](https://www.railway-technology.com/projects/lima-metro/).

Current status as of 2026: Cancelled/Abandoned. No portion of the original five-line system has ever been constructed.
