# The Xingu dam cascade

## The Cascade Plan

In 1979, Brazil's state electricity monopoly (Eletronorte) completed an ambitious master plan for the Xingu River basin. The plan called for a cascade of six dams: five on the Xingu main stem (Jarina, Kokraimoro, Ipixuna, Babaquara, and Kararaô) plus Cachoeira Seca on the Iriri tributary. [[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belo_Monte_Dam) The total flood zone would have covered approximately 2 million hectares, and installed capacity would have reached 24 gigawatts—making it one of the world's largest hydroelectric complexes. [[26]](https://www.wrm.org.uy/bulletin-articles/brazil-belo-monte-dam-would-turn-the-xingu-river-into-a-river-of-blood) The rationale was consistent with the development ideology: the hydroelectric capacity would fuel Brazil's industrial expansion; the flooded areas, having been "unused," could be sacrificed for the national interest.

## The 1989 Altamira Encounter and Indigenous Mobilization

This plan met resistance that had no precedent at the scale of the Trans-Amazonian Highway. On February 19–23, 1989, Indigenous nations and riverine communities converged on Altamira for the Encuentro das Nações Indígenas do Xingu (Xingu Nations Indigenous Encounter). [[25]](https://riverresourcehub.org/resources/xingu-encounter-altamira-brazil-3618/) [[29]](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/news/indigenous-gathering-altamira-brazil-defense-xingu-river) Over 3,000 participants—primarily Kayapó, Juruna, Arara, and other Xingu-basin nations—gathered at Altamira's port to publicly oppose the dam cascade. The encounter was explicitly organized to block the dams, and it succeeded. [[29]](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/news/indigenous-gathering-altamira-brazil-defense-xingu-river)

The timing was crucial. The 1988 Brazilian Constitution, signed just months before, had formalized Indigenous territorial rights and established Indigenous veto rights over development projects affecting their lands. [[9]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Trans-Amazonian_Highway) The World Bank, under changing environmental standards in the late 1980s, withdrew financing support for the project. International pressure from environmental organizations amplified local Indigenous opposition. By February 1989, the political calculus had shifted irreversibly.

## Cancellation Outcomes

Between 1989 and the mid-1990s, Eletronorte formally abandoned four of the six proposed dams: Jarina, Kokraimoro, Ipixuna, and Babaquara. No dams were constructed at these sites. Cachoeira Seca on the Iriri was similarly abandoned. Only the Kararaô site was pursued further, but as a standalone dam rather than part of a cascade. Kararaô was subsequently renamed Belo Monte and is discussed separately below because it ultimately achieved operational status and must be excluded from the category of failed projects.

The 1989 Altamira Encounter stands as a watershed moment: it marked the first time that large-scale Indigenous territorial assertion successfully halted a major Amazonian infrastructure project, and it established a precedent that subsequent projects would have to contend with Indigenous consent requirements.
