# The Bootless Bay Railway

## Construction and Operations

A second railway in the Port Moresby region actually achieved physical construction and operational status, but survived only briefly. The Bootless Bay Railway, operated by the New Guinea Copper Mines Ltd, served the Laloki and Dubuna copper mine workings located near Port Moresby. Construction commenced in 1918 and reached completion in 1921, with operations commencing in 1922. [[8]](https://exkiap.net/forum/viewtopic.php?t=1350) [[19]](https://www.mininghistory.asn.au/png-mining-history/) [[20]](https://mra.gov.pg/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Historical-Overview-of-Mining-in-PNG.pdf) The facility was operationally more successful than the colonial Port Moresby to Rona scheme, actually processing ore and generating significant employment.

At its peak, the mining operation employed approximately 1,000 Papuan laborers and 100 expatriate personnel. [[20]](https://mra.gov.pg/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Historical-Overview-of-Mining-in-PNG.pdf) Ore extracted from the Laloki and Dubuna deposits was transported by rail to a smelter facility at Bootless Bay, where initial processing occurred before export through Port Moresby's wharves. The operation thus formed an integrated production chain—mining, transportation, smelting, and maritime export—that generated tangible economic activity and connected the interior to the coast.

## Operational Collapse and Equipment Dispersal

The Bootless Bay Railway ceased operations in 1926, following what historical records describe as "operational difficulties." [[19]](https://www.mininghistory.asn.au/png-mining-history/) [[20]](https://mra.gov.pg/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Historical-Overview-of-Mining-in-PNG.pdf) Behind this euphemistic phrase lay more complex technical and commercial failures. The mine encountered intractable operational challenges: mine accidents, structural collapses within the ore bodies, refractory ore geology that resisted processing, deteriorating equipment, accumulating debts, and the stagnating global copper price of the mid-1920s. When the New Guinea Copper Mines Ltd sought financial support from the Australian Treasury to sustain operations, the government refused to advance additional funds. [[20]](https://mra.gov.pg/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Historical-Overview-of-Mining-in-PNG.pdf) The combination of technical dysfunction, commercial unviability, and absent state support proved terminal.

The railway equipment did not remain idle in Port Moresby. Most of the locomotives and rolling stock were dismantled and sold as scrap. A substantial portion of this equipment found its way to Japan in the 1950s and early 1960s; the last recorded locomotive shipment to Japan occurred in 1961. [[19]](https://www.mininghistory.asn.au/png-mining-history/) [[20]](https://mra.gov.pg/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Historical-Overview-of-Mining-in-PNG.pdf) This dispersal of equipment meant that no infrastructure remained to resume operations or serve as a preserved artifact of the enterprise.

## Ghostly Artifact and Broader Significance

In the late 1970s, nearly 50 years after the railway ceased operations, a piece of mining-era machinery was unearthed at Ela Beach in Port Moresby. Contemporary accounts describe it as a "contraption"—likely a steam locomotive or traction engine—that surfaced during urban development or excavation. [[8]](https://exkiap.net/forum/viewtopic.php?t=1350) The artifact was displayed publicly at some point, but subsequently lost to city council actions or development pressure; its current location or preservation status remains undocumented as of 2026. [[8]](https://exkiap.net/forum/viewtopic.php?t=1350)

The Bootless Bay Railway thus followed a trajectory from constructed infrastructure to operational success to commercial failure to physical dispersal and artifact loss. A later mining effort in the same field (Laloki and Dubuna) was undertaken in 1938–1942 by a different operator and achieved "moderate success," [[20]](https://mra.gov.pg/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Historical-Overview-of-Mining-in-PNG.pdf) suggesting that the earlier infrastructure failure reflected operational and commercial misjudgment rather than inherent resource limitation. The first mining entrepreneur and operator did not succeed; the field itself retained extractive potential.

Archival evidence for the Bootless Bay operation survives in the form of photographs held by the Australian National University Archives, including the Frank Gosson collection of photographs from the Astrolabe mineral field (Laloki and Dubuna mines), dated 1918–1924. [[18]](https://archivescollection.anu.edu.au/index.php/frank-gossons-collection-of-photographs-from-rouna-falls-papua-new-guinea) These photographs document the physical infrastructure, equipment, personnel, and operations of the enterprise at its peak, providing visual corroboration of the enterprise's technical sophistication and scale.
