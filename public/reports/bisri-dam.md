# The Bisri Dam

The Bisri Dam project illustrates how international financing structures, while well-intentioned, can collapse when local institutional capacity fails to meet explicitly stated conditions. The dam was designed to address a critical and chronic problem: 1.6 million people in the Greater Beirut and Mount Lebanon region faced severe water scarcity, relying on expensive tanker deliveries at approximately \$20 per cubic meter instead of public utility rates available where supply existed. [[3]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/bisri-dam) [[2]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2020/09/04/bisri-dam-project-cancelation)

The World Bank's Water Supply Augmentation Project (which included the Bisri Dam as its centerpiece) received financing commitment of \$244 million in undisbursed World Bank loans at the moment of cancellation. The Government of Lebanon, meanwhile, had already expended approximately \$197 million on project preparation and land expropriation—a substantial sunk cost reflecting roughly 32 percent of the total project budget consumed before any construction commenced. [[2]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2020/09/04/bisri-dam-project-cancelation) This expenditure was not wasted in the conventional sense; rather, it demonstrated the government's initial commitment: Lebanese authorities had processed 99.8 percent of required land expropriations, compensating 861 landowners with approximately \$155 million in total compensation. [[2]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2020/09/04/bisri-dam-project-cancelation) The project site itself was located 35 kilometers south of Beirut immediately upstream of the village of Bisri, with designed water transport through gravity tunnel to the Wardaniyeh treatment plant. [[3]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/bisri-dam)

The dam's failure to proceed was not due to technical obstacles or financial insufficiency—it was due to failure to meet three explicitly stated preconditions that the World Bank established as contractual requirements:

**First precondition: Finalized Ecological Compensation Plan (ECP).** The ECP required development of ecosystem offset measures to address environmental impacts from the dam's water diversion. The Government of Lebanon submitted a draft ECP on August 12, 2020. The World Bank provided technical comments on August 25, 2020. However, the Lebanese government did not revise the plan to address World Bank concerns, and stakeholder consultation on the revised plan never occurred by the September 4 deadline. [[2]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2020/09/04/bisri-dam-project-cancelation)

**Second precondition: Finalized Operation and Maintenance arrangements.** Sustainable operation of the dam required agreement on how the water infrastructure would be managed after construction. The Lebanese government submitted a Memorandum of Understanding on August 24, 2020—just days before the deadline. However, this MOU departed from requirements specified in the original loan agreement, making it noncompliant with World Bank standards. [[2]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2020/09/04/bisri-dam-project-cancelation)

**Third precondition: Contractor mobilization at the worksite.** At the September 4 deadline, the project contractor had not mobilized personnel, equipment, or materials at the dam construction site—a clear signal that implementation was not imminent. [[2]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2020/09/04/bisri-dam-project-cancelation)

On September 5, 2020—one day after all three preconditions remained unmet—the World Bank notified the Lebanese government of partial cancellation of disbursements under the Water Supply Augmentation Project. [[2]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2020/09/04/bisri-dam-project-cancelation) The World Bank was legally obligated to withhold funding when contractual conditions were not satisfied. What appeared on the surface as a project cancellation was, more precisely, the automatic consequence of the Lebanese government's failure to execute three specific institutional arrangements within a defined timeline.

The consequences were immediate and consequential. The Benefit Sharing Program—a commitment to distribute community benefits from the project to affected residents—was discontinued. [[2]](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2020/09/04/bisri-dam-project-cancelation) The 1.6 million people who remained in water scarcity were left without the infrastructure that would have provided access to gravity-fed water at utility rates, forcing continued reliance on expensive tanker purchases and suffering the humanitarian consequences of water insecurity.

What ended was not merely a dam; it was a pathway toward water security for the region. What survived was institutional incapacity—the recognition that Lebanon lacked the administrative structures necessary to implement large-scale infrastructure projects even when international financing was provided.
