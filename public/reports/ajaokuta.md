# Ajaokuta Steel: 47 Years Unfinished, Two Pathways Forward

On June 1983, the Light Section Mill at Ajaokuta produced its first batch of reinforcement bars from imported Ukrainian billets. The plant was 84 percent complete, five years ahead of schedule, under a Soviet design contract signed in 1976 for \$1 billion. Ajaokuta Steel Company Limited was positioned to transform Nigeria into a continental steelmaking power. Today, 47 years after establishment and 43 years after that test run, the facility remains idle, consuming approximately \$100 million annually in maintenance and personnel costs while producing zero tonnes of integrated steel. Yet the plant still stands - 98 percent physically complete - with two competing international revival proposals advancing as of September 2026.

## The Ambition

In 1979, President Shehu Shagari's administration chartered Ajaokuta Steel Company Limited to build an integrated blast furnace-basic oxygen furnace (BF-BOF) complex designed to produce 1.3 million tonnes of liquid steel annually, expandable to 5.2 million tonnes across three phases [[5]](https://www.gem.wiki/Ajaokuta_Steel_plant). The site selection was geographically rational: 80 percent of raw materials lay within 60 kilometers - iron ore deposits at Itakpe (200 million tonnes at 36 percent iron content), limestone, and coal resources - plus access to the Niger River for water and transport [[8]](https://businessday.ng/backpage/article/ajaokuta-steel-reviving-nigerias-failed-industrial-project/). The 24,000-hectare Ajaokuta location sat at the geographic crossroads of Nigeria's 18 geopolitical zones, positioning domestic market reach and regional export potential.

The design was ambitious in scale. A single 2,000 m³ blast furnace would generate 1,355 tonnes of pig iron daily. Two 130-tonne basic oxygen converters would refine that iron into 650 tonnes of finished steel daily. Four rolling mills (Light Section, Wire Rod, Billet, and Medium Section) would shape the output into rebars, structural steel, and wire products [[7]](http://ascl.minesandsteel.com/page/index/about-us). The complex included a coke oven battery, sintering plant, three continuous bloom casters, and a 110 MW captive thermal power station. Initial construction contract cost: \$1 billion. Cost by February 1980: \$12.7 billion - a 1,200 percent escalation before construction began [[10]](https://fij.ng/article/after-gulping-10b-in-43-years-ajaokuta-steel-is-still-almost-ready/).

## What Happened

Between 1980 and 1983, construction proceeded at remarkable pace under the Shagari administration. By mid-1983, 84 percent of the plant was complete [[9]](https://www.environewsnigeria.com/twists-turns-around-34-year-old-ajaokuta-steel-company/). On June 1983, the Light Section Mill was commissioned ahead of schedule, producing test batches of reinforcement bars and billets using imported feedstock. In April 1984, the Wire Rod Mill followed, also producing test runs of rods and angles from Ukraine-sourced material [[3]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380458456_STALLED_AJAOKUTA_STEEL_PROJECT_THE_CONTRIBUTIONS_OF_GLOBAL_INFRASTRUCTURE_NIGERIA_LIMITED_2000-2015). These rolling mill operations demonstrated that secondary steelmaking equipment functioned. However, neither mill could operate as commercial enterprises: rolling mills are finishing processes that require continuous feedstock from integrated steelmaking. They cannot function without a source of semi-finished billets or slabs - precisely what the blast furnace was designed to provide.

The critical distinction is this: rolling mills reduce already-molten steel from ingots through a series of progressively smaller rolling stands. They are secondary processing. A blast furnace converts iron ore into pig iron; basic oxygen converters convert pig iron into liquid steel. This is primary steelmaking. Ajaokuta never achieved primary steelmaking. The 2,000 m³ blast furnace was never ignited.

Progress stalled during the 1985-1991 period. The Soviet Union's collapse in 1991 severed the technical partnership with Tyazhpromexport (the original contractor). In 1994, the plant was certified 98 percent physically complete - 40 of 43 designed plants had been constructed - yet the facility had never transitioned to commercial operation. Tyazhpromexport withdrew in 1994, citing Nigeria's breach of contractual obligations [[1]](https://dailynigerian.com/resuscitation-ajaokuta-steel/).

Between 1994 and 2003, the facility was effectively abandoned. No administration committed working capital. Two major concession attempts followed: SOLGAS Energy (June 2003 to August 2004) promised a \$3.6 billion rehabilitation package and 1.3 million tonnes of production within 18-24 months, then failed to deliver, resulting in an ICC arbitration award against Nigeria [[6]](https://journal.hep.com.cn/fem/EN/10.15302/J-FEM-2018011). Global Infrastructure Nigeria Limited (June 2004 to April 2008) acquired concession rights to Ajaokuta and the iron ore mines, but was terminated by the Yar'Adua administration in April 2008, triggering a 14-year arbitration dispute.

The GINL arbitration consumed Nigeria's attention and resources. Initial claim: \$10-14 billion in damages. After mediation (2020-2022), Nigeria settled for \$496 million on September 3, 2022 - representing a 91 percent reduction and recovery of operational control [[15]](https://www.thisdaylive.com/index.php/2022/09/04/fg-agrees-to-pay-496m-settlement-claims-on-ajaokuta-steel/). The first \$250 million tranche was released by September 19, 2022 [[16]](https://www.theafricareport.com/242447/nigeria-releases-250m-as-first-tranche-to-indian-firm-for-ajaokuta-steel-settlement/).

In 2018, small-scale test production resumed at the rolling mills using imported steel billets, briefly demonstrating that equipment installed 34 years earlier remained mechanically functional.

## Why It Ended

The suspension of Ajaokuta was not a sudden collapse but rather a series of rational decisions made by operators who recognized that commissioning a blast furnace without guaranteed raw material supply, continuous power, and technical expertise was operationally suicidal.

First, raw material supply never materialized as designed. The Itakpe iron ore deposits contain approximately 36 percent iron content - unrefined. The government built the National Iron Ore Mining Company (NIOMCO) to beneficiate this ore to 63-64 percent concentrate suitable for blast furnace charging. NIOMCO was never reliably operational [[2]](https://nairametrics.com/2018/08/23/why-ajaokuta-steel-company-cannot-produce-steel/). Without processed ore, the blast furnace could not be commissioned.

Second, transport infrastructure collapsed into a 26-year delay. The Itakpe-Ajaokuta railway, contracted to Julius Berger in 1987, was designed to carry 15 million tonnes of iron ore annually. Original completion date: 2019. Actual completion: September 2020. Thirty-three years elapsed between contract and operational railway [[13]](https://punchng.com/warri-itakpe-ajaokuta-rail-line-completed-says-minister/). A blast furnace cannot be idled once ignited; it requires continuous 10-15 year operation or metallurgical damage occurs. Without the railway, guaranteeing 15 million tonnes of annual ore supply was impossible.

Third, power supply remained insufficient. The facility required 300-500 MW continuous electricity. The 110 MW captive thermal power plant was supplementary, not primary. Nigeria's national grid never reliably delivered 300-500 MW to a single facility. By Q2 2025, the facility had accumulated \$1.39 billion in unpaid electricity arrears [[11]](https://businessday.ng/business-economy/article/idle-ajaokuta-steel-mill-costs-nigeria-n49bn/).

Fourth, technical continuity was lost. When the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, the technical partnership that engineered the design vanished. No Nigerian institution possessed expertise in commissioning a 2,000 m³ integrated blast furnace. Subsequent administrations lacked either the technical depth or the political continuity to pursue this knowledge transfer.

Fifth, policy discontinuity compounded the challenge. Nine presidential administrations (Shagari through Tinubu) managed Ajaokuta. Each regime change reset priorities. Capital allocation occurred sporadically. The facility consumed approximately \$50 billion annually in maintenance and personnel costs while producing zero integrated steel. By 2026, the N6.04 billion annual salary budget alone - for a non-operational plant - demonstrated the institutional burden [[14]](https://portal.minesandsteel.gov.ng/Home/ViewNews/35).

## What Survived

Despite 47 years of suspension, substantial physical and institutional infrastructure remains intact. The 24,000-hectare site is structurally sound. Forty of 43 designed plants were constructed: the blast furnace shell, two BOF converters, four rolling mills, coke oven battery, sintering plant, continuous casters, and thermal power station all stand in place [[7]](http://ascl.minesandsteel.com/page/index/about-us). The rolling mill demonstration in 2018 confirmed equipment integrity after three decades of idle storage.

The Itakpe iron ore deposit (200 million tonnes at 36 percent iron content) remains mapped and partially accessible. The design documentation from Soviet engineering remains preserved. The statutory entity Ajaokuta Steel Company Limited persists under the Federal Ministry of Steel Development. Most critically, the Itakpe-Ajaokuta-Warri railway, operational since September 2020, finally resolves the 33-year transport bottleneck - one of three essential prerequisites for integrated operation [[13]](https://punchng.com/warri-itakpe-ajaokuta-rail-line-completed-says-minister/).

One critical unknown: the internal refractories of the 2,000 m³ blast furnace after 30 years of idle storage. Refractories are ceramic linings withstanding 1,500°C molten iron. If degraded, replacement could cost \$200-500 million - a substantial hidden cost.

## Current Revival Proposals

Two competing pathways are advancing. In September 2024, Nigeria's Federal Ministry of Steel Development signed a Memorandum of Understanding with a Russian consortium comprising Tyazhpromexport (the original builder), Novostal M (a Russian EAF manufacturer), and Proforce Manufacturing Limited (a Nigerian firm) [[17]](https://nairametrics.com/2024/09/20/fg-signs-mou-with-russian-firms-to-revive-ajaokuta-steel-plant-national-iron-ore-mining-company/). The consortium conducted a preliminary inspection in August 2024. Details of financing, equity split, and implementation timeline remain undisclosed.

Parallel to the Russian pathway, Chinese partnerships have advanced to "very advanced stage" since February 2026. The proposed structure is a \$2 billion production-sharing arrangement in which Chinese investors raise financing subject to approval by China's National Development and Reform Commission, recover investment through revenue-sharing (initially 60-70 percent of output), and Nigeria retains ownership [[4]](https://businessday.ng/news/article/42-year-old-ajaokuta-plant-set-for-reboot-with-2bn-chinese-backed-plan/). Minister Shuaibu Audu publicly committed in July 2026 to finalizing an agreement before December 31, 2026. As of September 8, 2026, no finalized agreement has been documented.

## What the Next Attempt Can Learn

Ajaokuta's 47-year arc offers eight documented lessons for future large-scale industrial projects:

**1. Upstream integration cannot be sequential.** Raw material production (mining and beneficiation), transport (railway), and primary smelting (blast furnace) must be operational simultaneously or not at all. Ajaokuta designed the furnace before Itakpe beneficiation was commissioned and before the railway was contracted. A blast furnace cannot idle; it requires either continuous operation or decommissioning. The 26-year railway delay made commissioning suicidal.

**2. Continuous operation requires working capital, not just construction capital.** A blast furnace operates 10-15 years without shutdown. Post-1996, Nigeria could not sustain operational funding. Future projects must secure 5+ years of working capital to prove viability before scaling.

**3. Technology must align with local institutional capacity.** Integrated BF-BOF steelmaking is capital-intensive (\$1-1.5 billion per Mtpa), requires sustained technical expertise, and depends on integrated supply chains. Nigeria lacked both the technical depth and supply-chain resilience for Soviet design. Electric arc furnace (EAF) mini-mills - capital at \$300-600 million per Mtpa, 18-30 month commissioning, scrap-fed, and operationally flexible - would have been commissioned by 1990 and productive today [[12]](https://www.steel-technology.com/articles/oxygenfurnace).

**4. Transport infrastructure must precede furnace design.** Ajaokuta waited 26 years for its railway. Without transport connectivity, integrated smelting is impossible.

**5. Institutional durability requires statutory independence.** Nine administrations pursued Ajaokuta without sustained commitment. Future projects need multi-administration legal guarantees (congressional authorization with penalty clauses), not executive promises.

**6. Modular scaling outperforms megaprojects.** Rolling mills succeeded; integrated ambition failed. Phased expansion - starting with 400,000-tonne rolling mill capacity on imported feedstock (2-3 years), generating cash flow, then phasing in primary smelting - would reduce risk.

**7. Infrastructure accountability must precede funding.** Nigeria allocated \$10 billion without tying capital release to measurable production milestones. Future agreements require quarterly targets, external audits, and penalty clauses.

**8. Honest sunk-cost analysis is essential.** After \$10 billion with zero integrated production, marginal cost of restart must be compared to greenfield alternative capacity. That analysis is rarely conducted, suggesting Ajaokuta persists through sunk-cost fallacy rather than economic viability.

The Ajaokuta site demonstrates that geography and design do not guarantee outcomes. Sustained infrastructure sequencing, institutional continuity, technical alignment, and operational working capital determine whether industrial megaprojects move beyond symbolic incompleteness to productive operation.
