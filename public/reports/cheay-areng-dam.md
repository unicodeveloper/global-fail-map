# Cheay Areng Dam

**Project scope**: The Cheay Areng Dam was conceived as a 108 MW hydroelectric facility on the Areng River in the remote Cardamom Mountains of central Koh Kong Province [[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheay_Areng_Dam). Located at coordinates approximately 11°37′11″N 103°30′46″E in the Areng River valley [[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheay_Areng_Dam), the dam would have inundated a critical forest ecosystem supporting indigenous Chorng (Khmer Daeum) communities who have inhabited the region for centuries.

**Developer and timeline**: The project was pursued by Sinohydro International (later rebranded as PowerChina's subsidiary), a Chinese state-owned hydroelectric specialist that had previously constructed the Kamchay Dam elsewhere in Cambodia [[21]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/app5.187). This was itself a third iteration of hydropower ambitions for the Areng: earlier versions had been abandoned by other Chinese firms, suggesting persistent financial or technical barriers [[23]](https://theecologist.org/2014/jul/28/cambodia-indigenous-protests-repel-dam-builders-so-far).

The project underwent feasibility studies beginning around 2006 [[25]](https://www.bangkokpost.com/thailand/special-reports/1236942/pulling-the-plug-on-power-in-cambodia), with formal environmental and social assessments conducted in the early 2010s. By March 2014, indigenous Chorng communities had organized a physical blockade of access roads to the proposed dam site, establishing an unprecedented grassroots barrier to construction. Over approximately 11 months, villagers maintained the blockade at the Areng checkpoint, preventing any equipment or personnel movement [[18]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321782113_Place_Attachment_and_Community_Resistance_Evidence_from_the_Cheay_Areng_and_Lower_Sesan_2_Dams_in_Cambodia).

**Formal halt and community resistance**: In January 2015—not 2017 as sometimes cited in later accounts [[24]](https://zeitschrift-ip.dgap.org/de/ip-die-zeitschrift/archiv/jahrgang-2017/mai-juni/zu-komplex-und-fragmentiert)—the Cambodian government formally halted the Cheay Areng project following sustained community opposition [[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheay_Areng_Dam). The halt occurred as a direct result of indigenous mobilization, supported by international environmental organizations including EarthRights International and Samreth Law Group, which documented that "the Areng communities do not support this project" and had "not received any information or opportunity for dialogue" [[19]](https://earthrights.org/media_release/human-rights-concerns-over-hydropower-development-in-cambodia-brought-to-the-un/).

The potential displacement would have affected approximately 1,500 indigenous people [[18]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321782113_Place_Attachment_and_Community_Resistance_Evidence_from_the_Cheay_Areng_and_Lower_Sesan_2_Dams_in_Cambodia), comprising roughly five indigenous villages with long-standing settlement patterns and resource dependence on the river for fishing, water, and forest access. One villager's testimony captures the scale of the threatened disruption: "I cannot leave my ancestors here. If I abandon them, I won't know who I am." [[20]](https://www.npr.org/sections/parallels/2017/08/26/546036670/i-will-lose-my-identity-cambodian-villagers-face-displacement-by-mekong-dam)

**Why it failed**: Three factors converged to block this project where other infrastructure push-through:
- **Indigenous place attachment and organized resistance**: Unlike urban or semi-urban relocations, indigenous Chorng communities demonstrated unprecedented coordination and willingness to sustain physical blockade for 11 months [[18]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321782113_Place_Attachment_and_Community_Resistance_Evidence_from_the_Cheay_Areng_and_Lower_Sesan_2_Dams_in_Cambodia).
- **International human rights mobilization**: Documentation by EarthRights International, which brought human rights concerns to the United Nations in July 2017 [[19]](https://earthrights.org/media_release/human-rights-concerns-over-hydropower-development-in-cambodia-brought-to-the-un/), created external pressure on the Cambodian government beyond domestic political calculations.
- **Forest conservation precedent**: The Central Cardamom Protected Forest's status as one of Cambodia's last intact forest systems gave conservation groups legitimate standing to oppose the project as a matter of national ecological interest [[23]](https://theecologist.org/2014/jul/28/cambodia-indigenous-protests-repel-dam-builders-so-far).

**What survived and what was lost**: No physical infrastructure was ever constructed. The dam site remains unbuilt; the Areng River continues to flow unimpeded. However, the project's documentation, feasibility studies, and design work persist in archives, indicating that abandonment was a policy decision rather than technical obsolescence. The forest and community remained intact precisely because the blockade prevented ground works from commencing.

Critically, the Cheay Areng case represents the rare instance where community resistance definitively halted a major infrastructure project in Cambodia, in sharp contrast to the Dara Sakor resort or coal plant cases where displacement occurred despite organized objection.
