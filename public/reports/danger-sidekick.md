# The T-Mobile Sidekick

## Geographic and Contextual Note

While Danger Inc. was headquartered in Palo Alto (adjacent to but not strictly within Mountain View), its proximity to Mountain View and its significance as a Silicon Valley technology failure warrant inclusion as a comparative case that illuminates broader patterns in the region's technology ecosystem.

## What Was Built

Danger Inc. was founded in December 1999 by Andy Rubin, Joe Britt, and others. [[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danger,_Inc.) The company specialized in hardware design, software, and services for mobile computing devices. Its most notable product was the **Danger Hiptop**, marketed by T-Mobile as the **T-Mobile Sidekick** beginning in 2002. The device featured a distinctive swivel design with a physical QWERTY keyboard, making it distinctive from contemporary mobile phones.

The Sidekick ran proprietary DangerOS and was cloud-native, with most data stored on Danger's servers rather than locally on the device. This cloud-first architecture was visionary for 2002 but created critical infrastructure vulnerability. The device became iconic among teenagers and early adopters, particularly popular for its messaging capabilities, distinctive design, and social networking integration.

## Acquisition by Microsoft

Microsoft acquired Danger Inc. in **February 2008 for approximately \$500 million**, viewing the company as a strategic acquisition that would provide mobile computing expertise and a platform for Windows Mobile integration. [[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danger,_Inc.)

However, the acquisition proved problematic. Microsoft's internal product divisions competed for resources and attention. Integration was slow and ineffective. The Sidekick platform was deprioritized as Microsoft focused on its own Windows Mobile strategy. Most critically, the infrastructure that Danger had built—which ran on Sun/Oracle RAC database systems—was not upgraded or migrated to Microsoft systems after acquisition.

## Catastrophic Data Loss and Service Discontinuation

On **October 2, 2009**, Microsoft's Danger data center experienced a catastrophic failure. [[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Sidekick_data_loss) [[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T-Mobile_Sidekick) The company lost the ability to access user data for T-Mobile US Sidekick subscribers. At its peak, approximately **800,000 US subscribers** relied on Danger's cloud infrastructure for their personal data, including contacts, photos, and messages.

The data loss resulted from **backup systems failing**. [[5]](https://availabilitydigest.com/public_articles/0411/sidekick.pdf) [[6]](https://www.independent.co.uk/tech/red-faces-at-microsoft-as-server-failure-turns-smartphone-dumb-a3233531.html) Microsoft announced that all data stored for Sidekick users would likely be permanently lost, a stunning acknowledgment of data center mismanagement. Recovery took over two months, and not all data was restored. However, later reporting indicated that Microsoft recovered more data than initially announced, though customer confidence was permanently damaged. [[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2009_Sidekick_data_loss)

## Service Discontinuation

Despite recovery efforts, Sidekick's market position had been destroyed. T-Mobile and Microsoft announced that Sidekick service would be **discontinued on May 31, 2011**, marking the end of data services for the platform, though voice calling remained briefly. [[8]](https://rcpmag.com/articles/2011/03/02/tmobile-kills-microsoft-danger-for-sidekick.aspx)

## Why It Failed: Organizational Integration Failure

The Sidekick's failure was not rooted in fundamental technological flaws. The cloud-native architecture was appropriate and forward-thinking. The hardware design was innovative. The problem was **post-acquisition organizational mismanagement**: Microsoft failed to integrate Danger's technology and culture, failed to adequately fund and maintain infrastructure, and failed to provide vision for the platform's evolution.

## What Survived

Surprisingly, important technological descendants emerged from Danger:

- **Android Architecture**: Andy Rubin, Danger's co-founder, co-founded Android Inc. (acquired by Google in 2005) before Microsoft acquired Danger. The experience of building mobile operating systems at Danger informed Android's architecture.
- **Sidekick Brand Resurrection**: In 2011, the Sidekick brand was revived as an Android-based device, though it never achieved the cultural significance or market success of the original DangerOS version.
- **Platform Architecture Lessons**: Danger's infrastructure demonstrated both the promise and peril of cloud-native mobile architecture, influencing subsequent platform development.

## Status

**Status:** Dissolved. Danger Inc.'s operations were ceased by Microsoft. The service was discontinued in 2011. The company represents a case where organizational mismanagement (Microsoft's failure to integrate and properly maintain infrastructure) destroyed a viable technology platform. Unlike SGI's case, where platform transition decisions were poor, or Google Glass, where market rejection was decisive, Sidekick failed primarily due to post-acquisition integration failure and infrastructure mismanagement.
