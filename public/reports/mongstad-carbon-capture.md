# Mongstad carbon capture

## The Flagship Initiative and Its Undoing

In 2006, the Norwegian government and Statoil signed an agreement to develop what was intended to become the world's first full-scale industrial carbon capture and storage facility at Mongstad [[17]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/eet.2068). This project represented the centerpiece of the Jens Stoltenberg government's (2005–2013) climate strategy [[27]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0263774x15614660), framed not merely as an environmental necessity but as a demonstration of Norwegian technological leadership. Officials invoked the comparison to the Apollo moon landings [[23]](https://www.businessinsider.com/norway-carbon-capture-technology-center-mongstad-2012-5), positioning the project as a metaphor for transformative ambition.

The original vision was to capture approximately 2 million tonnes of carbon dioxide per year from the refinery and integrated combined heat and power (CHP) plant, with long-term sequestration in geological formations beneath the seabed [[6]](https://www.power-technology.com/projects/monstadchp/). The project was divided into two phases. Phase 1 would establish a test center to evaluate multiple capture processes and determine the most economical approach. Phase 2 would scale the proven technology to full commercial operation.

On May 7, 2012, Phase 1 materialized with the official opening of the Technology Centre Mongstad (TCM) at Mongstad, heralded as the world's largest test facility for carbon capture and storage [[20]](https://www.bbc.com/news/science-environment-18009623) [[22]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-norway-carboncapture-idUSBRE8460SE20120507/). The facility was jointly owned by Gassnova (acting on behalf of the Norwegian government), Statoil, Shell, and Sasol, represented by the corporate entity TCM DA (Technology Centre Mongstad Development Alliance) [[21]](https://www.tnp.no/norway/economy/2916-the-opening-of-carbon-capture-plant-at-mongstad-is-celebrated/).

Less than sixteen months later, on September 20, 2013, the outgoing Stoltenberg government officially abandoned the Phase 2 full-scale project. Minister of Petroleum and Energy Ola Borten Moe announced: "The development of full-scale carbon dioxide capture at Mongstad is discontinued" [[16]](https://www.reuters.com/article/business/environment/norway-drops-carbon-capture-plan-it-had-likened-to-moon-landing-idUSBRE98J0QC/). The government was "still committed to research and development," he added, but the full-scale deployment was terminated [[14]](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/world-europe-24183443).

## The Economics of Unviability

The cancellation resulted from a convergence of technical and economic factors that had become increasingly transparent during the planning phase. Initial cost estimates had projected a full-scale facility could be built for a sum measured in billions of Norwegian krone. By 2011, internal government projections had escalated dramatically to approximately NOK 20 billion (roughly \$3 billion USD at 2011 exchange rates) [[19]](https://researchgate.net/publication/259761609_CCS_potential_in_Norway_-_Exploring_the_role_of_flagship_projects_The_Mongstad_and_Karsto_case_studies). Between 2007 and 2013, the Norwegian state had already expended approximately NOK 7.4 billion on planning, engineering, and the construction of the test facility [[15]](https://www.fni.no/publications/exploring-paths-and-innovation-in-norwegian-carbon-capture-and-storage-policy).

The core economic problem was technological: post-combustion amine-based carbon capture, the most mature capture technology available at the time, suffered from a severe penalty. Deploying the capture system would require approximately 25–30 percent additional energy input to the facility [[27]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0263774x15614660), effectively raising the operating cost of the refinery substantially. The solvent used to capture CO2 degraded over time, requiring continuous chemical replacement. Scaling from pilot (TCM testing approximately 100,000 tonnes per year) to full industrial operation (2 million tonnes per year) exposed technical challenges in managing solvent chemistry, corrosion, energy intensity, and capital complexity that had not been fully resolved in earlier small-scale trials [[27]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0263774x15614660).

Concurrently, European refining fundamentals were deteriorating. Margin compression in petroleum refining, combined with rising energy costs and the emerging feasibility of renewable energy alternatives, made the economics of operating any energy-intensive refinery increasingly challenging. A full-scale CCS installation in this environment was untenable without permanent government subsidies [[13]](https://bellona.org/news/ccs/2013-09-mongstad-ccs-demonstration-plant-wrecked-by-incompetence-and-big-oil-shenanigans) [[15]](https://www.fni.no/publications/exploring-paths-and-innovation-in-norwegian-carbon-capture-and-storage-policy).

## What Remained Operational

Critically, the cancellation of Phase 2 did not result in the abandonment of the test facility. The Technology Centre Mongstad continued operations under restructured ownership: Gassnova retained 77.5 percent ownership on behalf of the state; Equinor (Statoil renamed in 2018) became the operating partner with 7.5 percent; Shell held 7.5 percent; and Sasol, a South African chemicals company, held 7.5 percent [[2]](https://www.nsenergybusiness.com/projects/mongstad-facility-norway/) [[18]](https://www.gem.wiki/Technology_Centre_Mongstad). TCM transitioned from being the developmental foundation for a commercial project to becoming a permanent research infrastructure.

In this role, TCM has remained continuously operational and active. By 2024, the facility was hosting trials of advanced capture technologies, including filter-based approaches from Canadian company Svante Technologies and other innovators not available during TCM's initial commissioning [[34]](https://tcmda.com/news/svante-to-test-groundbreaking-carbon-capture-filter-technology-at-tcm) [[37]](https://www.filtsep.com/content/news/svante-to-test-carbon-capture-filter-technology-at-tcm-in-norway/) [[38]](https://www.svanteinc.com/press-releases/svante-to-test-groundbreaking-carbon-capture-filter-technology-at-tcm/). Test operations by Svante commenced in October 2024 and were scheduled to continue through February 2025, with potential extension through the end of 2025 depending on flue gas availability from Equinor's refinery operations [[36]](https://www.bctechnology.com/news/2024/10/4/Svante-to-Test-Groundbreaking-Carbon-Capture-Filter-Technology-at-Technology-Centre-Mongstad-(TCM)-in-Norway.cfm?showid=48192&j=0&perpage=10&page=1&startrow=1).

As of November 2024, TCM management announced plans for a "new TCM," indicating active stakeholder confidence in the facility's future direction [[35]](https://tcmda.com/news/on-the-way-to-the-new-tcm). The center had become, in effect, a permanent industrial research platform rather than a demonstration project leading toward commercial deployment [[39]](https://tcmda.com/news/i-have-my-heart-in-mongstad).

The distinction is crucial: the *commercial full-scale project* was cancelled because its economics and execution risks proved unacceptable. The *technology development facility* survived and thrives because the underlying science of carbon capture remains a strategic priority for Norway and the participating companies. This reflects a sober assessment that technology maturity, not fundamental feasibility, was the limiting factor.
