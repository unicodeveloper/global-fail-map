# The Mambilla hydroelectric project

## The Ambition and Design

The Mambilla Hydroelectric Power Project represents the largest single electrical generation facility ever designed for Nigeria. The planned installation calls for **3,050 megawatts (MW) of hydroelectric capacity**, a facility intended to provide **30% of Nigeria's total electrical output by 2030** and serve as Africa's second-largest hydroelectric power station. [[10]](https://www.nsenergybusiness.com/projects/mambilla-hydropower-project-nigeria/) The engineering architecture includes a roller-compacted concrete main dam at **1,300 meters elevation**, four smaller interceptor dams (Nya, Sum Sum, Nghu, Api Weir), **33 kilometers of hydraulic tunnels**, two underground power stations with a combined **12 turbine-generator units**, and ancillary works designed to generate **4.7 billion kilowatt-hours annually**. [[1]](https://penglobalinc.com/mambilla-hydroelectric-power-station-a-transformational-project-mired-in-controversy) The facility is sited on the **Donga River near Baruf, Kakara Village, Sardauna Local Government Area**, approximately at the research coordinates provided (9.917513°N, 8.89794°E). [[10]](https://www.nsenergybusiness.com/projects/mambilla-hydropower-project-nigeria/)

Beyond electricity generation, the project was envisioned as a multipurpose infrastructure serving irrigation (water supply for agricultural development of the plateau's highland climate), flood mitigation, and water supply for **over 400,000 people** across the region. [[6]](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2024/07/integrate-mambila-hydroelectric-power-project-transaction-from-1999-to-date-senate-tells-tinubu/) The plateau's elevation (averaging **1,600 meters above sea level**, with peak Chappal Waddi reaching **2,419 meters**) creates a temperate microclimate distinct from Nigeria's tropical and arid zones, and the dam was designed to enable year-round irrigated agriculture including crops unsuitable to lower elevations.

## Chronology of Attempts and Failures

The project's lineage extends back further than commonly noted. **A preliminary feasibility study was conducted by Moto Columbus in 1972**, marking the first documented technical examination of the site. [[1]](https://penglobalinc.com/mambilla-hydroelectric-power-station-a-transformational-project-mired-in-controversy) For thirty years, the project existed in study phases. The actual turning point came in **2003, when Sunrise Power Limited secured a Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) concession agreement** for a **\$6 billion contract** to design, construct, and operate the facility. [[2]](https://businessday.ng/energy/power/article/inside-details-of-the-6bn-mambilla-power-dispute/) This moment appeared to signal imminent construction, and international attention intensified.

However, the Sunrise Power contract proved fatal rather than foundational. The contract was **terminated in 2007**, and the project was re-awarded to **China General Contractors (CGGC)**. [[5]](https://www.premiumtimesng.com/news/626393-mambilla-hydropower-project-saga-ex-minister-accuses-obasanjo-of-distortion-of-facts.html) In **2009, the CGGC award was rescinded**, returning the project to government control. These terminations were ostensibly due to performance concerns, but deeper examination reveals contractual disputes and allegations of corruption. An ex-minister later accused the Obasanjo administration of corruption in the original contract award and the selection process; the official reason cited for the 2007 cancellation was **"proven corruption on the part of officials who served under President Obasanjo between 2003 and 2007."** [[5]](https://www.premiumtimesng.com/news/626393-mambilla-hydropower-project-saga-ex-minister-accuses-obasanjo-of-distortion-of-facts.html)

The early 2010s brought another attempt at resolution. In **2012, the Federal Government of Nigeria moved to restore the Sunrise Power concession**, pairing it with a new Chinese contractor arrangement. [[1]](https://penglobalinc.com/mambilla-hydroelectric-power-station-a-transformational-project-mired-in-controversy) For a moment, momentum seemed restored.

The watershed event came in **November 2017, when the Federal Executive Council (FEC) approved the Mambilla Hydroelectric Power Project and awarded the engineering-procurement-construction (EPC) contract to a consortium led by China Gezhouba Group, Sinohydro Corporation, and Chinese state contractors (CGCOC)**, with **China Eximbank financing 85% of the cost** and the **Nigerian government responsible for 15% cost contribution**. [[2]](https://businessday.ng/energy/power/article/inside-details-of-the-6bn-mambilla-power-dispute/) This represented the most substantial commitment: a named contractor, identified financing source, and government approval. The \$5.8 billion price tag was established as the baseline for the expanded 3,050 MW design. [[4]](https://constructionreviewonline.com/construction-projects/construction-of-africas-second-largest-hydroelectric-plant-still-shrouded-in-mystery-eight-years-since-inception/)

Yet October 2017 also saw the initiation of **ICC (International Chamber of Commerce) arbitration proceedings filed by Sunrise Power Limited**, claiming breach of contract and seeking damages for the 2012 rescission. [[2]](https://businessday.ng/energy/power/article/inside-details-of-the-6bn-mambilla-power-dispute/) The arbitration dispute spanned three years, effectively freezing major project advancement while legal claims were litigated. The matter was not resolved until **February 2020, when a settlement agreement was reached**; the Nigerian government agreed to a **\$200 million settlement payment** to Sunrise Power, though **the payment status remains unclear as of the date of this investigation**. [[2]](https://businessday.ng/energy/power/article/inside-details-of-the-6bn-mambilla-power-dispute/)

Between 2020 and 2022, a revised project scope emerged. In **April 2021, following an audit by the Nigeria Sovereign Investment Authority (NSIA), the project scope was reduced from 3,050 MW to 1,525 MW**, reducing the estimated cost proportionally to approximately **\$4 billion**. [[9]](https://energyforgrowth.org/article/what-could-the-mambilla-dam-do-for-nigeria/) This represented an implicit acknowledgment that full financing for the original design was unattainable.

## The Operational Collapse and Current Dormancy

Remarkably, even with a named contractor, financing commitment, and revised design, the project advanced only marginally. In **June 2022, surveyors and engineering teams abandoned the site, citing non-payment by contractors over a two-year period**. [[3]](https://dailytrust.com/setback-for-mambilla-hydropower-project-as-surveyors-leave-site/) This was not a formal project termination but a de facto work stoppage—construction ceased due to cash-flow failure rather than explicit cancellation.

By August 2025, **the project remained inactive**. The House of Representatives expressed formal outrage, noting that the project had been **"on the drawing board for over 48 years"** despite initial concept dating to 1972, and that zero kilowatts of electricity had been generated. [[7]](https://africa-energy-portal.org/news/nigeria-house-expresses-outrage-over-failed-take-mambilla-power-project) The Senate demanded a comprehensive review of all transactions from 1999 to present, signaling institutional loss of confidence in the project's execution. [[6]](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2024/07/integrate-mambila-hydroelectric-power-project-transaction-from-1999-to-date-senate-tells-tinubu/)

## What Was Built and What Remains

The record shows a curious discontinuity. **The main roller-compacted concrete dam was actually constructed as early as 1982**, during a singular construction episode that produced the physical structure but no integrated generation facilities. [[1]](https://penglobalinc.com/mambilla-hydroelectric-power-station-a-transformational-project-mired-in-controversy) The dam stands on the Donga River and remains structurally intact. However, no turbine-generator units have been installed, no power station buildings have been completed, no transmission infrastructure connects to Nigeria's grid, and no water intake or outlet works for power generation have been commissioned. The dam exists as a historical artifact of an earlier attempt, devoid of functional purpose.

The engineering studies, feasibility reports, and technical designs from multiple iterations (Moto Columbus 1972, CGGC 2008, Gezhouba consortium 2017, NSIA revised design 2021) remain preserved as institutional knowledge and archived documentation. These documents represent a significant technical and intellectual asset, though their utility is diminished by the repeated rescoping and design changes across five decades.

## Geographic and Economic Connection to the Plateau

The project is geographically rooted in the plateau's water resources and topographic advantages. The Donga River originates on the Mambilla Plateau and flows northeastward, providing year-round discharge from the plateau's high rainfall and springs. [[31]](https://zingtimes.home.blog/2019/07/18/an-amazing-story-of-mambila-plateau-located-in-gembu-town-of-taraba-state/) The elevation differential—1,300 meters for the dam, with the plateau summit at 1,600-2,419 meters—creates substantial hydraulic head suitable for hydroelectric generation. The Kakara Village location places the dam within the Sardauna Local Government Area, the principal administrative zone of the plateau region.

Beyond the dam site, the project's economic reach extended plateau-wide: **50,000 direct construction jobs** were anticipated during the 5-7 year construction phase, skilled workforce training for operation and maintenance, transmission line construction across the region, development of associated infrastructure (roads, worker housing, water systems), and agricultural irrigation serving 400,000+ people. The displacement of 10,000+ persons due to reservoir inundation was acknowledged, though **compensation was not completed as of October 2021**.

## Evidence of Failure: Disputes, Financing Obstacles, and Institutional Dysfunction

The Mambilla project embodies multiple failure modes simultaneously. First, **contractual dysfunction**: three contract awards (Sunrise 2003, CGGC 2007, Sunrise-restoration 2012, Gezhouba 2017) with intervening terminations and rescissions created legal ambiguity and exposed differing interpretations of contractual obligations. The 14-year arbitration dispute (2003-2017) demonstrated that disputes over contract interpretation consumed nearly as long as the original project timeline was supposed to require.

Second, **financing fragility**: the project required 85% financing from China Eximbank. By 2021, Eximbank's appetite for the full 3,050 MW design had diminished—whether due to risk assessment, internal lending constraints, or changed political relationship is unclear. The scope reduction to 1,525 MW was explicitly tied to financing availability rather than technical or regulatory changes. This suggests the Chinese financing institution assessed the project as higher-risk or lower-return than acceptable, effectively vetoing the original scale.

Third, **political and administrative capacity gaps**: the gap between November 2017 (FEC approval, contract award) and June 2022 (site abandonment due to non-payment) shows a five-year period during which a government with authority and contractor with commitment could not maintain cash flows or project coordination. This is not a problem of technical infeasibility or resource scarcity (Nigeria's government budget far exceeds \$6 billion annually), but rather allocation failure, bureaucratic dysfunction, or competing political priorities.

Fourth, **compensation and social obligation failures**: the non-completion of compensation for 10,000+ affected persons as of October 2021 indicates that even the ancillary social obligations attached to the project were not met, suggesting that internal project management and stakeholder coordination had deteriorated significantly.
