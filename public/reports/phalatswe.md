# Phalatswe

## The Deliberate Relocation Decision

In 1889, Khama III, the paramount chief of the Bamangwato (also called Ngwato or Bangwato), one of the eight principal chieftaincies of Botswana [[27]](https://grokipedia.com/page/ngwato_tribe), ordered the relocation of his capital city from Shoshong, sixty miles to the west, to a new settlement called Phalatswe (meaning "the new place" in Setswana) [[25]](https://uu.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:889868/FULLTEXT01). This move represented a major administrative, political, and religious reorganization of Khama's expanding state. Phalatswe was constructed as a comprehensive capital, designed to consolidate Khama's rule, align the Bamangwato with Christian missionary influence, and project a modern city concept of governance [[25]](https://uu.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:889868/FULLTEXT01).

## Population and Physical Scale

Historical sources provide inconsistent population estimates. Some sources refer to the settlement as supporting 30,000 people, while others cite figures approaching 300,000 [[23]](https://www.botswanatourism.co.bw/explore/old-palapye) [[24]](http://www.africa-wilderness-safaris.com/botswana/old-palapye-phalatswe/). The measurement methodology and time periods for these estimates are not clarified in the available documentation, making it unclear whether the discrepancy reflects different counting methods, population fluctuations over the thirteen-year span, or regional boundary definitions. What is consistent across all sources is the characterization of Phalatswe as a major, densely populated urban settlement for the region and period.

## Infrastructure and Urban Development

Phalatswe was designed as a complete capital, not merely a trading post or military garrison. The settlement included administrative buildings for Khama's government, residential quarters for officials and extended royal family, merchant trading establishments, communal grain storage facilities, and water management systems intended to support the large population [[25]](https://uu.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:889868/FULLTEXT01).

The spiritual center of the settlement was the London Missionary Society (LMS) Church, constructed 1891–1894. This structure was a significant architectural undertaking: a Gothic-style edifice constructed of 410 tonnes of burnt brick, designed to accommodate 1,000 worshippers at capacity [[23]](https://www.botswanatourism.co.bw/explore/old-palapye) [[26]](https://www.knowbotswana.com/old-palapye.html). The construction cost was substantial for the period: estimates range from 3,000 to 4,000 pounds sterling, with Khama III personally financing much of the project and mobilizing the Bamangwato people through mephato (regiments) to provide communal labor [[23]](https://www.botswanatourism.co.bw/explore/old-palapye). The church was more than a religious building; it was a physical manifestation of Khama's alliance with European missionaries and his commitment to Christianization of the Bamangwato [[25]](https://uu.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:889868/FULLTEXT01).

## The Water Crisis and Abandonment (1902)

After thirteen years of settlement and significant capital investment, Phalatswe was deliberately abandoned in 1902. The cause was a fundamental constraint: the water supply was insufficient to sustain the population. The settlement had depended on natural springs and waterfalls in the Photophoto Valley, but these proved inadequate to meet the needs of the 30,000–300,000 inhabitants as the settlement matured [[23]](https://www.botswanatourism.co.bw/explore/old-palapye) [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palapye).

Faced with a chronic water shortage that threatened the viability of the capital, Khama III ordered a relocation of the administrative center and majority of the population to Serowe, approximately 60 kilometers to the south [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palapye). Serowe had more reliable water sources and was closer to emerging trade networks. The relocation was executed over 1902–1905, with much of the urban population departing as government and commercial functions were transferred.

## What Survived: Ruins and Memory

The LMS Church at Phalatswe was not demolished following the abandonment. The front and back facades of the 410-tonne brick structure have remained standing for over 120 years, weathered but recognizable as evidence of the settlement's former urban ambition [[23]](https://www.botswanatourism.co.bw/explore/old-palapye) [[26]](https://www.knowbotswana.com/old-palapye.html). The grave of Mmabesi, the wife of Khama III, remains at the site near the church, marking the settlement as a place of personal and political significance [[23]](https://www.botswanatourism.co.bw/explore/old-palapye).

Phalatswe is now designated as an archaeological and historical site, located approximately 20 kilometers east-southeast of modern Palapye town, near the village of Malaka in the Tswapong Hills [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palapye) [[23]](https://www.botswanatourism.co.bw/explore/old-palapye). The Botswana Tourism Organisation maintains it as a heritage attraction, documenting its status as "Botswana's first city" [[26]](https://www.knowbotswana.com/old-palapye.html). In 2023, the government allocated 60 million Pula for museum development and heritage documentation at Old Palapye, indicating ongoing commitment to preserving the historical record of the settlement [[6]](https://palapye.cdc.gov.bw/index.php/tourist-attractions).

## Broader Significance and Displacement

The establishment of Phalatswe and its later abandonment coincided with significant political and religious transformation of the Bamangwato kingdom. Under Khama III's rule (1875–1923), the chiefdom underwent rapid centralization, Christian conversion, and incorporation into the British colonial sphere. Phalatswe represented the apex of this transformation—a fully Christianized, administratively organized settlement that embodied Khama's vision of modern governance rooted in indigenous Bamangwato authority [[25]](https://uu.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:889868/FULLTEXT01).

However, the settlement also displaced prior inhabitants. The Batswapong people, the original inhabitants of the Tswapong Hills region where Phalatswe was established, were subordinated or relocated by Khama's administrative expansion [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palapye). The settlement's water shortage and eventual abandonment thus concluded a chapter of territorial consolidation that had adversely affected existing populations in the region.
