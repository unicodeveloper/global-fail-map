# The Hwang stem cell papers

## What Was Attempted: The Promise of Patient-Specific Stem Cells

Between February 2004 and May 2005, **Woo Suk Hwang**, a professor of veterinary science at **Seoul National University**, announced two landmark publications claiming success in human embryonic stem cell research using somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT)—the same cloning technique that had produced Dolly the sheep in 1996. [[1]](https://learning.eupati.eu/mod/book/view.php?id=328&chapterid=212) [[24]](https://www.nytimes.com/2006/01/10/world/asia/timeline-key-developments-in-the-south-korea-stem-cell-scandal.html)

The first claim, published in Science in March 2004, announced the creation of a human embryonic stem cell line from a cloned embryo. Hwang's team reported that they had isolated stem cells from a reconstructed embryo created by replacing the nucleus of a human oocyte with a patient's somatic cell nucleus, and maintained the resulting cell line in culture. [[1]](https://learning.eupati.eu/mod/book/view.php?id=328&chapterid=212) [[24]](https://www.nytimes.com/2006/01/10/world/asia/timeline-key-developments-in-the-south-korea-stem-cell-scandal.html) The second claim, published in Nature in May 2005, was far more ambitious: Hwang announced the creation of **11 patient-specific embryonic stem cell lines**, each derived from a different donor, each matching the genetic profile of a specific patient. [[23]](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(06)67896-3/fulltext)

## Why It Mattered: The Prospect of Personalized Stem Cell Medicine

The promise was revolutionary. Patient-specific embryonic stem cells—genetically identical to each patient—offered the theoretical possibility of transplanted tissues and organs that would not trigger immune rejection, addressing one of the fundamental challenges in regenerative medicine. Hwang's claimed 11 cell lines suggested that clinical applications for neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer's, Parkinson's), metabolic disorders (diabetes), and spinal cord injury were within reach. [[7]](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMp058323) The work positioned South Korea as the global leader in stem cell science, displacing the United States, where political opposition to embryonic stem cell research had constrained federal funding. [[2]](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-pacific-14968613)

## Geographic Connections

**Seoul National University** (SNU) in Seoul was the institutional home of Hwang's laboratory and the stated location of all research. [[1]](https://learning.eupati.eu/mod/book/view.php?id=328&chapterid=212) [[20]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7012409_Commentary_Fraudulent_Human_Embryonic_Stem_Cell_Research_in_South_Korea_Lessons_Learned) **MizMedi Hospital**, also in Seoul, served as the supplier of human oocytes for the research—a role that would later become central to the fraud investigation. [[23]](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(06)67896-3/fulltext) These two institutions constituted the entire geographic footprint of the work.

## What Ended and Why: Deliberate Data Fabrication

The fraud was comprehensive and deliberate. In December 2005, an investigative panel at Seoul National University determined that Hwang's research team had fabricated vital data in the papers published in both Science and Nature. [[4]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-korea-cloning-idUSTRE59P0QF20091026/) The investigation found evidence of "deliberate deception," not honest error. [[21]](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/stem-cell-scandal) Analysis by the Medical School of Seoul National University found "not even one single meaningful DNA data"—suggesting the entire dataset had been fabricated or heavily manipulated. [[32]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1215/s12280-008-9041-x)

The ethical violations extended beyond scientific misconduct to research methodology itself. Hwang's team had obtained human oocytes (egg cells) for research from women, some of whom were research staff members at his own laboratory. The ethical protocols required that egg donors be fully informed and act purely voluntarily, with no inducement beyond compensation for medical costs. Evidence emerged that **up to 1,100 eggs may have been used** across the two published studies—far exceeding what donors had originally agreed to contribute. [[23]](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(06)67896-3/fulltext) The procurement practices violated South Korea's bioethics regulations and international standards for human subject research. [[23]](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(06)67896-3/fulltext) [[46]](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2002/09/09/02-22794/findings-of-scientific-misconduct)

Hwang himself initially fought the findings, then gradually admitted to fabrication, claiming he had been "duped" by junior researchers—a defense that did not survive judicial scrutiny. [[30]](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna13699190) In his trial, he testified to multiple instances of deliberate falsification, eventually accepting responsibility for the fraud. [[28]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/science/disgraced-south-korean-cloning-scientist-testifies-to-mistake-1.604569)

The Seoul National University investigation produced a comprehensive institutional response. The university issued a public apology, calling the scandal "a blemish on the country" that had embraced Hwang as a national hero. [[18]](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2006/1/11/university-sorry-over-cloning-scandal) The government withdrew the prestigious title of "Distinguished Professor" and terminated his position. Colleagues who participated in the fraud were also indicted; the court record indicates that **at least five of Hwang's colleagues faced criminal charges** related to the fabricated research. [[1]](https://learning.eupati.eu/mod/book/view.php?id=328&chapterid=212)

## Legal Consequences and Final Conviction

After a three-year trial, the Seoul District Court convicted Hwang on October 26, 2009, on two counts: **embezzlement of government research funds** (approximately KRW 704 million in misallocated resources) and **bioethics law violations** (procurement of human oocytes in violation of informed consent requirements). [[44]](https://www.nytimes.com/2009/10/27/world/asia/27clone.html) [[45]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-korea-cloning/south-korea-stem-cell-scientist-guilty-of-fraud-idUSTRE59P0QF20091026/) The judge imposed a two-year prison sentence, but suspended it—meaning Hwang avoided actual incarceration. [[44]](https://www.nytimes.com/2009/10/27/world/asia/27clone.html) [[42]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/science/s-korean-cloning-scientist-avoids-jail-1.821864) The Supreme Court upheld the conviction in 2010. [[5]](https://www.science.org/content/article/korean-supreme-court-upholds-disgraced-cloners-criminal-sentence)

The relatively light sentence (suspended rather than active imprisonment) reflected the court's acknowledgment that Hwang had no prior criminal record, admitted to the fabrication, and that scientific fraud, while serious, did not rise to the level of violent crime in sentencing guidelines. However, the conviction was clear: the work was fraudulent, and the misconduct was deliberate. [[43]](https://www.the-scientist.com/no-jail-time-for-hwang-43797)

## What Survived: Snuppy the Cloned Dog—A Legitimate Achievement

Paradoxically, while Hwang's human stem cell work was entirely fraudulent, one major achievement emerged from his laboratory during the same period and withstood independent verification: **Snuppy**, the world's first cloned dog. [[47]](https://www.nytimes.com/2005/08/04/health/presenting-snuppy-proof-that-dogs-can-be-cloned.html) [[48]](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2005/8/4/worlds-first-cloned-dog-unveiled)

In August 2005—while the stem cell fraud remained undetected—Hwang's team announced the successful cloning of an Afghan hound puppy, named Snuppy. The dog was born in April 2005 after 1,095 embryo transfers into surrogate mothers. [[50]](https://www.npr.org/2005/08/03/4784301/meet-snuppy-the-worlds-first-cloned-dog) [[51]](https://www.nytimes.com/2005/08/04/world/asia/south-koreans-clone-dog-for-the-first-time.html) The announcement was made at a press conference at Seoul National University. [[47]](https://www.nytimes.com/2005/08/04/health/presenting-snuppy-proof-that-dogs-can-be-cloned.html)

When Hwang's stem cell research collapsed into scandal, many questioned whether Snuppy was also fraudulent. However, independent laboratories conducted DNA fingerprinting on Snuppy and confirmed genetic identity with the donor dog. [[49]](https://www.wired.com/2005/08/south-korea-is-first-to-clone-dog/) [[50]](https://www.npr.org/2005/08/03/4784301/meet-snuppy-the-worlds-first-cloned-dog) The dog cloning was scientifically legitimate—a genuine technical achievement, even though the broader research enterprise of which it was a part had been built on fraud. [[7]](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMp058323)

Snuppy survived the scandal as a documented, verified success. The animal remains a historical record that Hwang's laboratory, despite systematic fraud in stem cell work, had achieved a genuine milestone in mammalian cloning.

## Institutional and Regulatory Legacy

The Hwang scandal prompted substantial institutional changes in South Korea. The Korean government established new oversight mechanisms for embryonic stem cell research, including strengthened informed consent requirements and independent ethics review for human oocyte procurement. [[33]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51983691_Ethical_Modernization_Research_Misconduct_and_Research_Ethics_Reforms_in_Korea_Following_the_Hwang_Affair) Seoul National University implemented internal audit procedures for high-profile research. [[32]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1215/s12280-008-9041-x) The scandal became a case study in research misconduct training globally, cited by institutions including the U.S. Office of Research Integrity and included in regulatory guidance for oversight of human subjects research. [[46]](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2002/09/09/02-22794/findings-of-scientific-misconduct)

Notably, the scandal did not extinguish South Korea's commitment to stem cell research. Beginning in 2011, the government announced a renewed investment of approximately **\$89 million** to rehabilitate the country's stem cell research capacity and restore international confidence in Korean stem cell science. [[2]](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-pacific-14968613) By 2011, South Korea had reported successful **spinal cord injury treatments using stem cells** in a clinical capacity, working to reclaim scientific leadership in the field. [[6]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-korea-stemcell-idUSTRE76610C20110707/) [[13]](https://www.foxnews.com/health/south-korea-back-in-stem-cell-spotlight-with-new-treatment) This recovery demonstrated institutional resilience rather than abandonment of the research direction.
