# The Soviet Supercollider in Protvino: An Unfinished Accelerator and the Lessons it Holds

A 21-kilometer underground tunnel, 5.1 meters in diameter, lies dormant 20 to 60 meters beneath the fields and research buildings of Protvino, Russia, approximately 100 kilometers south of Moscow. The tunnel was fully excavated by December 1994, intended to house a particle accelerator of 3 trillion electron volts - three times more powerful than originally envisioned for the Large Hadron Collider at that moment in time. No magnet was ever installed. As of September 2026, the facility remains unfinished, yet it is not abandoned. The Kurchatov Institute, which now oversees the Institute for High Energy Physics (IHEP) at Protvino, has allocated 176 million rubles - approximately $25 million - to assess the tunnel's condition and explore revival. Russia spends approximately $2.7 million annually to pump groundwater from the intact but dormant ring, a perpetual cost with no scientific return, a permanent tax on a project that was frozen three decades ago. [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) [[10]](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna41763260) [[1]](https://boda.su/en/posts/id2021-inside-unk-protvino-the-soviet-collider-that-never-ran)

This is not a story of folly, though it contains elements of ambition outpacing resources. It is the story of a scientific megaproject caught between two worlds: the late Soviet Union's assertion of technological prowess in the Cold War, and the rapid disintegration of the state that conceived it. Understanding why Protvino's 21-kilometer tunnel survives partially complete, and what it reveals about the construction of particle accelerators, matters for builders of future experiments.

## The Ambition: Soviet Prestige and the Acceleration Race

Particle physics in the 1980s was, quite explicitly, a competition. The Soviet Union, having achieved the U-70 synchrotron - then the world's highest-energy proton accelerator at 70 GeV, commissioned on October 14, 1967 - faced the prospect of losing scientific dominance in an arena that symbolized technological leadership during the Cold War. [[16]](https://cerncourier.com/a/forty-years-of-high-energy-physics-in-protvino/) [[17]](https://cerncourier.com/a/golden-jubilee-in-protvino/) The U-70 had held the rank of world's most powerful accelerator, but by the mid-1980s, Fermilab in the United States had surpassed it, and the European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN) was planning even larger machines. [[7]](https://www.nytimes.com/1986/07/27/us/soviet-building-atom-smasher-world-s-biggest.html)

The response was the UNK - Universalny Nuklonno-Nuklonniy Kollaidyer, or Universal Nucleon-Nucleon Collider - conceived not merely as a research instrument, but as a restoration of Soviet leadership in fundamental physics. The ambitious design called for a machine capable of producing proton-proton collisions at a center-of-mass energy of 3 TeV, an energy that would not be achieved by any other facility for decades. [[11]](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017APS..APR.S2008K/abstract) [[14]](https://grokipedia.com/page/unk_proton_accelerator) The technical design involved a sophisticated chain: the existing U-70 synchrotron would inject protons into a 21-kilometer main ring, where they would be accelerated to unprecedented energies. [[2]](https://repository.cern/records/jqhna-bpm60) [[16]](https://cerncourier.com/a/forty-years-of-high-energy-physics-in-protvino/)

The site itself reflected this prestige calculus. Protvino, a "science city" (nauchnyy gorod) built in the late 1950s and early 1960s, was founded specifically to house IHEP when the Institute for High Energy Physics was formally established on November 15, 1963. [[16]](https://cerncourier.com/a/forty-years-of-high-energy-physics-in-protvino/) [[17]](https://cerncourier.com/a/golden-jubilee-in-protvino/) The location was chosen from 40 candidates in March 1958 for its geologically stable Paleozoic seabed soil - sediment that could withstand the vibration-sensitive requirements of a high-precision particle detector without significant seismic noise. [[2]](https://repository.cern/records/jqhna-bpm60) That same stability would later prove crucial in keeping the empty tunnel structurally sound for three decades.

The leadership of this effort embodied Soviet scientific authority. Anatoli Alekseyevich Logunov, a theoretical physicist of considerable standing who had earned the Lenin Prize in 1970 for his contributions to high-energy physics, was appointed director of IHEP in October 1963, serving until 1974. [[33]](https://grokipedia.com/page/logunov) [[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Logunov) Logunov returned as director again from 1993 to 2003, and served as Vice-President of the USSR Academy of Sciences from 1974 to 1991 - positions that granted him the institutional weight to advocate for the UNK project in the corridors of Soviet scientific power. He was twice awarded the Order of Lenin and received the rank of Hero of Socialist Labour, honors that reflected the state's investment in his vision. [[33]](https://grokipedia.com/page/logunov) After his death in 2015, IHEP was renamed the Anatoli Logunov Institute for High Energy Physics in his honor. [[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_High_Energy_Physics)

The UNK was not a side project. It represented one of the largest Soviet construction efforts of the 1980s, consuming resources, engineering talent, and political commitment at a scale typically reserved for military or space projects. The decision to accelerate construction came in 1987, when it became clear that the U-70 had fallen to fourth place globally in accelerator power, a status deemed intolerable by Soviet science leadership. [[7]](https://www.nytimes.com/1986/07/27/us/soviet-building-atom-smasher-world-s-biggest.html) [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/)

## What Happened: Chronology of Construction and Suspension

Construction of the UNK began in 1983. [[9]](https://www.abandonedspaces.com/uncategorized/soviet-hadron-collider.html) [[11]](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017APS..APR.S2008K/abstract) The project was initially paced, but in 1988 and 1989, the Soviet State Committee for Utilization of Atomic Energy purchased modern tunneling equipment, dramatically increasing excavation speed. By 1989, approximately 13 kilometers - 62 percent of the intended ring - had been excavated. By May 1990, 19.6 kilometers had been bored. [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) [[7]](https://www.nytimes.com/1986/07/27/us/soviet-building-atom-smasher-world-s-biggest.html) The scale of this effort becomes clear when one considers that conventional tunnel-boring machines of the 1980s could advance 100 to 200 meters per week under optimal conditions; the Protvino project sustained this pace for years, an extraordinary industrial undertaking. The tunnel itself - 5.1 meters in internal diameter, located 20 to 60 meters beneath the surface - was engineered to specifications matching contemporary accelerator designs. [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16904) [[14]](https://grokipedia.com/page/unk_proton_accelerator)

The full ring closure, the moment when the tunneling approached completion, occurred in December 1994. By that date, 20.77 kilometers of the 21-kilometer ring had been excavated. [[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNK_proton_accelerator) [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) The breakthrough meant that the geometrical framework of the machine was complete; the tunnel was intact, the shafts connecting to the surface were in place, and the experimental hall excavation was finished. The infrastructure for ventilation, electrical service, and groundwater management was installed or designed. 

But 1994 was also the year that funding began to evaporate. The Soviet Union had collapsed in 1991. The immediate aftermath - the period 1990 to 1993 - was marked by a collapse in science funding of approximately 90 percent. [[11]](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017APS..APR.S2008K/abstract) Wages for scientists and engineers went unpaid. By 1994, the state could no longer sustain the project. Construction officially halted, though work persisted at minimal levels through 2013, when it finally ceased. [[1]](https://boda.su/en/posts/id2021-inside-unk-protvino-the-soviet-collider-that-never-ran) [[9]](https://www.abandonedspaces.com/uncategorized/soviet-hadron-collider.html) [[12]](https://blog.adafruit.com/2011/02/24/the-lost-soviet-particle-accelerator/) The machinery that might have been installed - the thousands of tons of superconducting magnets, the RF cavities, the cryogenic systems, the detector apparatus - remained on drawing boards or, in some cases, in prototype stages. Some magnet components were constructed, but thousands of finished and partially finished magnets were placed in storage at Protvino, where they remain today, valueless as scrap. [[13]](https://www.wired.com/1997/12/science-2/)

## Why It Ended: A Cascade of Documented Causes

The termination of the UNK was not the result of a single decision, but rather a cascade of converging pressures that made continuation impossible.

**Economic Collapse:** The disintegration of the Soviet Union in 1991 was the primary driver. The science budget contracted by an estimated 90 percent within the first year. [[11]](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017APS..APR.S2008K/abstract) The 1998 Russian financial crisis delivered a second blow. What little funding survived these shocks was insufficient to complete an accelerator while simultaneously maintaining the basic operations of IHEP and other Soviet-era research institutions. Construction moved into a low-level maintenance phase, draining approximately $3 to 30 million annually - just enough to keep the tunnel from being flooded by groundwater, but nowhere near sufficient to install magnets or complete the machine. [[1]](https://boda.su/en/posts/id2021-inside-unk-protvino-the-soviet-collider-that-never-ran)

**Geopolitical Reorientation:** Coinciding with, and arguably accelerating, the funding collapse was a fundamental strategic reorientation of Russian particle physics. Rather than complete the UNK, the Russian scientific establishment chose to participate in the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) being constructed at CERN near Geneva. The CERN Council approved the LHC design in December 1994 - precisely the moment that UNK excavation was finishing. [[18]](https://cern70.cern/switching-on-the-large-hadron-collider/) [[24]](https://timeline.web.cern.ch/timeline-header/93) Russia signed a participation agreement with CERN in the mid-1990s, committing to contribute components, expertise, and funding to the European machine rather than complete its own. [[18]](https://cern70.cern/switching-on-the-large-hadron-collider/) [[23]](https://tass.com/science/1309989) This was presented, in official terms, as the "economically rational" choice: leverage European funding and international collaboration rather than bear alone the estimated \$2 to 4 billion cost of completing the UNK with superconducting magnets. [[1]](https://boda.su/en/posts/id2021-inside-unk-protvino-the-soviet-collider-that-never-ran) [[13]](https://www.wired.com/1997/12/science-2/) From the perspective of Russian science policy, the decision was logical. The LHC would provide access to higher energies and world-class experimental infrastructure; Russian physicists could participate in international collaboration without bearing the full financial burden of construction. This reorientation, however rational in narrow economic terms, transformed the UNK from the centerpiece of Russian high-energy physics into an albatross.

**Hydrological Liability:** A third pressure was geological and hydrological. Protvino sits above a high water table. Groundwater on some sides of the facility exists above street level. [[1]](https://boda.su/en/posts/id2021-inside-unk-protvino-the-soviet-collider-that-never-ran) [[13]](https://www.wired.com/1997/12/science-2/) An unfilled tunnel in this environment would be destroyed by flooding within four years. To abandon the project entirely would require filling the 21-kilometer ring with earth and stabilizing material - an operation that, according to calculations by Alexander Vasilevski, the chief engineer of the UNK project, would cost approximately $200 million. [[13]](https://www.wired.com/1997/12/science-2/) This was roughly equivalent to the cost of completing the accelerator itself. Thus, a perverse logic emerged: it was cheaper to maintain the tunnel indefinitely by pumping groundwater than to abandon it entirely by filling it. The annual pumping cost - approximately $2.7 million or 80 million rubles as of 2011 - created a permanent, non-negotiable budget item with zero scientific return. [[10]](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna41763260) This cost persists to the present. [[8]](https://www.tunneltalk.com/Russia-20May2021-Abandoned-Protvino-nuclear-physics-facility.php)

**Institutional Resource Reallocation:** A fourth factor was the reallocation of Russian particle physics resources toward other projects deemed more feasible. The Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) at Dubna, a multistate Soviet-era institution, pivoted toward the Nuclotron-based Ion Collider Facility (NICA), a heavy-ion accelerator designed to study the properties of nuclear matter at extreme densities. [[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclotron-based_Ion_Collider_Facility) [[27]](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/22981764-project-nuclotron-based-ion-collider-facility-jinr) NICA construction began in 2013; the facility achieved its first beam in the Nuclotron booster on December 19, 2020. [[28]](https://cerncourier.com/a/nica-booster-achieves-first-beam/) In effect, the Russian particle physics community decided to build a smaller, achievable machine at Dubna rather than attempt to complete the larger, costlier UNK at Protvino.

## What Survived: Infrastructure, Institution, and Dispersed Knowledge

Despite its incompletion, the UNK project left tangible and intangible legacies.

**Physical Infrastructure:** The 21-kilometer tunnel remains structurally sound. The 5.1-meter bore, the 20 to 60-meter depth, the ventilation systems, the electrical conduits, the access shafts - all remain in place. The experimental hall excavation is complete. The groundwater pumping infrastructure, installed in the 1990s and regularly maintained, keeps the tunnel dry. [[1]](https://boda.su/en/posts/id2021-inside-unk-protvino-the-soviet-collider-that-never-ran) [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) This is not a flooded ruin. It is a preserved, if unused, asset. The thousands of magnets and generators designed or constructed for the UNK remain in storage at Protvino, mostly in warehouses, their engineering and material value essentially preserved despite their lack of commercial utility. [[13]](https://www.wired.com/1997/12/science-2/)

**The U-70 Synchrotron:** The most significant surviving achievement is the U-70 synchrotron itself - the machine that was designed to serve as the injector for the UNK. The U-70, a 1.5-kilometer ring built between 1961 and 1967, achieved its first proton beam on October 14, 1967, at 76 GeV, then a world record. [[16]](https://cerncourier.com/a/forty-years-of-high-energy-physics-in-protvino/) [[17]](https://cerncourier.com/a/golden-jubilee-in-protvino/) The synchrotron employed 120 combined-function dipole magnets with a total weight exceeding 20,000 tons. [[2]](https://repository.cern/records/jqhna-bpm60) Although not used as a primary accelerator since the 1990s, the U-70 remains operational. It has been upgraded for heavy-ion experiments, demonstrating capability to accelerate carbon ions to 34.1 GeV per nucleon as of 2011. [[5]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Protvino) [[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_High_Energy_Physics) As of 2025, it is described as "the most powerful accelerator in Russia" for its sustained operations and experimental program. [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) The U-70's survival is crucial: it proved that investing in a machine with independent scientific value, rather than as merely a component of a larger system, provides institutional resilience. Had the U-70 been designed solely as a pre-accelerator for the UNK, with no standalone physics capability, it would have become obsolete when the UNK was suspended.

**Institutional Continuity:** IHEP survived the Soviet collapse and the suspension of the UNK. Under the directorship of Lev Solovyov (1974–1993) and his successors Nikolai Tyurin (2003–present), the institute maintained international scientific collaboration, particularly with CERN. [[16]](https://cerncourier.com/a/forty-years-of-high-energy-physics-in-protvino/) [[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_High_Energy_Physics) In 2009, IHEP was formally integrated into the National Research Center Kurchatov Institute, the successor organization to the Soviet Union's atomic energy establishment. [[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_High_Energy_Physics) The institutional framework that had supported the UNK's conception remained in place, capable of maintaining the facility and preserving the scientific expertise concentrated at Protvino.

**Human Capital and Diaspora:** The scientists and engineers associated with the UNK did not disappear when the project was suspended. Many dispersed to international collaborations. The Institute for High Energy Physics has maintained participation in major CERN experiments - ALICE, ATLAS, CMS, and LHCb - manufacturing detector components and providing expert personnel. [[5]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Protvino) Fermilab in the United States conducted a scientific collaboration with Soviet and Russian institutions for more than four decades (1972–2017), involving approximately 14 Soviet and Russian institutions on experiments such as CDF and DZero. [[35]](https://news.fnal.gov/2016/04/fermilab-russia-four-decades-scientific-collaboration/) The knowledge that had been embedded in the UNK project - metallurgy, cryogenics, magnet design, accelerator physics - did not vanish. It was preserved, in diaspora, in the work of individuals who had contributed to the Soviet machine.

## Current Status and Revival: Assessment and Restored Ambition (September 2026)

As of September 2026, the UNK is not permanently abandoned. It is unfinished, but under active assessment.

In February 2025, Physics World reported that the Kurchatov Institute had allocated 176 million rubles - approximately $25 million - to evaluate the current condition of the "unfinished 600 GeV Proton Accelerator and Storage Complex UNK." [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) This allocation was not merely a maintenance budget; it represented a deliberate decision to comprehensively assess the facility's state with a view toward potential revival.

Mikhail Kovalchuk, the president of the Kurchatov Institute, provided context for this decision. According to Physics World, Kovalchuk stated that "enormous intellectual and material resources" had been invested in UNK design before its suspension, and that "Western sanctions" - the comprehensive restrictions imposed on Russian scientific participation in international projects following Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine - had provided "additional impetus to restore the project and maintain Russian scientific independence." [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) In December 2023, CERN suspended all cooperation with Russia and Belarus. [[19]](https://www.space.com/cern-end-cooperation-with-russia-2024) [[20]](https://amp.interestingengineering.com/cern-halts-russian-lhc-collaboration) [[21]](https://www.thegenevaobserver.com/at-cern-when-politics-collides-with-science-international-scientific-collaboration-survived-the-cold-war-and-other-conflicts-russias-war-on-ukraine-might-have-killed-it/) [[22]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cern-suspends-collaborations-with-russia/) This rupture meant that Russian scientists, who had contributed approximately 8 percent of CERN's staffing and an estimated ten percent of the physics output of the LHC experiments, were effectively barred from continuing work that had been central to Russian particle physics for thirty years. [[20]](https://amp.interestingengineering.com/cern-halts-russian-lhc-collaboration) [[25]](https://www.science.org/content/article/ukrainian-physicists-call-russia-s-ouster-cern)

The assessment announced in 2025 specifically targets a "600 GeV Proton Accelerator" - a design specification that represents a significant departure from the original 3 TeV ambition. This distinction merits careful attention. The revised 600 GeV specification, according to technical sources including a July 2026 arXiv paper on muon collider applications at the Protvino site, would employ conventional (warm) magnets rather than superconducting systems. [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16904) This approach is lower in energy and lower in cost compared to the full 3 TeV superconducting design. The original UNK design envisioned a chain of three linked accelerators: the U-70 (70 GeV injector), UNK-1 (a 400–600 GeV conventional magnet ring), and UNK-2 (a 3 TeV superconducting ring). [[14]](https://grokipedia.com/page/unk_proton_accelerator) [[11]](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017APS..APR.S2008K/abstract) The revised approach would focus on the UNK-1 stage - the 600 GeV warm accelerator - as a proof-of-concept and potentially fundable initial phase. A future upgrade pathway to full 3 TeV operation with superconducting magnets remains theoretically possible, contingent on technology access and funding availability.

The proposed physics case for the 600 GeV machine, as outlined in the Physics World 2025 report, involves the production of an intense neutrino beam. The concept calls for proton-on-target interactions to generate pions and kaons, which decay into neutrinos that would be directed toward the Baikal Deep Underwater Neutrino Telescope (Baikal-GVD) - a neutrino observatory located 3.5 kilometers offshore in Lake Baikal, at a depth of 1,100 to 1,300 meters. [[30]](https://www.inr.ru/eng/ebgnt.html) The distance between Protvino and Baikal is approximately 4,000 kilometers, positioning the neutrino beam for a long-baseline oscillation experiment. [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) The Baikal-GVD detector, which was officially launched in March 2021 and has been expanded incrementally, currently operates 14 detector clusters containing 4,212 optical modules sensitive to Cherenkov radiation from high-energy neutrinos. [[29]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Baikal_Deep_Underwater_Neutrino_Telescope) [[32]](https://www.appec.org/news/baikal-gvd-the-deep-underwater-neutrino-telescope-in-lake-baikal-has-been-officially-launched-and-keeps-growing/) In April 2025, the Baikal telescope detected galactic neutrinos of approximately 200 TeV energy, demonstrating its sensitivity to events within the energy range that a Protvino neutrino beam would probe. [[29]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Baikal_Deep_Underwater_Neutrino_Telescope) [[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baikal_Deep_Underwater_Neutrino_Telescope) The science case - testing neutrino oscillation parameters, searching for dark matter candidates (WIMPs and other exotic particles), and exploring sources of high-energy cosmic neutrinos - represents cutting-edge astroparticle physics.

The status of the revival, however, remains uncertain. The 2025 assessment was framed as a condition evaluation, not a commitment to construction. Physics World reported that Kovalchuk indicated "what ideas come forward" would shape the decision process, suggesting that the science case and technological feasibility remain under review. [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) No official timeline for assessment completion has been disclosed. No formal government commitment to fund the completion of even the 600 GeV stage has been announced publicly. The revival remains in the assessment and planning phase as of September 8, 2026. [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16904)

## Lessons for Future Builders

The history of the UNK at Protvino - its ambition, suspension, and potential revival - offers eight distinct lessons for future builders of particle accelerators and other megaproject scientific facilities.

**Geological Stability Does Not Guarantee Hydrological Viability:** The site selection criteria emphasized seismic stability, a correct requirement. The Paleozoic seabed sediment underlying Protvino proved stable against vibration. However, the hydrological environment - the high water table and seasonal variability in groundwater levels - was not adequately incorporated into the cost-benefit analysis. A machine designed for underground operation must account not only for construction-phase challenges, but for the perpetual cost of environmental management. At Protvino, the annual pumping cost ($2.7 million) is fixed regardless of scientific productivity and represents a permanent tax on resources. Future projects must model the cost of indefinite facility preservation before committing to excavation. The cost-of-permanence is not a secondary factor; for underground facilities, it may be the dominant long-term cost. [[1]](https://boda.su/en/posts/id2021-inside-unk-protvino-the-soviet-collider-that-never-ran) [[10]](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna41763260) [[13]](https://www.wired.com/1997/12/science-2/)

**Injector Design Resilience Matters:** The U-70 synchrotron survived the UNK collapse because it was designed as a standalone, productive research facility before becoming a component of a larger system. Its continued operation since 1967 demonstrates that a machine with independent physics value provides institutional resilience. Projects designed solely as accelerator stages within larger chains - with no intrinsic scientific program - become orphaned when larger machines are cancelled. Future designs should ensure that each major subsystem, particularly injectors and injector chains, has its own defined physics program and standalone scientific value. [[2]](https://repository.cern/records/jqhna-bpm60) [[16]](https://cerncourier.com/a/forty-years-of-high-energy-physics-in-protvino/) [[17]](https://cerncourier.com/a/golden-jubilee-in-protvino/)

**Geometric Completion Is Not Operational Readiness:** The UNK tunnel was 94 percent geometrically complete by 1995. The remaining challenges - magnets, RF systems, cryogenic infrastructure, detector apparatus, operational control systems, commissioning and beam tuning - represented the vast majority of the actual complexity and cost. A machine that is 80 percent operationally complete (with magnets installed, subsystems integrated, and commissioning underway) is more valuable than one that is 95 percent tunneled but lacks all functional systems. Future projects must phase completion to deliver operational milestones, not excavation milestones. Progress should be measured in achievable beam energies and successful physics runs, not in tunnel kilometers. [[14]](https://grokipedia.com/page/unk_proton_accelerator) [[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNK_proton_accelerator)

**Maintenance Costs Become Perpetual if Unresolved:** The decision to keep the Protvino tunnel intact, rather than fill and abandon it, created a permanent budget obligation with no scientific return. This decision, while economically rational at the moment (filling would cost roughly $200 million, equivalent to completion), has generated thirty years of $2.7 million annual costs. [[10]](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna41763260) [[13]](https://www.wired.com/1997/12/science-2/) The total cost of preservation now approaches $80 million. Builders must confront the cost-of-permanence explicitly in feasibility analyses. Is preservation or abandonment the lower-cost path? This question must be answered before construction begins, not thirty years later. [[8]](https://www.tunneltalk.com/Russia-20May2021-Abandoned-Protvino-nuclear-physics-facility.php) [[1]](https://boda.su/en/posts/id2021-inside-unk-protvino-the-soviet-collider-that-never-ran)

**Talent Dispersal Following Suspension Is Irreversible:** Scientists and engineers associated with the UNK dispersed to other countries and projects when the machine was suspended. Implicit knowledge - accumulated understanding of design choices, failure modes, and optimization strategies - departed with them. Restarting a suspended megaproject three decades later requires rebuilding not only the machine, but the workforce and the institutional knowledge base. The recovery of dispersed expertise is difficult and costly. [[35]](https://news.fnal.gov/2016/04/fermilab-russia-four-decades-scientific-collaboration/) [[33]](https://grokipedia.com/page/logunov)

**Geopolitical Fracture Cannot Be Engineered Away:** The UNK was conceived as a unilateral Soviet project, a statement of technological prowess. Its reorientation toward LHC participation reflected post-Cold War reintegration. The recent suspension of CERN-Russia cooperation and the renewal of interest in UNK revival reflect another geopolitical fracture. Large accelerators, designed at national or regional scales, are vulnerable to political disruption. Future megaprojects should be structured to enable broad international participation and coalition support, reducing the dependence on any single nation's sustained commitment. The LHC model - a multinational, multilingual, multi-institutional enterprise - demonstrates that dispersed governance, while more complex administratively, provides greater resilience to political rupture. [[18]](https://cern70.cern/switching-on-the-large-hadron-collider/) [[19]](https://www.space.com/cern-end-cooperation-with-russia-2024) [[21]](https://www.thegenevaobserver.com/at-cern-when-politics-collides-with-science-international-scientific-collaboration-survived-the-cold-war-and-other-conflicts-russias-war-on-ukraine-might-have-killed-it/) [[25]](https://www.science.org/content/article/ukrainian-physicists-call-russia-s-ouster-cern)

**Tunnel Reuse as a Salvage Strategy:** A July 2026 arXiv paper by L.V. Dudko and colleagues examining the application of a muon collider at the Protvino site concludes that the existing 21-kilometer tunnel "is precisely the kind of asset a compact lepton collider can exploit." [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16904) While a muon collider would employ different physics principles and detection strategies than the original proton machine, the existence of the preserved tunnel represents a significant salvage value. Rather than greenfield construction of a new 21-kilometer ring - an effort that would require excavation, environmental assessment, and geological oversight - reuse of existing infrastructure reduces costs and schedules. This lesson applies broadly: when large underground infrastructure is suspended, preservation for alternative uses (rather than filling and abandonment) provides optionality. Future projects should design with modularity and technology-agnostic parameters, allowing for alternative physics programs if primary goals are deferred. [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16904)

**Staged, Fundable Milestones Are Essential:** The original UNK was conceived as a single, large project with a binary outcome: completion or failure. The revised 600 GeV → 3 TeV staged pathway reflects a different philosophy: initial proof-of-concept at reduced energy and cost, with option for later upgrade. This approach makes the project fundable in phases and creates interim scientific returns that justify continued investment. Future megaprojects should break into stages aligned with funding cycles, technological maturity, and scientific deliverables. A collider that can produce world-class physics at 600 GeV, with a documented pathway to 3 TeV, is more rational than betting the entire project on a single jump to full energy. [[3]](https://physicsworld.com/a/russia-plans-to-revive-abandoned-soviet-era-particle-accelerator/) [[14]](https://grokipedia.com/page/unk_proton_accelerator) [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16904)

## Conclusion: An Unfinished Asset Under New Scrutiny

The 21-kilometer tunnel beneath Protvino remains. It is neither a monument to failed ambition nor a museum artifact. As of September 2026, it is a physical asset under reassessment by the Russian scientific establishment, with assessment funded and underway, its future contingent on technology access, geopolitical stability, and scientific justification. The tunnel will not fill itself. The costs of preservation will persist. The question of completion - whether at 600 GeV, 3 TeV, or in some other configuration - remains open.

For builders of future accelerators and megaprojects, Protvino offers concrete lessons grounded in three decades of real experience: plan for permanence, design for resilience, phase for funding, and account for the geopolitical contexts that shape even the most ambitious scientific endeavors.
