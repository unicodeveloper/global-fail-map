# Mineral Beach

## The Crisis: Sinkholes Emerge

Beginning in the 1980s but accelerating dramatically in the 2000s and 2010s, the Dead Sea region experienced an unprecedented sinkhole crisis. Approximately **5,000 sinkholes** have formed since the 1980s [[3]](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/sinkholes-threaten-israels-dead-sea-tourist-trade-n392461), with an average of **400 new sinkholes appearing annually** in recent years. These depressions reach diameters of up to **600 meters** and depths exceeding 30 meters [[3]](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/sinkholes-threaten-israels-dead-sea-tourist-trade-n392461).

The mechanism is straightforward: as the Dead Sea's water level declines approximately **1 meter per year**, the water table in surrounding rock strata drops, causing undersaturated groundwater to dissolve subsurface salt deposits. The resulting cavities collapse unpredictably, creating sinkholes overnight. Freshwater influx from springs and groundwater into the highly saline Dead Sea environment accelerates the chemical dissolution of salt layers [[3]](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/sinkholes-threaten-israels-dead-sea-tourist-trade-n392461).

## Mineral Beach Resort: Closure in June 2015

**Mineral Beach Resort** at **Mitzpe Shalem** on the western (Israeli) Dead Sea shore was a popular thermal spa and bathing facility. In **June 2015**, a sinkhole **materialized overnight**, engulfing the resort's parking lot and destroying treatment and bath facilities [[1]](https://weather.com/travel/news/dead-sea-sinkholes). The unpredictable and sudden nature of the collapse rendered the site unsafe for continued operation.

**Status**: Abandoned (June 2015 onward).

**What was lost economically**: Mineral Beach Resort had attracted approximately **250,000 visitors annually** before closure [[2]](https://www.timesofisrael.com/dead-sea-facing-ecological-disaster-but-regional-tensions-makes-solutions-difficult/). The resort represented a primary economic engine for the surrounding Kibbutz Ein Gedi community. The kibbutz reported cumulative sinkhole-related economic losses exceeding **\$25 million since 1995** [[3]](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/sinkholes-threaten-israels-dead-sea-tourist-trade-n392461).

## Ein Gedi Beach: Closure 2015–2016

**Ein Gedi Beach**, located near the Ein Gedi Nature Reserve on the western shore, was a public bathing facility. It was closed to public access in **2015–2016** due to the frequency and unpredictability of sinkhole formation. The infrastructure—lifeguard stations, walkways, umbrellas, and facilities—remained standing but deteriorated and abandoned [[4]](https://www.jpost.com/jerusalem-report/article-884469).

**Status**: Abandoned (as of 2015–2016, continuing through January 2026).

## Recent Documentation: Tourism Repurposing

As of **January 2026**, both facilities remain closed to their original recreational purpose. However, the abandoned infrastructure has been partially repurposed by tour operators and environmental organizations as an **educational and disaster-tourism venue**. Visitors now tour the sites specifically to observe the environmental catastrophe—abandoned lifeguard stations, rusted umbrellas, crumbling facilities, and visible sinkhole scars. Ein Gedi's situation has transformed from resort destination to cautionary spectacle, representing one of the planet's most dramatic examples of environmental infrastructure collapse [[2]](https://www.timesofisrael.com/dead-sea-facing-ecological-disaster-but-regional-tensions-makes-solutions-difficult/).

## The Eastern Shore: Arab Potash Company Losses

The phenomenon is not limited to Israeli-side tourism. On the eastern shore, the **Arab Potash Company** (operating the Jordanian mineral extraction facility) suffered a **major sinkhole incident in 2000** that caused approximately **\$38 million in direct losses**. Additional predicted future sinkhole-related costs for the Arab Potash Company were estimated at **\$70–90 million** [[3]](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/world/sinkholes-threaten-israels-dead-sea-tourist-trade-n392461).

## What Survived

Physical infrastructure survives in deteriorated form but serves no original purpose. The sites themselves have become environmental testimony to the Dead Sea's decline and now function as educational venues documenting the region's ecological crisis.
