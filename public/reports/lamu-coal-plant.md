# The Lamu Coal Plant

## Origins and Licensing (2016)

The most consequential infrastructure conflict in Lamu's recent history centers on a **1,050 megawatt (MW) coal-fired power plant proposed by Amu Power Company Ltd.** to be constructed in **Kwasaki, Lamu County, approximately 20 kilometers from Lamu Town**. The project represented one of East Africa's largest single coal investments, with an estimated capital cost of **\$2 billion**.[[47]](https://ke.boell.org/en/2019/06/28/tribunal-halts-construction-amu-coal-power-plant-kenya)

The National Environment Management Authority (NEMA) issued an environmental license to Amu Power in September 2016 after completing what NEMA designated as an Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA). At the time, the project appeared to follow standard regulatory channels, with no requirement for independent environmental verification or enhanced community consultation protocols. The plant's footprint would encompass agricultural and pastoral lands in Kwasaki, displacing rural farming communities and triggering acquisition of land held under customary tenure systems.[[51]](https://archive.crossborderinformation.com/Article/Kenya+Court+blocks+Lamu+coal+project.aspx?date=20190711)

## Community Resistance and Litigation (2016–2019)

Opposition materialized quickly from coalition organizations including **Save Lamu**, **Natural Justice**, **Environmental Law Alliance Worldwide**, and the **Katiba Institute**. These groups, with support from international climate advocacy networks, challenged both NEMA's licensing decision and the quality of the underlying environmental assessment. The litigation strategy centered on three substantive grounds: (1) procedural violation—lack of meaningful public participation in the EIA process; (2) environmental inadequacy—insufficient assessment of climate, air quality, water, and ecosystem impacts; and (3) constitutional violation—failure to respect rights to information, participation, and a clean environment as guaranteed under Kenya's 2010 Constitution.[[49]](https://climatecasechart.com/non-us-case/save-lamu-et-al-v-national-environmental-management-authority-and-amu-power-co-ltd/)

The case moved through Kenya's judicial system with deliberate pace. Between 2016 and 2019, the coal plant remained in a licensing limbo—not yet under construction, but formally authorized and legally defended by the government and project proponent.

## Tribunal Ruling and License Revocation (June 26, 2019)

Kenya's National Environmental Tribunal (NET) handed down judgment on **June 26, 2019**, finding in favor of the community challengers. The tribunal's decision **set aside (cancelled) the environmental license issued by NEMA**. The judgment established findings of fact that reverberated through Kenya's environmental law: the EIA had been inadequate in scope, community consultation had been perfunctory and non-responsive, and NEMA's licensing decision lacked sufficient evidentiary foundation for such a consequential project.[[44]](https://naturaljustice.org/great-day-for-lamu-as-tribunal-dismisses-coal-plant-license/)

Critically, the tribunal did not rest its decision on scientific evidence of harm or even climate policy disagreement. Instead, the ruling centered on **procedural and constitutional failures**—NEMA and Amu Power had violated the mandatory steps and rights-respecting processes that Kenyan law required. The tribunal's language was direct: the license issuance was described as "contemptuous" of proper environmental governance.[[50]](https://africanlii.org/articles/2019-07-04/carmel-rickard/contemptuous-report-on-planned-major-coal-power-plant-dismissed-by-kenyas-environmental-tribunal)

## Financing Withdrawal and De Facto Abandonment (2019–2025)

Following the tribunal ruling, the project's trajectory shifted decisively. **General Electric, positioned as the potential major equipment supplier and technical partner, withdrew its involvement** (timing of withdrawal not precisely specified in available sources, but occurring in the 2019–2020 period). Without GE's participation, alternative financing and technical partnerships did not materialize.

The Industrial and Commercial Bank of China (ICBC), previously a potential lending partner for infrastructure financing, **announced its withdrawal of financing plans in 2020**, citing environmental and reputational concerns. These withdrawals reflected broader shifts in international financial institutions' climate commitments and ESG (environmental, social, governance) standards applied to coal-powered projects in Africa.[[36]](https://thepeoplesmap.net/project/lamu-coal-power-plant/)

As of August 2025, **no construction had commenced on the Kwasaki site**. The facility remains unconstruct despite the licensing authority's theoretical continuation post-2019. The project entered a state of legal suspension, neither formally cancelled by the government nor actively revived. A secondary legal proceeding—the Land and Environment Court's handling of broader LAPSSET-related land claims—was expected to deliver a relevant ruling in September 2025, but that decision had not been finalized as of the research cutoff.[[34]](https://www.theelephant.info/analysis/2025/04/01/the-battle-for-lamu-how-a-210m-tall-coal-plant-threatens-kenyas-future/)

## Unresolved Issues for Affected Populations

Residents of Kwasaki who had their land acquired (often under colonial-era compulsory acquisition powers without modern constitutional safeguards) face prolonged uncertainty. Approximately **2,000 to 3,000 small farmers** had their cultivation rights suspended pending construction, which was presented as imminent when land was taken in 2016. A decade later, with the project effectively abandoned but never formally cancelled, these communities remain without restoration of use rights, alternative livelihood support, or meaningful compensation.

The project's suspension also left in place the institutional apparatus of land acquisition—survey boundaries, ownership transfers in government records, restrictions on alternative land use—without the corresponding development benefits that the government had promised would justify the displacement.
