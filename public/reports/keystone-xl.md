# Keystone XL: A Pipeline Project's Thirteen-Year Arc from Ambitious Vision to Political Termination

## Executive Summary

The Keystone XL pipeline project represents one of the most contested infrastructure proposals of the early 21st century. Proposed by TC Energy (formerly TransCanada Corporation) and ConocoPhillips in July 2008, the project envisioned carrying 830,000 barrels per day of Alberta oil sands crude along a route to Steele City, Nebraska, with onward connections to Gulf Coast refineries. TC Energy's 2020 plan specified 1,947 kilometres for the Hardisty-Steele City project. Over thirteen years, the project accumulated regulatory approvals and legal victories alongside sustained opposition from Indigenous nations, environmental groups, and state officials. It survived two presidential rejections under Obama, was revived by Trump with expedited permits, began construction in 2020, and then collapsed within months of Biden's January 20, 2021 permit revocation. The termination was announced definitively on June 9, 2021. This investigation examines the technical ambition behind the project, the consequential sequence of decisions that advanced or blocked it, the evidence for why it ultimately could not survive a shift in political will, and the physical and financial legacies that persisted.

## The Original Ambition: Project Scope and Economic Vision

TransCanada Corporation, a Calgary-based pipeline operator, and ConocoPhillips announced the Keystone XL project in July 2008 as a \$7 billion expansion to the existing Keystone Pipeline System. [[1]](https://macleans.ca/economy/economicanalysis/keystone-xl-a-timeline/) The proposal was straightforward in its geopolitical logic: establish a dedicated corridor from Canada's largest oil-producing region directly to US refining capacity, bypassing bottlenecks and creating a continental energy system less dependent on global crude markets or water transport via tanker.

The pipeline would originate in Hardisty, Alberta - the hub where crude from the Western Canadian Sedimentary Basin (including oil sands operations) was already being aggregated - and terminate in Steele City, Nebraska, where it would connect to existing infrastructure serving US Gulf Coast refineries. [[2]](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/keystone-xl-pipeline) The total length was 875 miles within US territory, with the Canadian proposal comprising 529 kilometres from Hardisty, Alberta, to Monchy, Saskatchewan, plus pump stations and associated facilities. [[3]](https://apps.cer-rec.gc.ca/PPS/en/pipeline-profiles/keystone) The project involved not just the main pipeline but supporting infrastructure: pump stations, three operational storage tanks of 55,600 cubic metres each, and integration points with the existing Keystone system phases. [[4]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1163165/000136231009002769/0001362310-09-002769.txt)

The design specifications reflected the project's purpose. The pipeline would feature a 36-inch diameter pipe - a standard size for crude transport but significant in its ability to move volume continuously. [[5]](https://www.fraserinstitute.org/commentary/resurrected-keystone-pipeline-would-be-great-news-alberta-and-all-canada) The nameplate capacity was 830,000 barrels per day, which, if fully utilized, would deliver approximately 35 million gallons of crude daily to US refineries. [[6]](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/06/09/tc-energy-terminates-keystone-xl-pipeline-project.html) This was a substantial proposed transport capacity, but it was not a measure of additional U.S. oil consumption and should not be presented as 1% of U.S. crude demand.

Economic projections were central to advocacy for the project, but construction employment and permanent operating jobs are different measures. The State Department estimated approximately 42,100 direct, indirect and induced jobs supported during construction, using annual job-equivalents, and approximately 50 jobs during operations. It also estimated a US$3.4 billion contribution to U.S. GDP during construction. These estimates do not support the claim that Cornell projected 2,500 permanent operational jobs [[7]](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43787.html).

Cost estimates changed with scope, timing and currency. The revised U.S. portion was estimated at US$5.3 billion in 2012, rising in subsequent reporting to approximately US$8 billion. Original whole-project estimates, later northern-section budgets and Canadian-dollar figures are not directly comparable, so they should not be combined into a simple doubling calculation [[7]](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43787.html). In March 2020, TC Energy described a US$8 billion construction investment, financed by Alberta's US$1.1 billion equity contribution, a US$4.2 billion provincially guaranteed credit facility and US$2.7 billion from TC Energy [[8]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2020/2020-03-31tc-energy-to-build-keystone-xl-pipeline/).

## Technical and Commercial Design: The Oil and the Route

Keystone XL was designed to carry Canadian oil-sands crude as well as oil from the U.S. Bakken region. Diluted bitumen, one important intended cargo, is heavy oil blended with lighter diluents so it can flow through a pipeline; the proposal was not exclusively for that crude type [[7]](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43787.html).

State Department environmental assessments were explicit about the distinction. The Final Environmental Impact Statement released January 31, 2014, assessed the lifecycle emissions of oil-sands crude, including extraction, upgrading, refining and use. The phrase "one of the most polluting kinds of oil" should not be treated as a verified quotation from that assessment. [[9]](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/economy/state-to-release-keystones-final-environmental-impact-statement-friday/2014/01/31/3a9bb25c-8a83-11e3-a5bd-844629433ba3_story.html) The full lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions from Canadian tar sands crude were estimated to produce approximately 17 percent more greenhouse gas emissions on a lifecycle basis than the average crude refined in US refineries. [[10]](https://foreffectivegov.org/state-department-report-acknowledges-climate-change-impacts-keystone-xl)

The chosen route, however, proved far more contentious than the commodity itself. The original proposed pathway ran through Nebraska's Sandhills region - a unique landscape of grass-stabilized sand dunes covering 20,000 square miles, with shallow groundwater and highly porous soils. [[11]](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/sciencebites/chapter/ogallala-aquifer-nebraskan-sandhills-potentially-threatened-by-the-keystone-xl-pipeline/) The Sandhills sit atop the Ogallala Aquifer, part of the larger High Plains Aquifer system that covers portions of eight states, supplies drinking water to 82 percent of people within its boundary, and provides 30 percent of the nation's groundwater for irrigation purposes. [[11]](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/sciencebites/chapter/ogallala-aquifer-nebraskan-sandhills-potentially-threatened-by-the-keystone-xl-pipeline/) The aquifer is the lifeblood of agricultural production across Nebraska and neighboring states, with the Sandhills region itself representing one of the largest continuous grassland ecosystems remaining in North America.

In June 2011, University of Nebraska professors sent a formal letter to Secretary of State Hillary Clinton warning that the Sandhills region was "particularly vulnerable to crude oil pollution from pipeline spill." [[12]](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/15062011/nebraska-scientists-keystone-xl-pipeline-risk-sandhills-water-supply-ogallala-transcanada-hillary-epa/) Hydrogeologist Jim Goeke, a professor emeritus at the University of Nebraska-Lincoln, warned that an oil spill in the region would contaminate local groundwater as readily as rainwater penetrating porous soil. [[13]](https://www.reuters.com/article/idUS339164205320120426/) Shallow groundwater and permeable soils raised location-specific contamination concerns. That is distinct from the likelihood of a pipeline rupture: the National Research Council's 2013 assessment found no evidence that diluted bitumen was more likely than other crude oils to cause releases [[7]](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43787.html).

University of Nebraska hydrochemist Roy Spalding and graduate student Aaron Hirsh proposed an alternative in December 2012: the Risk-Managed Route (RMR), which would run southeast through Holt, Antelope, and Pierce counties. [[14]](https://news.unl.edu/newsrooms/unltoday/article/unl-scientists-cites-keystone-pipeline-risks-minimal-manageable) Rather than crossing pristine Sandhills, the RMR would route the pipeline through pre-contaminated groundwater areas (where nitrate and sulfate from fertilizer application over 100+ miles had already compromised water quality). Spalding's prior research suggested that existing center-pivot irrigation systems in these agricultural areas could potentially remediate petroleum contamination through natural volatilization processes - stripping volatile compounds like benzene from contaminated water as it was sprayed into the air. [[14]](https://news.unl.edu/newsrooms/unltoday/article/unl-scientists-cites-keystone-pipeline-risks-minimal-manageable) The route remained roughly the same length but avoided the most sensitive ecological and water resource areas.

TransCanada ultimately studied 14 different route alternatives, with 8 affecting Nebraska alone. [[7]](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43787.html) In January 2013, Nebraska Governor Dave Heineman approved a revised Nebraska route. This should not be conflated with the Mainline Alternative Route considered in the later Public Service Commission process. [[7]](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43787.html) Heineman had previously opposed the original Sandhills route; Hillary Clinton was secretary of state, not president, during that review. However, environmental organizations noted that the revised route, while improved, still crossed high water tables and areas above the Ogallala Aquifer where vulnerability to spill impacts remained significant. [[15]](https://blog.nwf.org/2012/09/nwf-says-new-keystone-xl-map-revisits-old-problems/)

## The Organizations and People: Who Built and Opposed the Project

The Keystone XL project was not a singular vision but rather the product of evolving corporate strategy, changing regulatory environments, and shifting political coalitions across thirteen years and multiple administrations.

**TC Energy Corporation** was the project's primary owner and developer. Originally incorporated as TransCanada Corporation in 1951, the company rebranded to TC Energy in May 2019. [[2]](https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/keystone-xl-pipeline) In June 2009, just one year after the project's announcement, ConocoPhillips divested its stake, making TC Energy the sole owner. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) The transaction made TransCanada the sole owner. The sale alone does not establish ConocoPhillips' assessment of Keystone XL's eventual political risk.

The most visible leadership during the critical 2010-2021 period was **Russ Girling**, who became President and CEO of TC Energy on July 1, 2010 - after the initial concept and proposal but inheriting the regulatory battle. [[17]](https://globalnews.ca/news/7348070/tc-energy-russ-girling-retires/) Girling led the company through its 2019 rebranding, overseeing what was characterized as "unprecedented growth" and significant stock price appreciation. [[18]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/calgary/girling-russ-retire-tc-energy-calgary-ceo-transcanada-1.5732270) However, by September 2020, Girling announced his retirement effective December 31, 2020. [[18]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/calgary/girling-russ-retire-tc-energy-calgary-ceo-transcanada-1.5732270) He did not lead the company through the final rejection. **François Poirier**, TC Energy's Chief Operating Officer, succeeded Girling as President and CEO on January 1, 2021 - inheriting the project in its final weeks of life. [[19]](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/business/article-tc-energys-russ-girling-retires-with-keystone-unfinished-can-his/)

On the Canadian government side, **Premier Jason Kenney** of Alberta became the most significant political actor. When Kenney took office as Premier in 2019, he made pipeline development a centerpiece of his economic agenda. On March 31, 2020, Kenney's government announced a historic \$1.5 billion Canadian dollar equity investment in the Keystone XL project, coupled with a \$6 billion loan guarantee. [[20]](https://calgaryherald.com/business/varcoe-keystone-xl-to-proceed-with-7b-provincial-commitment) This was no ordinary government support - Kenney explicitly stated that "without this investment, Keystone XL would not be built." [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) The statement demonstrated that Alberta's backing was material to the decision to proceed. It does not establish that all private financing was unavailable on every set of terms. By March 2020, the oil market was in turmoil from the COVID-19 pandemic and the Saudi-Russia price war, but more fundamentally, investors globally were becoming wary of long-cycle fossil fuel infrastructure with uncertain regulatory futures.

**Opposition organizations** crystallized around several institutional actors. The Indigenous Environmental Network (established 1990) served as a coordinating hub for Indigenous resistance. [[21]](https://studentorgs.kentlaw.iit.edu/ckjeel/2021/09/30/not-just-keystone-xl-the-indigenous-fight-for-environmental-justice/) The Sierra Club, Center for Biological Diversity, Natural Resources Defense Council (NRDC), Friends of the Earth, and Bold Alliance mobilized constituencies around climate and water protection. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) [[22]](https://www.npr.org/2021/06/10/1005019784/keystone-xl-pipeline-developer-cancels-project-ending-decade-long-battle) The Native American Rights Fund (NARF) represented tribal litigants, including the **Rosebud Sioux Tribe** and **Fort Belknap Indian Community**, whose lawsuits invoked protections under the Fort Laramie Treaties of 1851 and 1868. [[23]](https://narf.org/cases/keystone/)

## The Sequence of Consequential Decisions: A Thirteen-Year Political and Legal Battle

The project's history can be divided into eight distinct phases, each marked by pivotal decisions that either advanced or arrested progress.

### Phase One: Canadian Approval (2009-2010)

TC Energy filed its application with Canada's National Energy Board on February 27, 2009. [[24]](https://www.cer-rec.gc.ca/en/applications-hearings/view-applications-projects/keystone-xl/project-background.html?wbdisable=true) The NEB conducted a comprehensive environmental and engineering review. On March 11, 2010, the Board approved the project, attaching 22 conditions related to safety, environmental protection, and landowner rights. [[25]](https://apps.cer-rec.gc.ca/REGDOCS/Item/View/614188) On April 27, 2010, the NEB issued the Certificate of Public Convenience and Necessity (OC-56), formally authorizing the pipeline's construction in Canada. [[25]](https://apps.cer-rec.gc.ca/REGDOCS/Item/View/614188) This Canadian approval would remain valid throughout the project's history, providing a foundation for the revival efforts that emerged after 2024.

### Phase Two: First US Rejection Under Obama (2010-2012)

The State Department's lead role in the US approval process introduced a layer of political discretion absent in Canada. In April 2010, the State Department released a draft Environmental Impact Statement concluding that the pipeline would have limited environmental effect. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) However, by June-July 2010, opposition mounted from the EPA, scientists, and legislators who questioned the project's necessity and climate implications. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/)

Congress inserted a constraint on the process: in December 2011, Congress imposed a 60-day decision mandate on the Obama administration. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) This procedural pressure forced a decision before substantive environmental review could be completed. On January 18, 2012, the Obama administration rejected the permit, citing insufficient time to review TransCanada's revised Nebraska route proposal. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) The rejection was not absolute - TransCanada was explicitly permitted to reapply with an improved route design.

### Phase Three: Strategic Segmentation and Southern Leg Approval (2012-2014)

TransCanada responded strategically. Recognizing that the northern leg's presidential permit was vulnerable, the company decoupled the project. On February 27, 2012, TransCanada announced that the southern leg of the system - from Cushing, Oklahoma, to the Gulf Coast refineries in Texas - would be built as a separate project requiring no presidential permit (as both endpoints were within US territory). [[1]](https://macleans.ca/economy/economicanalysis/keystone-xl-a-timeline/) This southern segment, which cost \$2.3 billion, was completed in 2013 and placed into service in January 2014. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) Its operation demonstrated that a domestic segment could proceed separately; it did not by itself establish the profitability of the unbuilt northern extension.

### Phase Four: Nebraska Route Battles and Environmental Review (2012-2015)

After submitting the revised Nebraska route on April 18, 2012, and reapplying to the State Department on May 4, 2012, TransCanada faced a prolonged review process. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) Nebraska Governor Dave Heineman approved the revised route on January 22, 2013. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) However, a Nebraska lower court ruled in February 2014 that the state law allowing gubernatorial route approval was unconstitutional. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) That decision was reversed on January 9, 2015, when the Nebraska Supreme Court vacated the lower-court judgment. Four justices would have found the law unconstitutional, but Nebraska required five votes to invalidate it; this was not a four-justice majority endorsing its constitutionality. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/)

Meanwhile, the State Department conducted successive draft and final environmental impact reviews. The Draft EIS released March 1, 2013, found that incremental greenhouse gas emissions would range from 0.07 to 5.3 MMTCO2e annually, representing 0.001% to 0.08% of US total emissions (based on 2010 baseline of 6,822 MMTCO2e). [[26]](https://www.energy.gov/nepa/doeeis-0433-keystone-xl-pipeline) The Final EIS released January 31, 2014, provided more comprehensive lifecycle analysis: construction emissions of 0.24 MMTCO2e, annual operational emissions of 1.44 MMTCO2e per year, and net incremental lifecycle emissions of 1.3 to 27.4 MMTCO2e annually when accounting for displaced crude sources. [[27]](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43415.html)

These State Department findings were internally contested. Secretary of State John Kerry's analysis flagged the lifecycle climate impacts as significant. The analysis noted that tar sands crude produces 17 percent more greenhouse gas on a lifecycle basis than average crude refined in the US. [[10]](https://foreffectivegov.org/state-department-report-acknowledges-climate-change-impacts-keystone-xl) However, the State Department's market analysis concluded that "approval or denial of any one crude oil transport project is unlikely to significantly impact the rate of extraction in the oil sands or the continued demand for heavy crude oil at refineries." [[28]](https://www.npr.org/2014/01/31/269529696/state-dept-delivers-unwelcome-news-for-keystone-opponents) This finding became central to the permitting logic: even if Keystone XL were denied, other pipelines might transport the same crude via different routes.

Competing academic analyses emerged. A Stockholm Environment Institute working paper (2013) found that additional oil supply enabled by Keystone XL could lower global prices and increase oil consumption by as much as 510,000 barrels per day (equivalent to 62% of KXL's capacity), potentially adding up to 93 million metric tons CO2e annually - approximately 4-5 times greater than State Department estimates. [[29]](https://mediamanager.sei.org/documents/Publications/Climate/SEI-WP-2013-11-KeystoneXL-price-effects.pdf) A peer-reviewed study in Nature Climate Change (August 2014) found the pipeline could have climate impacts up to 110 MMTCO2e annually, four times the State Department's upper estimate. [[30]](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/keystone-xl-would-increase-greenhouse-gas-pollution/)

### Phase Five: Congressional Pressure and Obama's Second Rejection (2014-2015)

With Republican control of Congress following the 2014 midterms, the project became a legislative battleground. In November 2014, Republicans introduced a bill to approve the permit, which failed in the Senate. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) Undeterred, in January 2015, the Senate passed the Keystone XL Pipeline Approval Act with 62 votes (bipartisan support), followed by House approval on February 11 with 270 votes. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) On February 24, 2015, President Obama vetoed the legislation. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) The Senate's override attempt on March 4, 2015 fell short at 62-37, short of the required two-thirds supermajority. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/)

Obama's position hardened. On November 6, 2015, the Obama administration formally rejected the application, concluding that the project "did not serve the national interest" of the United States. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) This rejected the pending application; it did not legally preclude a later application under another administration. The decision reflected a calculus that the climate implications and water risks outweighed the economic benefits, particularly as alternative crude sources were becoming available and US oil production was rising due to shale development.

### Phase Six: Trump Era Reversal and Acceleration (2017-2020)

The 2016 election transformed the project's fortunes. On January 24, 2017, President Donald Trump signed a presidential memorandum inviting a renewed application and expediting its review. [[31]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/keystone-xl-pipeline-timeline-1.3950156) On March 23, 2017, the State Department issued a new Record of Decision approving the project, and the State Department issued the presidential permit under delegated authority. [[31]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/politics/keystone-xl-pipeline-timeline-1.3950156) The permit granted cross-border authority to construct and operate the pipeline.

However, the legal battles persisted. On November 8, 2018, Montana District Court Judge Brian Morris vacated Trump's 2017 Record of Decision, finding it violated the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA). [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) Morris identified five categories of NEPA violations: (1) disregarding John Kerry's climate analysis from the Obama administration; (2) inadequate cultural resource surveys affecting Native American sacred sites; (3) insufficient oil spill modeling; (4) no cumulative greenhouse gas analysis; (5) failure to analyze whether current oil prices supported project viability. [[32]](https://biologicaldiversity.org/w/news/press-releases/keystone-xl-faces-new-legal-challenge-over-faulty-federal-review-2020-07-14/)

The subsequent permit history shows the project's political vulnerability. Trump issued a new presidential permit in March 2019 after the 2018 ruling, and Biden revoked that permit in January 2021. A later judicial observation about a future president issuing another permit must not be attributed to a 2018 ruling as if it anticipated the specific Biden revocation [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/).

Despite the injunction, TC Energy began construction in northern Montana on April 5, 2020. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) The company's aggressive schedule reflected awareness that political circumstances could shift. However, the company faced a new legal obstacle: on April 15, 2020, a federal district court vacated the Army Corps of Engineers' Nationwide Permit 12 in litigation over its Endangered Species Act consultation, affecting authorization for water crossings. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) On July 6, 2020, the Supreme Court stayed the district court's order except as it applied to Keystone XL. The Court did not leave the restriction in force for all new oil and gas pipelines [[33]](https://www.supremecourt.gov/orders/courtorders/070620zr_2d83.pdf). [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/)

It was at this moment of regulatory and legal uncertainty that Alberta's government intervened. On March 31, 2020, Premier Kenney announced the \$1.5 billion equity investment and \$6 billion loan guarantee. [[20]](https://calgaryherald.com/business/varcoe-keystone-xl-to-proceed-with-7b-provincial-commitment) Kenney's statement - that the pipeline "would not be built" without this public capital - indicated that provincial support was central to the immediate construction decision, not proof that operating the completed pipeline could never be commercially viable. The global oil market was collapsing due to COVID-19 and geopolitical tensions, and the energy sector was in severe contraction. The cited announcement does not establish the terms or outcomes of every potential private investment discussion.

### Phase Seven: Biden's Permit Revocation and Project Termination (2021)

On January 20, 2021, President Joe Biden signed Executive Order 13990, revoking the permit for the Keystone XL pipeline. [[6]](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/06/09/tc-energy-terminates-keystone-xl-pipeline-project.html) [[34]](https://www.axios.com/biden-keystone-pipeline-9ffcedfb-42c1-4778-8183-27d858f0c966.html) The order stated: "The Permit is hereby revoked. Leaving the Keystone XL pipeline permit in place would not be consistent with my Administration's economic and climate imperatives." [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) Biden had campaigned explicitly on canceling Keystone XL, and the permit revocation was one of his first acts in office.

TC Energy suspended construction after the permit revocation and reviewed its options. On June 9, 2021, 140 days later, it announced termination after consultation with the Government of Alberta. Its statement committed to regulatory and environmental obligations during project exit and thanked a broad range of supporters. It did not contain the report's asserted quotations about extensive consultations or being "no longer viable" [[35]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2021/2021-06-09-tc-energy-confirms-termination-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project/).

The financial impact was substantial. TC Energy recorded an impairment charge of Canadian \$2.2 billion (\$1.81 billion USD). [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) TC Energy announced the elimination of more than 1,000 jobs. [[22]](https://www.npr.org/2021/06/10/1005019784/keystone-xl-pipeline-developer-cancels-project-ending-decade-long-battle) The company had already incurred substantial costs on partially completed Canadian sections - approximately 150 kilometers of pipeline had been laid during the 2020 construction phase before work halted. [[36]](https://constructionreviewonline.com/michels-canada-selected-to-construct-260km-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project-in-canada/)

Alberta's financial position was even more dire. The province formally recognized a loss of approximately C\$1.3 billion from its equity investment in the project. [[37]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/calgary/keystone-xl-termination-1.6059683) Finance Minister Travis Toews initially suggested the final bill would not be "materially" above \$1.3 billion, but the total loss - including opportunity costs and economic development forgone - likely exceeded this figure. [[38]](https://globalnews.ca/news/7686121/alberta-keystone-xl-investment-cost-to-taxpayers/) In defending the investment, Toews characterized it as a "calculated decision" made under conditions of extreme market uncertainty, amid severe oil-market disruption and uncertainty over this project's permitting. [[39]](https://globalnews.ca/news/7939964/alberta-finance-minister-keystone-xl-loss/)

## Why It Ended: The Convergence of Political, Legal, and Financial Pressures

The project's termination resulted from a convergence of distinct but reinforcing factors, with the January 2021 permit revocation the immediate trigger for suspending construction and the later termination decision.

**Presidential Permit Risk.** The cross-border permit was vulnerable to changes of administration. The successive 2017 approval, 2019 replacement and 2021 revocation show that risk in practice. It is inaccurate to describe all legislation as requiring a congressional supermajority to repeal or to treat one district-court observation as establishing an immutable rule for every future infrastructure permit [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/).

**Accumulated Legal and Regulatory Burden.** The project faced a series of court decisions, each requiring supplemental environmental review or new permits. The 2018 Montana District Court ruling vacating the 2017 permit; the 2020 Supreme Court decision upholding the stay of the Army Corps water crossing permit; the repeated requirements for supplemental environmental impact statements - each decision imposed delay and additional cost. The burden of proving compliance with NEPA, Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act, and cultural protection statutes (particularly Fort Laramie Treaty obligations) meant that legal defense consumed resources and calendar time indefinitely.

**Financing and Government Support.** Alberta's 2020 equity investment and loan guarantee enabled the construction decision during exceptionally difficult oil-market conditions. This demonstrated the importance of public risk-sharing, but not that no possible financial return could compensate any private investor. Project-level financing conclusions should not be generalized to all fossil-fuel or pipeline investment. TC Energy itself planned a US$2.7 billion investment and announced long-term shipper commitments, so the project still had corporate capital and commercial support [[8]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2020/2020-03-31tc-energy-to-build-keystone-xl-pipeline/).

**Industry Support Was Not Decisive.** ConocoPhillips' 2009 divestment consolidated ownership in TransCanada but does not establish a particular judgment about political risk. Nor does the ultimate cancellation prove that refiners and other industry participants made no effort to support it. TC Energy's termination statement explicitly thanked customers, industry associations, contractors, unions and other supporters [[35]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2021/2021-06-09-tc-energy-confirms-termination-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project/).

**Climate Politics and Governance Shift.** The project became emblematic of fossil fuel opposition in US politics. For environmental organizations and Indigenous nations, Keystone XL's cancellation represented a watershed moment in demonstrating that climate politics could override infrastructure permitting. For the Biden administration, permitting climate-aligned projects while canceling fossil fuel infrastructure was central to the "clean energy" pivot. The permit revocation was not primarily a technical decision about this specific pipeline's emissions; it was a statement about the administration's energy policy direction.

## Environmental and Water Resource Opposition: The Substantive Technical Dimensions

Beyond the political dimensions, the project faced sustained opposition rooted in legitimate environmental and water resource concerns that merit detailed examination.

**The Ogallala Aquifer Vulnerability.** The High Plains Aquifer (Ogallala) covers approximately 27 percent of US irrigated land and is the source of drinking water for 82 percent of people living within its boundary. [[11]](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/sciencebites/chapter/ogallala-aquifer-nebraskan-sandhills-potentially-threatened-by-the-keystone-xl-pipeline/) The Nebraska Sandhills region - the original pipeline route - represents one of the most vulnerable sections of this aquifer due to its hydrogeology. The Sandhills consist primarily of coarse sand and gravel with minimal natural filtration. The grass cover stabilizing the landscape is maintained through active management; disturbing it during pipeline construction would create erosion hazards, and the infrastructure footprint would permanently alter a fragile ecosystem.

Diluted bitumen can present difficult spill-response conditions, but pipeline rupture risk and post-release behaviour must be distinguished. The National Research Council did not find evidence that it was more likely to cause pipeline releases than other crude oils. Once spilled, weathering and interaction with sediment can cause some oil to submerge or sink; it is not correct to say all diluted bitumen invariably has density above water or migrates straight down through an aquifer [[7]](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R43787.html). TransCanada's own spill risk assessment, provided to Canadian regulators, estimated that the Keystone system overall would experience a spill of more than 50 barrels "not more than once every 7-11 years" for the entire US length of pipe. [[40]](https://www.the-independent.com/news/world/americas/keystone-leaks-oil-pipeline-documents-canada-alberta-hardisty-texas-south-dakota-a8079661.html) In South Dakota alone, the company assessed spill risk at once every 41 years. These models proved overly optimistic.

**Existing Keystone Pipeline Spill History.** The operating Keystone Pipeline system (distinct from the XL expansion, but operated by the same company with the same crude) had experienced numerous leaks since it came online in 2010:

- May 2011: Approximately 14,000 gallons in North Dakota (valve failure at pumping station near South Dakota border) [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/)
- April 2016: Approximately 400 barrels, or 16,800 U.S. gallons, near Freeman, South Dakota. This is the same incident as the frequently rounded 17,000-gallon figure, not a separate North Dakota spill [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/).
- The 2016 Freeman incident should be counted once; location and unit differences in retellings do not create additional spills.
- November 2017: approximately 407,400 gallons (about 9,700 barrels) in Marshall County, South Dakota near North Dakota border - the seventh largest onshore oil spill since 2010 in the United States; the spill was caused by damage that occurred during the original 2008 pipeline installation but was not discovered until 2017; regulatory investigations examined installation-related damage and the response [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) [[41]](https://duluthnewstribune.com/4530417-one-year-after-one-nations-largest-oil-pipeline-leaks-happened)
- October 2019: 383,000 gallons (9,120 barrels) in Walsh County, North Dakota near Edinburg; the fourth significant spill in approximately 10 years of operation; affected wetlands [[42]](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/keystone-oil-pipeline-leaks-383000-gallons-in-north-dakota) [[43]](https://www.businessinsider.com/keystone-pipeline-leak-spilled-9000-barrels-oil-north-dakota-wetlands-2019-11)

This history was not abstract to opponents. The November 2017 spill was detected during the incident, not a year afterward; the latent construction damage dated from years earlier. The evidence does not support a claim that contamination extended along 41 miles of pipeline before discovery. Opponents cited these events as reasons to scrutinize installation quality and spill response, while recognizing that the operating Keystone system was distinct from the proposed XL extension.

## The International Trade Disputes: TC Energy and Alberta's Compensation Claims

The project's termination triggered two parallel efforts to recover losses through trade law mechanisms.

### TC Energy's NAFTA Claim

In early July 2021, TC Energy formally notified the United States of its intention to file a claim under NAFTA Chapter 11 (Investor-State Dispute Settlement), seeking more than \$15 billion USD in compensation. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) [[44]](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/canadas-alberta-province-files-trade-challenge-over-scrapped-keystone-xl-2022-02-09/) The claim asserted that the permit revocation violated NAFTA investment protections and constituted an unfair expropriation of the company's investment.

The claim proceeded to arbitration before an ICSID tribunal. In a July 12, 2024 award, publicly announced by TC Energy on July 16, the claim was dismissed on jurisdictional grounds by a 2-1 majority. [[45]](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/tc-energy-says-its-15-bln-claim-keystone-xl-project-thrown-out-by-us-tribunal-2024-07-16/) The tribunal's reasoning was technically precise: NAFTA itself terminated on June 30, 2020, when it was replaced by the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA). The permit revocation occurred on January 20, 2021 - after NAFTA's termination. The USMCA's Annex 14-C provisions, which grandfather legacy claims for investments made during NAFTA, only protect against breaches occurring while NAFTA was in force. Because the permit revocation occurred after NAFTA terminated, the tribunal concluded it lacked jurisdiction to hear the claim. [[46]](https://cdhowe.org/publication/jon-johnson-tc-energy-loses-its-keystone-xl-claim-against-us-2/)

The dissenting arbitrator, Henri Alvarez, argued that the legacy investment conditions had been satisfied because the investment was made while NAFTA was in force, and that substantive obligations should survive termination. However, the majority position prevailed. TC Energy was ordered to pay US legal costs of \$1,482,119.29. [[45]](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/tc-energy-says-its-15-bln-claim-keystone-xl-project-thrown-out-by-us-tribunal-2024-07-16/) In its official statement, TC Energy noted that the claim was "not recognized in financial statements, nor factored into outlook, any potential recoveries." [[47]](https://www.tcenergy.com/newsroom/statements/2024-07-16-tcenergy-disappointed-in-tribunals-ruling-that-nafta-claim-cannot-proceed/) This described the accounting treatment of a contingent recovery; it did not establish that the company had abandoned its legal position.

The award interpreted the temporal reach of the NAFTA legacy-claims provisions. It was a jurisdictional decision, not a finding that the permit revocation was substantively lawful, and not a general authorization for governments to evade investment obligations by terminating any treaty.

### Alberta's NAFTA/CUSMA Claim

On February 9, 2022, Alberta initiated a separate claim under legacy NAFTA provisions, seeking approximately \$1.3 billion CAD (or \$1.6 billion CAD in expanded claims) as compensation for its equity investment loss. [[48]](https://globalnews.ca/news/8606716/alberta-government-compensation-cancelled-keystone-xl-pipeline/) This was an unprecedented claim: rather than a company claiming investor-state protection, a provincial government was asserting that the federal US government had violated fair and equitable treatment obligations to a Canadian subnational government.

Alberta Energy Minister Sonya Savage stated: "We remain disappointed about the events and circumstances." [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) The claim asserted that the permit revocation was politically motivated and that the US failed to provide fair and equitable treatment required under trade law.

The cited reporting establishes that Alberta initiated a separate compensation claim in 2022. It does not verify a final or pending procedural status as of September 2026. That current status should be checked against the actual case record before drawing conclusions about its outcome.

## What Survived and Was Redeployed: The Prairie Connector Project

The most significant surviving legacy was not financial or political, but physical and regulatory. Approximately 150 kilometers of pipeline had been installed in Canada during the 2020 construction phase before work halted in January 2021. [[36]](https://constructionreviewonline.com/michels-canada-selected-to-construct-260km-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project-in-canada/) This preserved infrastructure offered a potential basis for a different proposal, subject to new development decisions and regulatory obligations.

When TC Energy faced financial pressures, the company executed a strategic restructuring. In late 2024, TC Energy spun off its liquids pipeline business, including the partially-laid Keystone XL pipe and the operating Keystone system - into a new company called **South Bow Corporation**. [[49]](https://energynow.ca/2026/05/how-keystone-came-back-from-the-dead/) South Bow inherited not just the 150 kilometers of installed Canadian pipeline but also the valid regulatory permits from the Canada Energy Regulator. The Canadian pipeline permits remained active despite the project's termination, because the regulatory approval occurred at the provincial/federal level independent of the US presidential permit.

Using this legacy infrastructure as a foundation, South Bow partnered with **Bridger Pipeline LLC**, a Wyoming-based operator, to develop the **Prairie Connector** project in Canada, connecting to a separate downstream Bridger Pipeline Expansion in the United States. These are connected proposals, not interchangeable names for one project [[50]](https://www.southbow.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/FactSheet_Prairie-Connector_June-2026.pdf). [[36]](https://constructionreviewonline.com/michels-canada-selected-to-construct-260km-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project-in-canada/) [[49]](https://energynow.ca/2026/05/how-keystone-came-back-from-the-dead/) The project represents a revised, smaller version of the original ambition:

- **Capacity**: 550,000 barrels per day (versus the original 830,000 bbl/day - a 34% reduction)
- **Route**: Originating in Alberta, running to Phillips County, Montana, then south to Guernsey, Wyoming, bypassing the Sandhills entirely
- **Canadian scope**: Approximately 380 km of new pipeline plus about 150 km of preserved pipe, totaling roughly 530 km in Canada. The U.S. Bridger segment is separate; the original 1,188 km combined figure omitted the new Canadian construction [[50]](https://www.southbow.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/FactSheet_Prairie-Connector_June-2026.pdf).
- **Cost estimate**: South Bow said in May 2026 that cost estimates and financing still needed to be finalized before sanction. A reported U.S.-segment budget should not be presented as the combined Canadian-U.S. project cost [[51]](https://www.southbow.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/South-Bow-Announces-Successful-Open-Season.pdf).
- **Construction timeline**: The June 2026 fact sheet targeted a mid-2027 investment decision, construction during mid-2027 to 2028, and service in late 2028, subject to permitting, financing and approval [[50]](https://www.southbow.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/FactSheet_Prairie-Connector_June-2026.pdf).
- **Shipper commitments**: By mid-2026, South Bow announced securing 20-year binding commitments from customers, a critical milestone indicating market demand for the capacity. [[52]](https://pgjonline.com/news/2026/may/keystone-xl-revival-clears-key-hurdle-as-south-bow-eyes-2027-decision) [[53]](https://www.pipeline-journal.net/news/south-bow-secures-shipper-commitments-proposed-keystone-xl-revival-eyes-2027-decision) [[54]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/calgary/south-bow-calgary-keystone-xl-pipeline-contracts-signed-9.7216750)

The Prairie Connector's route deliberately avoids the Sandhills and the most sensitive Ogallala Aquifer sections, routing instead through Montana and Wyoming. This routing change reflected the crystallized opposition to the original Sandhills route - opposition that persisted even after the original project was terminated.

Political circumstances shifted in 2025-2026. In October 2025, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney discussed Keystone XL revival in a White House meeting with President Trump. [[55]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/keystone-xl-expansion-timeline-9.7312229) On February 19, 2025, Trump stated on Truth Social that Keystone XL developers should "get it built NOW." [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) Most significantly, on April 30, 2026, Trump signed a permit for the Bridger Pipeline Expansion - a separate 3-foot diameter pipeline for Canadian crude crossing Montana and Wyoming, clearing a key regulatory hurdle. [[36]](https://constructionreviewonline.com/michels-canada-selected-to-construct-260km-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project-in-canada/) [[56]](https://fortune.com/2026/05/13/frankenpipelines-inside-trumps-bid-resurrect-keystone-xl-stretch-dakota-access-north/)

By August 2026, South Bow stock price rallied sharply on reports that Trump had publicly suggested renewed interest in Keystone XL revival. [[57]](https://energynewsbeat.co/crude-oil/south-bow-shares-rally-after-trump-talks-keystone-xl-revival/) The company targeted a final investment decision in mid-2027, with construction potentially beginning in 2027 and operation by late 2028. [[54]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/calgary/south-bow-calgary-keystone-xl-pipeline-contracts-signed-9.7216750)

## Financial Legacies and Lessons: The Stranded Investment

The financial outcome of Keystone XL termination was substantial and remains instructive.

**TC Energy's Loss**: The company recorded a \$2.2 billion Canadian (\$1.81 billion USD) impairment charge in its 2021 financial statements. [[16]](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/tracker/keystone-xl-pipeline/) Beyond this accounting charge, the company incurred legal and development costs, employment termination expenses, and opportunity costs from the diverted management attention and capital allocation. Most consequentially, the termination demonstrated to investors and creditors that even major pipeline projects with presidential permits and years of regulatory progress could be unilaterally cancelled by a new administration. This risk profile persisted throughout TC Energy's subsequent operations, affecting the company's cost of capital and investor composition.

**Alberta's Loss**: The province reported an approximately C$1.3 billion cost following cancellation, distinct from the initially announced C$1.5 billion equity commitment. The investment became politically contentious: critics questioned the exposure to a foreseeable U.S. election risk, while government ministers defended the decision under the market conditions of 2020. The announced commitment, money actually disbursed and final net loss should not be treated as interchangeable [[37]](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/calgary/keystone-xl-termination-1.6059683) [[39]](https://globalnews.ca/news/7939964/alberta-finance-minister-keystone-xl-loss/).

**Indirect Economic Impacts**: The termination affected employment in Alberta's oil industry and in manufacturing sectors that had mobilized supply chains expecting construction. Economic development incentives that had been promised to regions along the planned route evaporated. However, the loss was partially offset by the continued operation of the southern Keystone segment (completed 2014), which generated ongoing employment and revenue.

## Conclusion: A Project Ended, but Infrastructure Logic Persists

The Keystone XL pipeline's termination on June 9, 2021, resulted from a convergence of political, legal, and financial pressures that made completion impossible. The permit revocation by President Biden on January 20, 2021, was the direct cause, but the underlying vulnerability of the project had been evident for years: it depended on unilateral presidential discretion, faced accumulated legal barriers, required substantial public financial support, and had become a symbol in climate politics.

The project's thirteen-year arc - from 2008 proposal through 2021 termination - reveals how regulatory and political institutions can constrain large infrastructure projects even when they satisfy narrow technical criteria. The repeated environmental impact statements, court injunctions, supplemental legal reviews, and shifts in administered policies created cumulative friction that made completion increasingly costly and uncertain. By 2020, when Alberta's financial backing was central to the construction decision and the permit's future was uncertain, Alberta's government capital infusion was necessary but insufficient. The permit revocation was not a surprise but the culmination of foreseeable political dynamics.

What survived was neither the pipeline nor most of the investment. Rather, the surviving legacies were the regulatory permits in Canada (which remained valid), the approximately 150 kilometres of preserved pipe proposed for reuse, and the demonstrated demand for crude transportation capacity to US refineries (which motivated the Prairie Connector successor). These legacies suggest that while this particular project ended, the underlying infrastructure logic - moving Canadian crude to US refining capacity - persists, merely in revised form and reduced scale.

The project's termination also served as a precedent for future climate politics: it demonstrated that major fossil fuel infrastructure could be blocked even after substantial capital deployment, regulatory approval, and years of development. For renewable energy projects and climate policy advocates, Keystone XL's failure became a reference point. For energy companies and governments, it represented the increasing political risk of long-cycle hydrocarbon infrastructure in jurisdictions with contested climate commitments.
