## A terminated extension, not the whole pipeline

Keystone XL was a proposed crude-oil pipeline from Hardisty, Alberta, to Steele City, Nebraska. TC Energy formally terminated the project on June 9, 2021, after the United States revoked its cross-border presidential permit on January 20. The existing Keystone pipeline system was a separate, operating asset. Cancelling XL did not mean shutting that system down. [[1]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2021/2021-06-09-tc-energy-confirms-termination-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project/) [[2]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2020/2020-03-31tc-energy-to-build-keystone-xl-pipeline/) [[3]](https://apps.cer-rec.gc.ca/PPS/en/pipeline-profiles/keystone)

The pin marks Hardisty as the planned origin of the route. It does not imply that the whole project occupied a single site: this was an infrastructure proposal spanning provinces, states, landowners and two national regulatory systems. [[4]](https://www.cer-rec.gc.ca/en/applications-hearings/view-applications-projects/keystone-xl/project-background.html)

## What was being built

The project was intended to create additional capacity for moving Canadian and US crude to connected refining markets. In its March 2020 construction announcement, TC Energy described approximately 1,947 kilometres of pipeline and capacity of 830,000 barrels a day. Those are the announced design figures for that version of the project, not a measure of completed construction. [[2]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2020/2020-03-31tc-energy-to-build-keystone-xl-pipeline/)

Canada's energy regulator separately documents the Canadian component, including its route from Hardisty toward the international boundary and the approvals attached to it. The Canadian approval did not remove the requirement for a US presidential permit at the border. The project therefore needed several different kinds of permission to remain valid at the same time. [[4]](https://www.cer-rec.gc.ca/en/applications-hearings/view-applications-projects/keystone-xl/project-background.html)

## An approval could be reversed

The political history was not a simple movement from proposal to permit to construction. US administrations took different positions on the project, and the cross-border permission remained vulnerable to reversal. That exposure survived even after years of planning and spending.

In March 2020, TC Energy announced that it would proceed with construction with backing from Alberta. By January 2021, President Joe Biden had revoked the US permit. TC Energy's eventual termination announcement explicitly links that revocation to the suspension of construction. [[1]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2021/2021-06-09-tc-energy-confirms-termination-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project/) [[2]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2020/2020-03-31tc-energy-to-build-keystone-xl-pipeline/)

The five-month interval between suspension and termination matters. January 20 records the loss of the permit and the construction suspension; June 9 records the developer's decision to end the project. They are distinct events, not interchangeable cancellation dates. [[1]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2021/2021-06-09-tc-energy-confirms-termination-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project/)

## Why this attempt ended

The immediate documented reason was the removal of an authorization needed to build and operate the cross-border route. TC Energy then ended the project after reviewing its options and consulting Alberta. That is an evidentially firmer account than saying that the pipe was technically unbuildable or that its economics alone had been disproved. [[1]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2021/2021-06-09-tc-energy-confirms-termination-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project/)

Climate policy, energy security, land rights and the economic interests of different regions all formed part of the dispute. The permit question translated those disagreements into a practical constraint: without the necessary permission, the intended route could not proceed as planned.

## What remained after termination

The termination did not make previous spending disappear. Alberta's project account records the province's involvement and the costs associated with its investment. The headline cost of the whole pipeline proposal, an investor's impairment charge and a government's net loss are different quantities. They should not be combined into one apparently precise price of failure. [[5]](https://www.alberta.ca/keystone-xl-pipeline-project)

Nor should partial construction be confused with a completed operating extension. TC Energy's termination notice said it would continue coordinating safe termination, wind-down and regulatory obligations. It does not provide a complete inventory of every metre of installed pipe or the ultimate destination of every component. [[1]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2021/2021-06-09-tc-energy-confirms-termination-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project/)

The original Keystone system remained distinguishable in the Canadian regulator's pipeline profile. That surviving network is an important counterweight to the dramatic name: one route extension ended, while related infrastructure continued to exist. [[3]](https://apps.cer-rec.gc.ca/PPS/en/pipeline-profiles/keystone)

## Location and confidence

Hardisty is a route-origin pin, not TC Energy's corporate headquarters and not a claim that all abandoned construction lies nearby. Confidence is high in the route association, permit-revocation date and termination announcement because company and regulatory records converge. [[1]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2021/2021-06-09-tc-energy-confirms-termination-of-keystone-xl-pipeline-project/) [[2]](https://www.tcenergy.com/announcements/2020/2020-03-31tc-energy-to-build-keystone-xl-pipeline/) [[4]](https://www.cer-rec.gc.ca/en/applications-hearings/view-applications-projects/keystone-xl/project-background.html)

The broader lesson is an inference: a cross-border megaproject must survive changes in political authority as well as engineering and financing challenges. The sources establish what permission was lost and what the developer did next; they do not establish that the same outcome was inevitable from the project's beginning.
