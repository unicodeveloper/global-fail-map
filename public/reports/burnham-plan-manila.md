# The Burnham Plan of Manila

## What Was Attempted

In 1905, American urban planner Daniel Burnham and Pierce Anderson created a comprehensive masterplan for Manila's modernization under American colonial administration [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnham_Plan_of_Manila). The plan embodied City Beautiful Movement principles and envisioned Manila as a modern metropolis rivaling world capitals. Central to this vision was a **continuous parkway along the Manila Bay waterfront from Luneta to Cavite**, complemented by a government civic center with integrated street systems, harbor facilities, and parks [[13]](https://sinaunangpanahon.com/shaping-manila-american-urban-planning-in-the-philippines-1900-1941/).

The bay-front element was not decorative; it represented the plan's core commitment to recreational public access, with reclaimed waterfront land dedicated to public use rather than private development. The plan also included a comprehensive street hierarchy, beautified esteros (canals), and distributed civic monuments.

## Geographic Connection and Implementation

The plan directly engaged Manila Bay as its central design element. **Roxas Boulevard** (originally Dewey Boulevard) was constructed as part of Burnham's vision and survives as a main thoroughfare [[13]](https://sinaunangpanahon.com/shaping-manila-american-urban-planning-in-the-philippines-1900-1941/). The **Luneta Park (Rizal Park) waterfront enlargement** proceeded with reclamation work. **Three major government buildings** were completed: the Legislative Building, Finance Building, and Agriculture Building. The **Manila Hotel**, designed with panoramic bay views, was constructed. The **moat system in Intramuros** was converted into a canal system per Burnham's specifications [[13]](https://sinaunangpanahon.com/shaping-manila-american-urban-planning-in-the-philippines-1900-1941/).

## What Was Abandoned

By 1939, when President Manuel L. Quezon shifted the seat of government to Quezon City, the unified Burnham vision was effectively terminated. The comprehensive waterfront parkway system was never completed [[13]](https://sinaunangpanahon.com/shaping-manila-american-urban-planning-in-the-philippines-1900-1941/). The integrated civic center concept was never fully realized. The complete estero system rehabilitation was abandoned. The systematic park network that was to connect the entire metropolitan area was superseded by fragmented green space management by multiple agencies.

The **Battle of Manila in February-March 1945** destroyed many completed structures, particularly in the Intramuros civic core [[13]](https://sinaunangpanahon.com/shaping-manila-american-urban-planning-in-the-philippines-1900-1941/). Postwar reconstruction prioritized rapid rebuilding for basic function rather than comprehensive aesthetic and planning principles. By the 1950s, Manila's planning abandoned the unified Burnham framework entirely [[13]](https://sinaunangpanahon.com/shaping-manila-american-urban-planning-in-the-philippines-1900-1941/).

## Current Status and Legacy

The Burnham Plan is today a symbolic reference only. A 2006 law on the National Museum Complex invoked the Burnham Plan name, but this represented nostalgia rather than implementation [[13]](https://sinaunangpanahon.com/shaping-manila-american-urban-planning-in-the-philippines-1900-1941/). No serious revival effort post-1945 has attempted to resurrect the comprehensive waterfront vision. Modern Manila Bay is characterized by fragmented, ad hoc development rather than the integrated public realm Burnham envisioned.

The plan's abandonment was driven by three factors: **(1) Political discontinuity**—shift of capital to Quezon City removed Manila from government focus; **(2) Institutional fragmentation**—multiple agencies managed elements independently; **(3) War destruction and postwar pragmatism**—reconstruction prioritized speed over vision.
