# The Minsk ATETS

## What Was Planned

The Minsk ATETS (Атомная теплоэлектростанция, Atomic Thermal-Electric Power Station) was a dual-purpose nuclear installation designed to supply both electricity and district heating to Minsk, Belarus's capital. [[14]](https://nashaniva.com/en/394715) [[15]](https://sosny.bas-net.by/en/npp_construction/npp_project) Planned during the peak of Soviet confidence in nuclear technology, the facility represented an ambitious attempt to solve Minsk's energy deficit using atomic power, with secondary benefit of providing process heat for residential and industrial district heating systems.

**Technical Specifications:**
- Two VVER-1000 reactors (Soviet pressurized water reactor type)
- Electrical output: 2,000 MW (megawatts) combined [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astravets_Nuclear_Power_Plant)
- Thermal output: 1,800 Gcal/hour (gigacalories per hour) for district heating [[15]](https://sosny.bas-net.by/en/npp_construction/npp_project)
- Individual reactor capacity: 900 MW net / 940 MW gross [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astravets_Nuclear_Power_Plant)
- Location: Pukhovichy District, between the settlements of Svislach and Rudzensk, approximately 40 kilometers southeast of central Minsk [[14]](https://nashaniva.com/en/394715) [[15]](https://sosny.bas-net.by/en/npp_construction/npp_project) [[16]](https://jamestown.org/is-nuclear-power-the-panacea-for-belarusian-energy-problems/)

## Timeline of Construction and Cancellation

- **1980**: Initial planning and site identification completed
- **1983**: Ground-breaking and construction commencement
- **April 26, 1986**: Chernobyl nuclear disaster (Ukraine)
- **1987**: Construction formally abandoned; facility discontinued by CPSU (Communist Party of the Soviet Union) Central Committee and Council of Ministers resolution dated July 1, 1987 [[14]](https://nashaniva.com/en/394715) [[15]](https://sosny.bas-net.by/en/npp_construction/npp_project)

The decision to cancel came less than nine months after Chernobyl. In terms of construction progress, the facility was substantially advanced when cancelled—sufficient capital, workforce, and materials had been mobilized, but the political and psychological environment shifted dramatically. A facility 40 kilometers from Minsk's 1.5-million-person urban population became politically untenable following the evacuation of Pripyat and the radiophobia that swept Eastern Europe. Soviet authorities recognized that public opposition and regional safety concerns would prevent operational commissioning, even if technical completion were achieved.

## What Ended and Why

The ATETS project was formally terminated by a resolution of the supreme political authorities of the Soviet Union. The primary driver was not technical failure but **public radiophobia following Chernobyl**. [[16]](https://jamestown.org/is-nuclear-power-the-panacea-for-belarusian-energy-problems/) The timing was critical: in the USSR's final five years, as glasnost began to permit dissent and public opinion carried greater weight, the political cost of completing the Minsk facility exceeded its energy benefits. Nearby Belarus was also part of the Chernobyl fallout zone, with significant radioactive contamination in areas southeast of Minsk—precisely the regions where the ATETS would have been located.

Unlike some cancelled Soviet projects that were shelved due to cost overruns, technical problems, or resource scarcity, the Minsk ATETS was cancelled despite being constructible and financially viable within Soviet parameters. It was a political decision driven by changed risk perception.

## What Survived and Was Repurposed

**The Repurposing as a Fossil-Fuel Facility:**

Rather than losing the invested capital and workforce entirely, Soviet (later Belarusian) engineers converted the partially-completed facility into a conventional combined heat and power plant (CHPP), known as **Minsk CHPP-5**. [[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Belarus) This installation was completed on the abandoned ATETS foundation and equipment, retaining the original infrastructure envelope and workforce while substituting fossil fuel combustion for nuclear fission.

- **First unit (330 MW)**: Entered service August 4, 1999 [[14]](https://nashaniva.com/en/394715)
- **Second unit**: Completed 2012
- **Current status (2026)**: Operational as Minsk's primary district heating source, serving thousands of residential and industrial consumers

This repurposing represents a pragmatic salvage of capital and expertise. The workforce trained for nuclear operations was redirected to conventional thermal plant operation. The thermal piping networks and heat distribution infrastructure planned for the original nuclear facility were adapted to the fossil-fuel plant.

**Druzhny Settlement — The Worker Community:**

The broader legacy of the ATETS project includes the **Druzhny settlement**, an exemplary "atomograd" (atomic city) constructed to house workers and engineers for the nuclear facility. Built between 1983 and 1986, Druzhny was designed with a distinctive "butterfly" master plan layout and was intended to become a permanent center of scientific and technical expertise. The settlement had a capacity of approximately 5,000–10,000 residents.

Following the Chernobyl disaster and the subsequent Soviet evacuation of contaminated zones in Ukraine, approximately **1,200 workers and their families from Pripyat, Ukraine**, were resettled to Druzhny in May 1986. These evacuees—many of them engineers, technicians, and administrators from Chernobyl itself—brought both expertise and trauma to the community. Druzhny became a dual-purpose settlement: an atomograd for the nascent Minsk ATETS project and a refuge community for Chernobyl evacuees.

When the ATETS was cancelled in 1987, the western section of Druzhny's master plan (residential areas designed to accommodate the full nuclear workforce) was **abandoned**. The "butterfly" layout was never completed; the western wing remained undeveloped, with empty foundations and unfinished infrastructure visible in forested areas. However, the core settlement persisted and evolved. By 2026, Druzhny functions as a satellite suburb of Minsk with approximately 10,000 inhabitants, having shifted from a specialized nuclear worker community to a general residential area. [[14]](https://nashaniva.com/en/394715)

The evacuation of Pripyat workers to Druzhny created an accidental secondary legacy: a concentration of expertise in nuclear safety and operations in a single location, and a permanent diaspora of Chernobyl experience into Belarus's emerging nuclear culture. This context is historically significant for understanding why Belarus later pursued the Astravets plant despite regional opposition—a subset of decision-makers and engineers had direct experience of successful nuclear operation before catastrophe.
