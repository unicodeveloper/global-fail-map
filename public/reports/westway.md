# Westway

## The Proposal and Its Promise

Westway emerged in 1969 when planner Samuel Ratensky proposed a comprehensive redevelopment of Manhattan's deteriorating West Side waterfront [[2]](https://www.thedailybeast.com/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was/) [[4]](https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was). The project crystallized into its final form by 1974: a \$2.1 billion undertaking to demolish the aging West Side Elevated Highway (which had been structurally failing since the 1970s) and replace it with a six-lane underground highway buried beneath forty acres of landfill and parkland [[2]](https://www.thedailybeast.com/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was/) [[3]](https://giltroy.com/the-daily-beast/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was/) [[4]](https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was). The project would have extended from the Battery in Lower Manhattan northward to 42nd Street, creating a new waterfront zone of parks, housing, shops, and offices above ground while accommodating traffic underground [[2]](https://www.thedailybeast.com/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was/) [[3]](https://giltroy.com/the-daily-beast/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was/).

Westway represented the last gasp of traditional New York urbanism: a massive infrastructure project funded largely by the federal government (the plan allocated 90% federal funding) that promised to solve transportation congestion while simultaneously creating new public space [[1]](https://www.thirteen.org/nyvoices/highlights/road_not_taken.html) [[2]](https://www.thedailybeast.com/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was/) [[4]](https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was). The federal government had already approved substantial funding and declared the project a critical metropolitan transportation investment.

## The Environmental Challenge

The fatal flaw in Westway's design was environmental. The project required landfill—specifically, the creation of new land extending into the Hudson River. Environmental consultants hired by the project were required by the Clean Water Act to assess impacts on the Hudson River estuary, including effects on fish populations and water quality.

On June 20, 1984, a landmark disclosure revealed that the environmental consultants had deliberately concealed adverse environmental findings in the Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) [[6]](https://villagepreservation.org/2014/06/25/fighting-westway/). The hidden data showed that the Hudson River sediments at the proposed landfill site contained heavy contamination and PCBs, and that the landfill would have had serious negative impacts on striped bass larvae and other aquatic life [[6]](https://villagepreservation.org/2014/06/25/fighting-westway/). The deliberate concealment of this evidence became, legally and politically, the project's undoing.

## Litigation and Final Abandonment

In 1982, the U.S. District Court, citing Clean Water Act violations and the deficiencies in environmental review, blocked the project [[2]](https://www.thedailybeast.com/westway-new-yorks-great-highway-that-never-was/). The Court of Appeals upheld this decision in September 1985, finding that adequate environmental review had not been conducted [[5]](https://westsidehighwaystudy.com/history). By that time, the concealment of adverse environmental data had become public knowledge, delegitimizing the project entirely. On September 10, 1985, the project was officially abandoned [[6]](https://villagepreservation.org/2014/06/25/fighting-westway/).

The federal funding allocated to Westway—estimated at over \$2 billion—was not lost entirely. Instead, Congress redirected portions of the funds toward alternative transportation and waterfront improvement projects. The most significant alternative that was built: New York State Route 9A, a surface-level boulevard that replaced the deteriorating West Side Elevated Highway between 1996 and 2002. Route 9A achieved many of Westway's original transportation goals without the environmental destruction and ecological damage that Westway would have caused [[5]](https://westsidehighwaystudy.com/history).

## Geographic Location and Current Status

Westway's footprint was Manhattan's west side waterfront, running along the Hudson River from Battery Park (Lower Manhattan) northward to approximately 42nd Street in Midtown. The failed highway itself (the West Side Elevated Highway that Westway was designed to replace) was demolished and replaced with Route 9A by the early 2000s. The waterfront has been redeveloped as parks and mixed-use development, but without the buried highway component that Westway promised. As of September 2026, the West Side of Manhattan has become one of the city's premier waterfront neighborhoods, with Hudson River Greenway parks, residential and commercial development, and public access to the river—outcomes that would have been impossible with Westway's massive infrastructure footprint underground.
