# F. W. Woolworth

## Founding and Explosive Growth

Frank Woolworth opened his first dime store on February 22, 1879, in Utica, New York—which failed after just three months. [[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._W._Woolworth_Company) Five months later, on June 21, 1879, he opened a second store in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, which succeeded, becoming the origin of the five-and-dime empire.

The company incorporated formally on February 16, 1905, and by 1912 had achieved a full corporate merger unifying 596 stores under the Woolworth banner. The F. W. Woolworth Company became one of the most dominant retail enterprises in American history.

## The Woolworth Building and NYC Dominance

The iconic Woolworth Building at 233 Broadway in Lower Manhattan served as the company's headquarters beginning with its completion in 1913. Frank Woolworth paid \$13 million in cash for the building, then the most expensive office structure ever built. The building stood as the tallest structure in the world until 1930. [[27]](https://www.historicwoolworths.com/history) President Woodrow Wilson participated in the ceremonial opening, signifying the national importance of the Woolworth empire.

By 1979, on the company's 100th anniversary, Woolworth operated the largest department store chain in the world, according to Guinness World Records. Woolworth stores occupied every borough of New York City and extended across Long Island and upstate New York. The company employed hundreds of thousands across nearly 6,000 U.S. locations at peak scale.

## Peak Scale and Market Dominance

At the time of Frank Woolworth's death in 1919, the company reported 1,081 stores and \$119 million in annual sales—extraordinary figures for a retailer in an era of limited interstate commerce. [[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._W._Woolworth_Company) By the second half of the 20th century, Woolworth represented a quintessential American retail format, the five-and-dime store, where customers could find variety goods at low prices.

## Competitive Disruption and Strategic Inertia

Woolworth's decline unfolded not through bankruptcy but through obsolescence. Walmart and Kmart—companies that originated from the same dime-store tradition but reimagined the format for modern shopping patterns (suburban locations, high-traffic corridors, ample parking, greater selection, everyday low prices through logistics discipline)—outcompeted Woolworth's traditional small-footprint, urban-focused five-and-dime model.

Supermarkets encroached on Woolworth's merchandise categories. Grocery stores began selling health and beauty products, housewares, and other dry goods that had been Woolworth staples, capturing customers who could buy groceries and variety goods in one trip.

Woolworth launched the Woolco discount chain in 1962, attempting to compete directly with Walmart and Kmart on their own terms, but Woolco failed in the United States and was closed by 1983. Strategic inertia—the company's reluctance to modernize store formats or divest failing locations—meant that Woolworth stores occupied expensive real estate in declining retail districts while competitors opened new format stores in emerging suburban centers.

## The Voluntary Liquidation and Corporate Rebranding

Unlike Lehman Brothers (involuntary bankruptcy) or Wanamaker's (acquired into dissolution), Woolworth chose a voluntary liquidation. On July 17, 1997, the F. W. Woolworth Company announced the closure of all 400 remaining American five-and-dime stores, ending the 117-year retail era. [[26]](https://www.nytimes.com/1997/07/18/business/woolworth-gives-up-on-the-five-and-dime.html) The closure eliminated 9,200 jobs. Stock closed on the final day at \$27.56, up \$2.50 from the previous session—a surprising rally suggesting investors believed the company's remaining assets and strategic repositioning held value.

The company did not simply disappear. On June 12, 1998, Woolworth renamed itself Venator Group, Inc. (Latin for "sportsman"), reflecting a pivot toward sporting goods retail. The company relocated from the iconic Woolworth Building (which was sold in 1998) to 34th Street in Manhattan by 1999. On October 20, 2001, Venator renamed itself Foot Locker, Inc., adopting the brand of its pre-1997 subsidiaries.

## International Operations and Asset Divestiture

Woolworth had operated stores internationally through the 20th century, but these operations were divested: UK operations closed in 1982, Mexican operations in 1997, and German operations in 1997. By the time the American five-and-dime stores closed, international presence was minimal.

## What Survived

Foot Locker, Inc. survived as the legal continuation of the Woolworth Company, maintaining the same stock trading history and corporate registration despite the name change. Foot Locker operated as an athletic footwear and apparel retailer into the 21st century, including survival through the 2008 financial crisis and sustained operations through 2025. However, on November 25, 2024, Dick's Sporting Goods announced the acquisition of Foot Locker for \$2.4 billion, resulting in the final absorption of the Woolworth legacy. The Woolworth Building at 233 Broadway survives as an iconic New York landmark, selling in 1998 and serving subsequent ownership and commercial purposes.
