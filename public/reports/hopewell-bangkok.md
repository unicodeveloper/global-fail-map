# The Hopewell Project

The Bangkok Elevated Road and Train System (BERTS), popularly known as the Hopewell Project, represented one of Asia's most audacious infrastructure undertakings of the early 1990s. It was also one of the most notorious failures.

## Scope and Initial Approval

The project's scale was staggering. BERTS was designed as a 60.10-kilometre elevated highway and rail corridor system connecting multiple zones of Bangkok: starting from Hua Lamphong Railway Station (the city's main railway terminus), running north to Don Mueang Airport, and incorporating east-west segments to serve the broader metropolitan area. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok_Elevated_Road_and_Train_System) [[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_Rapid_Transit_Master_Plan_in_Bangkok_Metropolitan_Region) The entire scheme was approved by the Thai Cabinet on 9 November 1990 for an estimated 80 billion baht—an enormous sum for that era—without a formal feasibility study. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok_Elevated_Road_and_Train_System)

## The Partnership and Political Approval

The joint venture partners were the Thai Ministry of Transport, the State Railway of Thailand, and Hopewell Holdings, a Hong Kong-based developer headed by Gordon Wu. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok_Elevated_Road_and_Train_System) [[16]](https://coconuts.co/bangkok/news/news-section-of-bangkok-abandoned-highway-project-collapses/) The approval process was rapid and politically driven rather than technically rigorous. Construction began in 1990, with crews mobilizing across Bangkok to begin pile-driving and viaduct construction.

## Construction Progress and Financial Crisis

For two years, visible progress was made. Concrete pillars rose throughout Bangkok's skyline. However, in 1992, the Anand Panyarachun government suspended the project following political tensions and the collapse of the Lavalin Skytrain, which had created public skepticism of mega-projects. Work stalled but did not formally terminate. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok_Elevated_Road_and_Train_System) [[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_Rapid_Transit_Master_Plan_in_Bangkok_Metropolitan_Region)

By 1997, as construction had barely reached 10–13 percent completion, the Asian Financial Crisis struck Thailand. On 2 July 1997, the Thai baht collapsed against the US dollar. By July and August 1997, depositors withdrew over \$1.2 billion from Thai financial institutions as public confidence evaporated. [[31]](https://www.nytimes.com/1997/03/06/IHT-depositors-withdraw-12-billion-as-stocks-and-currency-slump-thailand.html) Between August and December 1997, the Thai government closed 56 of 58 finance companies, officially eliminating nearly all of the nation's secondary lenders. [[32]](https://www.nytimes.com/1997/12/09/business/worldbusiness/IHT-only-2-of-58-companies-are-cleared-to-reopen.html) [[34]](https://www.nytimes.com/1997/12/09/business/56-troubled-lenders-closed-by-thailand.html) The consortium funding the Hopewell Project, like every major developer in Thailand, suddenly found itself unable to raise capital, secure project financing, or continue payments to contractors.

The Cabinet formally terminated the Hopewell Project in 1998. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok_Elevated_Road_and_Train_System) [[17]](https://the-singapore-lgbt-encyclopaedia.fandom.com/wiki/BTS_Skytrain)

## Physical Legacy and Ongoing Disputes

The decision to abandon BERTS left over 1,000 concrete pillars standing across Bangkok—particularly between Bang Sue and Don Mueang—creating an eerie landscape that journalists and residents nicknamed "Thailand's Stonehenge." [[16]](https://coconuts.co/bangkok/news/news-section-of-bangkok-abandoned-highway-project-collapses/) The pillars marked the intended route; the absence of connecting beams and decking rendered them useless.

Demolition proved protracted and costly. The east-west segments of the viaduct were demolished between 2005 and 2007 during the construction of the Suvarnabhumi Airport Link (a competing rail project that succeeded). [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok_Elevated_Road_and_Train_System) The north-south pillars remained standing for over a decade before demolition began in 2013. [[16]](https://coconuts.co/bangkok/news/news-section-of-bangkok-abandoned-highway-project-collapses/)

The project also proved dangerous. On 1 March 2012, a 150-metre section of the abandoned structure collapsed near Bang Khen in north Bangkok, close to Wat Samian Nari temple. [[16]](https://coconuts.co/bangkok/news/news-section-of-bangkok-abandoned-highway-project-collapses/) Investigations revealed that scrappers had stolen metal scaffolding and support components, undermining the structural integrity of the concrete pillars. The collapse occurred during rush hour but, fortunately, did not result in the mass casualties that could have occurred.

## Protracted Litigation

Unlike many failed projects, the Hopewell Project generated decades of legal dispute. In April 2019, the Supreme Administrative Court ordered the State Railway of Thailand to pay Hopewell Holdings 11.88 billion baht in compensation, plus 7.5 percent annual interest—totalling approximately 25 billion baht when compounded. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok_Elevated_Road_and_Train_System) However, in September 2023, the Central Administrative Court partially overruled this decision, reducing or reassessing the obligation. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangkok_Elevated_Road_and_Train_System) As of the research period (2026), litigation was ongoing, illustrating how institutional failures can extend litigation timelines across decades.
