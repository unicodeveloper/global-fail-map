# The Trans-Amazonian Highway

## The Ambition

On June 16, 1970, Brazilian military President Emílio Garrastazu Médici announced one of the world's most audacious highway projects: the Trans-Amazonian Highway, a 4,977-kilometer east-west corridor intended to connect Brazil's impoverished northeast with the Amazon basin and Peru. [[8]](https://newint.org/features/1980/10/01/brazil) The declared purpose was both geopolitical—to assert Brazilian sovereignty in the Amazon during the Cold War—and developmental: "to march towards the interior" and transform the region into a productive zone through directed settlement. [[12]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/politics-and-government/trans-amazon-highway-announced) The ceremonial first felling of trees occurred on October 9, 1970, in Altamira itself, establishing the city as the symbolic and logistical headquarters of the entire enterprise. [[8]](https://newint.org/features/1980/10/01/brazil)

Construction commenced with extraordinary urgency. The first section between Belém and Altamira was formally opened on August 27, 1972, and by the mid-1970s, approximately 4,260 kilometers of the planned 4,977-kilometer route had been marked, cleared, or partially paved. [[9]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Trans-Amazonian_Highway) The ambition was explicit: a spine road with perpendicular "feeder roads" branching into forest, 100-hectare plots laid out for settler families, and "agrovilas"—planned agricultural villages housing 50 families each—every 10 kilometers along the route. [[10]](https://thedarkatlas.com/posts/trans-amazonian-highway-br-230)

## Construction Reality and Failure of the Western Sections

The western third of the highway—the most ambitious and most remote segments intended to reach Peru—never achieved the status of functional infrastructure. Multiple sources confirm that construction mortality from malaria and yellow fever was severe. Contemporary field research by U.S. ecologist Philip Fearnside documented that colonization projects along the newly opened Trans-Amazonian corridor in Pará encountered disease incidence that halted progress. [[7]](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/08/for-50-years-philip-fearnside-has-calculated-the-costs-of-losing-the-amazon/) [[22]](https://mongabay1.substack.com/p/for-50-years-philip-fearnside-has) The Pastoral Land Commission (Comissão Pastoral da Terra) found that in stretches between Altamira–Itaituba and Marabá–Altamira, 48% of initial 1,187 assigned land plots had been abandoned within six years of allocation. [[6]](https://revistapesquisa.fapesp.br/en/roberto-araujo-oliveira-santos-junior-the-limits-of-an-amazonian-dream/)

Beyond disease and settlement failure, the fundamental infrastructure itself could not withstand the Amazon's hydrological and soil conditions. The road, once constructed, deteriorated within months of completion. Seasonal flooding rendered the western sections impassable for 4–6 months annually. [[9]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Trans-Amazonian_Highway) Evaluations attribute this infrastructure decay to the absence of prior cost-benefit analyses and the fundamental unsuitability of the region for the transport model being imposed: adequate local agricultural productivity existed on only 17% of the land along the corridor, making the economic premise of the settlement scheme untenable. [[9]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Trans-Amazonian_Highway)

## Current Status as of 2026

As of September 9, 2026, the Trans-Amazonian Highway exists in fragmented form. The eastern third—from Belém through Altamira to the Xingu—remains paved and functional. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BR-163_(Brazil_highway)) The central third consists partly of paved sections and partly of deteriorating dirt track. The western third, particularly segments intended to reach Peruvian territory, remains impassable during wet seasons and is effectively abandoned. [[5]](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/environment/chpt/transamazon-highway) No ceremonial completion or official closure document exists; rather, the western terminus into Peru was progressively deprioritized and funding withdrawn through the 1980s and 1990s.

The official length of the project as constructed is often cited as 4,260 kilometers by the mid-1970s, with the remaining 717 kilometers never reaching serviceable condition. [[4]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259470596_The_Transamazon_Highway_Past_present_and_future) [[11]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282574458_The_Transamazon_Highway_Past_Present_Future) Modern citations to the Trans-Amazonian Highway typically refer only to the functioning eastern sections, implicitly conceding that the western ambition was never realized.

> **Human Toll:** The Trans-Amazonian construction and colonization scheme coincided with catastrophic disease outbreaks among Parakanã Indigenous peoples in the construction zone. Parakanã populations experienced 70% mortality within six weeks of first contact with construction workers in 1971—a collapse attributed to measles and other introduced pathogens for which the Parakanã had no immunity. [[7]](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/08/for-50-years-philip-fearnside-has-calculated-the-costs-of-losing-the-amazon/)
