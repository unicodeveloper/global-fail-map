# Forest City

The contemporary landscape of Johor Bahru is dominated by five major residential and commercial projects that have stalled in various states of completion, leaving thousands of buyers without possession and developers unable to fulfil contractual obligations. These cases constitute the most acute unrealised vision crisis in the city's modern history.

## Forest City: From \$100 Billion Megaproject to "Ghost City" (2006–Present)

Forest City represents the largest and most extensively documented failed vision in Johor Bahru's contemporary history. Conceived in 2006 by China's Country Garden Holdings, one of the nation's largest developers, Forest City was designed as an integrated property development and special financial zone on reclaimed land in southwestern Johor Bahru (Gelang Patah). [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_City,_Johor) The project occupied four artificial islands built from dredged seabed material. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_City,_Johor)

The scope was extraordinary: Forest City was marketed as capable of housing 700,000 residents—more than doubling Johor Bahru's entire population. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_City,_Johor) [[14]](https://www.quora.com/What-happened-to-the-Forest-City-development-in-Johor-Malaysia-which-was-expected-to-house-700-000-people) The total investment was estimated at approximately \$100 billion, making it among the world's costliest megaprojects. [[7]](https://www.architecturaldigest.com/story/forest-city-an-abandoned-dollar100-billion-development) [[1]](https://www.dlubal.com/en/news-and-events/news/blog/000179) Marketing imagery depicted futuristic architecture including spacecraft-shaped towers draped in greenery, positioned to appeal to affluent Singaporean retirees, expatriate professionals, and international wealth. [[8]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/johor-country-garden-forest-city-property-crisis-debt-china-3715791) [[23]](https://www.iminvestors.com/danga-bay.html)

Construction commenced and the first phase was officially inaugurated on March 6, 2016 by Malaysian Prime Minister Najib Razak, creating an impression of assured success. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_City,_Johor) However, occupancy never approached projections. By June 2023, approximately 8,000–9,000 residents occupied the development—about 1% of designed capacity. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_City,_Johor) [[7]](https://www.architecturaldigest.com/story/forest-city-an-abandoned-dollar100-billion-development) By August 2023, only 15% of total construction had been completed despite seven years of operation. [[7]](https://www.architecturaldigest.com/story/forest-city-an-abandoned-dollar100-billion-development) Of the 16,000 units sold by 2019, occupancy remained at roughly 1%. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_City,_Johor)

The project encountered three convergent crises. First, a regulatory policy reversal in Malaysia under Prime Minister Mahathir's government restricted foreign residency in the project—famously characterised as "buyers can buy but cannot stay." [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_City,_Johor) [[14]](https://www.quora.com/What-happened-to-the-Forest-City-development-in-Johor-Malaysia-which-was-expected-to-house-700-000-people) This policy, intended to protect Malaysian housing stock for citizens, devastated buyer confidence among the international clientele that formed the project's economic foundation. Second, Country Garden Holdings faced a severe financial crisis beginning in 2023, defaulting on bond payments and becoming engulfed in China's broader property sector collapse. [[8]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/johor-country-garden-forest-city-property-crisis-debt-china-3715791) [[10]](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-67610677) Third, the environmental baseline proved challenging: the development site disturbed protected seagrass meadows in the Johor Strait, raising ecological concerns. [[21]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sjtg.12437)

By 2023–2024, Forest City had acquired the informal designation of the world's most expensive "ghost city." [[1]](https://www.dlubal.com/en/news-and-events/news/blog/000179) [[10]](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-67610677) However, Forest City was not entirely abandoned. In response to the financial and market collapse, Malaysian authorities designated the project as a Special Financial Zone (SFZ) in 2024–2025, creating a policy framework for potential recovery through financial services and technology investment rather than residential property alone. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_City,_Johor)

A symbolic marker of deeper stall: Forest City's Network School subsidiary, a premium international school intended to serve the development's expatriate community, officially closed in July 2026—a decade after the project's inaugural opening and after occupancy remained at ghost-town levels. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_City,_Johor) This closure underscored the demographic failure: there were insufficient foreign residents to sustain the ecosystem of services designed for them.

As of September 2026, Forest City remains operationally active with some services functioning but commercially failed relative to its \$100 billion vision. It is neither fully abandoned nor successfully realised—a permanent monument to overambitious financing and regulatory miscalculation.

## Sovereign Bay: A Decade of Abandonment and Attempted Revival (2016–Present)

Sovereign Bay represents a more straightforward abandonment case. Located in Permas Jaya, this luxury service apartment project was marketed to Singaporean retirees and investors with a promised completion date of October 2019. [[4]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-bahru-sick-abandoned-stalled-property-buyers-limbo-5379511)

By September 2025—six years past deadline—the project remained unfinished with no demonstrated construction progress. The Ministry of Housing classified Sovereign Bay as an abandoned project. [[4]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-bahru-sick-abandoned-stalled-property-buyers-limbo-5379511) Singaporean buyers, including a swimming coach who committed to paying \$2,000 SGD per month for a 10-year service charge on a \$1 million RM unit (approximately \$350,000 USD) despite not holding possession, remained locked in financial obligations without access to their purchased units. [[4]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-bahru-sick-abandoned-stalled-property-buyers-limbo-5379511)

A potential resolution emerged in April 2026 when an unnamed Chinese developer reportedly acquired Sovereign Bay with intent to complete construction. [[9]](https://forums.hardwarezone.com.sg/threads/cna-what-next-for-buyers-of-johor-bahru-condos-uncompleted-for-years.7198691/) However, the developer identity remains unconfirmed and no completion timeline has been publicly released as of September 2026. This case illustrates a pattern in which projects abandoned for years occasionally attract distressed asset buyers seeking to recover value, but revival remains fragile and timelines uncertain.

## The Peak: Thirteen Years Overdue and Deteriorating (2016–Present)

The Peak represents a case of sustained abandonment with deteriorating asset quality. This twin-tower service apartment project in Tebrau was developed by Gabungan AQRS with an original promised completion of 2016.

As of 2025, the project remains incomplete after 13 years of delay, making it substantially overdue even relative to the stalled projects surrounding it. [[4]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-bahru-sick-abandoned-stalled-property-buyers-limbo-5379511) One tower exists only as a bare concrete skeleton; the other shows no substantive progress toward completion. The sales gallery in Kuala Lumpur itself remains under construction—a sign that the project lacks even the financial capacity to maintain its own sales infrastructure. [[4]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-bahru-sick-abandoned-stalled-property-buyers-limbo-5379511)

Australian buyers who invested A$180,000 (approximately \$130,000 USD) towards retirement in Malaysia abandoned their commitment and ceased loan payments after recognising that completion was indefinitely postponed. [[4]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-bahru-sick-abandoned-stalled-property-buyers-limbo-5379511) No credible completion date has been announced, and there is no evidence of active developer pursuit of project completion or financial restructuring.

## Jade Palace: Seven Years Overdue, Completion Still Uncertain (2020–Present)

Jade Palace, a luxury service apartment development at Danga Bay by Chinese developer Greenland Group, was originally contracted for completion in 2020. [[4]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-bahru-sick-abandoned-stalled-property-buyers-limbo-5379511) As of December 2024, the Malaysian Deputy Minister of Housing expressed hope that construction might be completed by the third quarter of 2027—a delay of seven years and a timeline that remains speculative rather than contractually binding. [[4]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-bahru-sick-abandoned-stalled-property-buyers-limbo-5379511)

The case illustrates how some projects persist in states of indefinite delay without formal abandonment declarations. Buyers remain in legal limbo, unable to take possession yet unable to obtain full refunds.

## Kampong Skudai Kiri: The Relocation Scheme That Never Completed (2003–Present)

Unlike the service apartment projects, Kampong Skudai Kiri represents an unrealised social vision with direct human impact. This was a planned relocation and resettlement scheme for a fishing village community targeted for displacement by Danga Bay waterfront development. The scheme sought to resettle 784 households—the core population of the fishing village—which would be expropriated when Danga Bay development proceeded.

A government policy adopted in 2005 aimed at achieving "zero squatter" status by 2007, committing to resettle all 784 households. [[15]](https://s3.us-west-1.wasabisys.com/p-library/books/99375ca27c889e55608b22c33ffe64c6.pdf) However, implementation stalled dramatically. By 2010, when academic field research documented the scheme, only 80 of the 784 households—approximately 10%—had been relocated. [[15]](https://s3.us-west-1.wasabisys.com/p-library/books/99375ca27c889e55608b22c33ffe64c6.pdf) The remaining 704 households remained in the original village facing uncertainty, inadequate compensation, and forced waiting.

The economics of relocation proved prohibitive for affected families. The replacement housing offered to relocated families cost between RM300,000 and RM500,000 (approximately \$68,000–\$113,000 USD) per unit. Although Bumiputera (indigenous Malaysian) subsidies and government compensation were available in principle, the actual amounts proved insufficient for most households to afford the replacement units. [[15]](https://s3.us-west-1.wasabisys.com/p-library/books/99375ca27c889e55608b22c33ffe64c6.pdf) Fishing communities, which had derived livelihoods from river access at the village site, lost their primary income source upon relocation without viable alternative employment.

The relocation scheme was formally suspended indefinitely in the early 2010s. The 2007 completion deadline passed without achievement, and no revised timeline was established. The village itself was left in a state of permanent precarity—inhabited by residents with no secure tenure, waiting for an expropriation that remained perpetually threatened but never executed.

A parallel crisis involved the Orang Seletar, an indigenous maritime community with customary territorial claims to Danga Bay and surrounding waters. Since 2012, large tracts of ancestral land—including customary use areas—were progressively sold to overseas investors developing Danga Bay projects. [[21]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/sjtg.12437) The Orang Seletar initiated legal action to recover compensation for customary land loss. In 2017, the Johor High Court ruled in favour of the Orang Seletar, ordering compensation for loss of customary territory. [[24]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-strait-orang-seletar-climate-change-coastal-developments-2766396) However, implementation of the judgment was delayed, and as of 2022 (five years after the court ruling), the Orang Seletar faced ongoing ecosystem degradation from coastal development even as compensation remained incomplete. [[24]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-strait-orang-seletar-climate-change-coastal-developments-2766396)

Contemporary reporting from 2022 noted that the Orang Seletar—indigenous sea nomads who have inhabited the Johor Strait between Singapore and Peninsular Malaysia for centuries—faced existential threat: "In 10 years, we may no longer be here," as one community member stated, due to combined effects of climate change, dwindling seafood stocks, and coastal development displacement. [[24]](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/asia/malaysia-johor-strait-orang-seletar-climate-change-coastal-developments-2766396) Families were temporarily relocated to Dewan Muafakat in Johor, living in tents, the emotional toll of displacement compounded by loss of traditional occupation. [[22]](https://oranglautsg.substack.com/p/orang-asli-kon-seletar)

As of September 2026, the Kampong Skudai Kiri relocation scheme remains substantially unrealised. Most of the original 784 households have not been relocated. The fishing village persists in legal limbo. The Orang Seletar continue pursuing compensation implementation nearly a decade after court judgment.
