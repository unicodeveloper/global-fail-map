# Shahestan Pahlavi

## What Was Planned

On 554 to 600 hectares of north-central Tehran, the Shah's government envisioned "one of the world's largest planned city centres." [[25]](https://www.nytimes.com/1975/09/01/archives/teheran-planning-one-of-the-worlds-largest-plazas.html) The British consulting firm Llewelyn-Davies International prepared the master plan, approved in late 1974. The design featured a ceremonial urban fabric of monumental scale: a **30-meter monument in Shah and Nation Square**, a 12-hectare Grand Plaza, King Boulevard lined with ministerial headquarters, luxury residential towers, the Pahlavi National Library, state museums, state theater, foreign ministry headquarters, and administrative complexes. The project represented the apex of pre-Revolutionary modernist urbanism—a built expression of the Shah's technological and institutional power. [[26]](https://www.nytimes.com/1975/01/29/archives/iran-plans-big-project-to-make-teheran-a-major-world-capital.html) [[27]](https://www.nytimes.com/1975/01/16/archives/a-changing-iran-wonders-whether-the-gain-will-exceed-the-loss.html)

Land acquisition occurred from 1969 through 1971. The Abbasabad Lands Development Plan Act passed in June 1971. [[3]](https://www.academia.edu/1386910/Tehran_the_history_of_decentralization_1800_2000) By October 1973, international design competitions attracted entries from renowned architects Kenzo Tange and Louis I. Kahn, signaling the project's prestige. [[29]](https://www.konesh.space/post/errors-of-scale-the-story-of-tehran-s-abbasabad-lands) Llewelyn-Davies' master plan was approved in late 1974, and earthworks commenced in 1975.

## Timeline and Geographic Connection

The site spans the Abbasabad hills in north-central Tehran, a 554–600 hectare expanse designated within the Seventh Municipal District. [[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbas_Abad_(Tehran)) The cost estimate reached \$3 billion at 1975 pricing. [[3]](https://www.academia.edu/1386910/Tehran_the_history_of_decentralization_1800_2000) Contemporary press coverage documented the project's ambitions in detail. [[25]](https://www.nytimes.com/1975/09/01/archives/teheran-planning-one-of-the-worlds-largest-plazas.html) [[26]](https://www.nytimes.com/1975/01/29/archives/iran-plans-big-project-to-make-teheran-a-major-world-capital.html) [[27]](https://www.nytimes.com/1975/01/16/archives/a-changing-iran-wonders-whether-the-gain-will-exceed-the-loss.html)

## Why It Ended

The Islamic Revolution of February 1979 repudiated the Shah's modernization vision. The new revolutionary leadership explicitly rejected ceremonial secular urbanism, monumental plazas meant to glorify state power, and the architectural language of Western modernism. The Shahestan Pahlavi project became a symbol of everything the revolution opposed: unchecked state spending, foreign architectural influence, and "un-Islamic" urban design.

## Post-Revolutionary Fragmentation: Errors of Scale

The site was not left empty. Rather, it underwent a series of competing, uncoordinated master plans (1983, 1986, 1998, 2001, 2004, 2005) imposed by different state ministries and revolutionary institutions. [[1]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02665433.2024.2436144) Four major expressways—including the Haghani Expressway—were constructed during 1998–2005, fragmenting the original 600-hectare vision into "seven fragmentary islands surrounded by a sea of asphalt." [[1]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02665433.2024.2436144) [[29]](https://www.konesh.space/post/errors-of-scale-the-story-of-tehran-s-abbasabad-lands)

Planning Perspectives academic journal documented this breakdown in an article titled "Fragmented: highways and the failure of the Abbasabad project in Tehran," analyzing how oversized ceremonial geometry (12-hectare plazas, 30-meter monuments, King Boulevard) proved incompatible with fragmented land control and vehicular circulation networks. The authors characterized this phenomenon as "Errors of Scale"—borrowing architecture historian Robin Middleton's critique of Le Corbusier's failed Plan Voisin of 1925. Monumental plazas functioned as "temporary expo sites" rather than integrated urban fabric. [[1]](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02665433.2024.2436144) [[29]](https://www.konesh.space/post/errors-of-scale-the-story-of-tehran-s-abbasabad-lands)

## What Was Built Within the Fragmented Framework

Despite the failure to realize the coherent master plan, substantial projects were constructed on portions of the Abbasabad site:

- **Taleghani Park (30 hectares)**: The sole major surviving element from the pre-1979 plan; completed and now functional.
- **Imam Khomeini Musalla (Grand Mosque)**: Begun in 1987, remains incomplete as of 2025 despite 30+ years of intermittent construction.
- **Government office towers**: Built post-2005, violating the coherent master plan's density guidelines.
- **National Library of Iran**: 97,000 square meters of floor space, constructed.
- **Central Bank Museum**: 106,000 square meters.
- **Islamic Revolution and Holy Defence Museum**: Completed.
- **Tehran Book Garden**: Constructed.
- **Pole Tabiat pedestrian bridge**: Completed in 2014, designed by Zaha Hadid Architects as a symbolic reconciliation between modernist form and public space.
- **Bayhaqi Bus Terminal**: Completed in 1992.
- **Sohrevardi Metro Station**: Opened in 2016, serving the cultural complex.

## Current Status and Governance Impasse

By December 2024, the Site functioned as an archipelago of isolated mega-buildings disconnected by freeways, violating the coherence intended by any master plan. In December 2024, a cooperation agreement was signed between Tehran Municipality and national cultural organizations focusing on preservation and environmental restoration—a pragmatic acknowledgment that the original ceremonial vision would never be realized. [[28]](https://grokipedia.com/page/abbas_abad_tehran) The site has effectively entered a second phase: from failed imperial center to cultural/administrative patchwork.

The Abbasabad case illustrates how political revolution, fragmented state authority, and transportation infrastructure imposed on historical planning can reduce even a \$3 billion conceived project into functional failure—not through lack of construction, but through lack of coherence.
