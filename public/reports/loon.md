## Internet balloons reach customers, then stop

Loon tried to connect people to the internet using a moving network of balloons in the stratosphere. The idea progressed from experiments to commercial service with Telkom Kenya in July 2020. In January 2021, Alphabet announced that Loon would wind down because the route to a sustainable business remained too long and risky. [[1]](https://telkom.co.ke/press-release/telkom-and-loon-announce-progressive-deployment-of-loon-technology-to-customers-from-july/) [[2]](https://www.theverge.com/2021/1/21/22243484/alphabet-google-shutting-down-loon-internet-balloon-company-x) [[3]](https://x.company/collection/loon/)

The shutdown announcement was reported on January 21, 2021. It did not mean the balloons had never carried useful traffic. Nor did it mean every balloon was immediately removed from the sky that day. A corporate decision and the operational process of safely winding down a flying network are different events. [[1]](https://telkom.co.ke/press-release/telkom-and-loon-announce-progressive-deployment-of-loon-technology-to-customers-from-july/) [[2]](https://www.theverge.com/2021/1/21/22243484/alphabet-google-shutting-down-loon-internet-balloon-company-x)

## A network in moving air

Loon's balloons were carried by stratospheric winds. Navigation depended on finding useful winds at different altitudes and coordinating a fleet, rather than simply holding a balloon above one town. The team's own account describes launch operations, weather models and journeys from launch sites in Puerto Rico or Nevada toward Kenya. [[4]](https://x.company/blog/posts/take-me-to-kenya/)

Radio equipment linked the balloons to ground stations and mobile networks. For a user, the intended experience was an ordinary connection to an LTE-capable phone, not a special satellite terminal. Behind that simple interface were launches, aviation permissions, power budgets, communications links and eventual recovery of the balloon hardware. [[1]](https://telkom.co.ke/press-release/telkom-and-loon-announce-progressive-deployment-of-loon-technology-to-customers-from-july/) [[4]](https://x.company/blog/posts/take-me-to-kenya/)

## Kenya was a real deployment

Telkom's July 7, 2020 announcement described service beginning that month across a region of nearly 50,000 square kilometres in central and western Kenya. It named locations including Eldoret, Nakuru, Kisumu and Narok. The announcement described approximately 35 or more balloons participating in the service. [[1]](https://telkom.co.ke/press-release/telkom-and-loon-announce-progressive-deployment-of-loon-technology-to-customers-from-july/)

It also stated limitations. Wind patterns and restricted airspace could interrupt availability, and the solar-powered deployment initially offered service during specified daytime and evening hours. Those details matter because a technical demonstration and a consistently available communications service have different requirements. [[1]](https://telkom.co.ke/press-release/telkom-and-loon-announce-progressive-deployment-of-loon-technology-to-customers-from-july/)

The footprint should not be mistaken for a subscriber count or a claim that every person inside the outlined region received continuous service. The launch announcement does not establish either of those quantities.

## Why the company wound down

X's retrospective says that commercial viability proved more distant and risky than hoped. Contemporary closure coverage carried the same central explanation: the team had not found a way to bring costs low enough for a long-term sustainable business. [[2]](https://www.theverge.com/2021/1/21/22243484/alphabet-google-shutting-down-loon-internet-balloon-company-x) [[5]](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/01/21/alphabet-cancels-loon-project-to-beam-internet-to-earth-from-balloons.html) [[3]](https://x.company/collection/loon/)

That statement leaves room for technical accomplishment. Loon had built and operated a system; the unanswered question was whether its costs, reliability, deployment demands and attainable revenue could support the business at scale. Public material does not provide a complete unit-economics model that assigns an exact share of the problem to each factor.

## A deliberately preserved inheritance

The Loon Collection is a concrete surviving output. It includes technical, operational and scientific documents, flight-related resources and data. X also records patent transfers to partners and a non-assertion pledge covering more than 200 other patents. The precise terms live in the linked pledge; this is not a claim that every Loon patent became public domain. [[3]](https://x.company/collection/loon/)

Some optical-communications work continued through Taara, which uses light links for connectivity. X explicitly traces that relationship in its project archive. A useful subsystem could continue in a different setting even though the complete balloon-network business ended. [[6]](https://x.company/projects/taara/) [[7]](https://x.company/projects/loon/)

These are inspectable remnants, not just a vague assertion that the company learned something. They let future engineers see part of what was tried and reuse knowledge without recreating the whole venture.

## What the pin means

The Mountain View, California pin marks Loon's corporate base. Kenya was the commercial deployment geography; Puerto Rico and Nevada were launch locations described by the team. Keeping those roles separate prevents a headquarters marker from pretending to show where connectivity was delivered. [[1]](https://telkom.co.ke/press-release/telkom-and-loon-announce-progressive-deployment-of-loon-technology-to-customers-from-july/) [[8]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-alphabet-loon-factbox/factbox-inside-loons-internet-balloon-venture-idUSKCN1TW1H0/) [[4]](https://x.company/blog/posts/take-me-to-kenya/)

Confidence is high in the service launch, wind-down decision and published technical inheritance. Detailed subscriber economics and the complete reasons behind the investment decision remain less visible. The documented ending is a company wound down after real deployment, not a blanket verdict against stratospheric communications.
