# Loon: The Rise and Collapse of Alphabet's Stratospheric Internet Dream

## The Original Vision: Reaching the Last Billion

In June 2013, Google announced an ambitious project with a deceptively simple premise: float solar-powered balloons at 60,000 feet above the Earth's surface to beam internet to remote areas beyond the reach of conventional infrastructure [[1]](https://www.wired.com/2013/08/googlex-project-loon/). The vision was explicit and expansive - to connect "the last billion users" [[1]](https://www.wired.com/2013/08/googlex-project-loon/), the economically marginal populations in regions too remote, sparsely populated, or commercially unattractive for traditional telecommunications investment. Project Loon was not a backup technology or a niche solution; it was conceived as a fundamental reimagining of global connectivity infrastructure.

The project began informally in 2011 under Rich DeVaul at Google X, the company's secretive research division [[2]](https://x.company/projects/loon/) [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). Its first person to connect was Charles Nimmo, a New Zealand sheep farmer [[1]](https://www.wired.com/2013/08/googlex-project-loon/), a symbolic choice emphasizing Loon's focus on rural populations in developed economies before expanding to emerging markets. By the time of its formal public announcement in Christchurch, New Zealand, Loon had already demonstrated sustained flight and basic networking, lending credibility to what otherwise seemed like pure science fiction [[1]](https://www.wired.com/2013/08/googlex-project-loon/).

What made this vision compelling was timing. In 2011, global internet penetration stood at approximately 30 percent; Loon's architects focused on communities where conventional connectivity was difficult or expensive to deploy. This technological and business case seemed airtight: as terrestrial infrastructure reached saturation in wealthy markets, the stratosphere represented an unclaimed frontier where Alphabet could simultaneously achieve humanitarian impact and build a profitable business. The path to dominance appeared clear - be first at scale in the stratosphere, establish partnerships with local carriers who would control last-mile access, and operate a supporting backbone of floating connectivity.

The error in this reasoning would not become apparent for nearly a decade, and only then through operational evidence rather than theoretical analysis.

## Technical Architecture: Extraordinary Innovation in Service of Flawed Economics

Loon's technical achievements were undeniably remarkable and, in several respects, remain unmatched in the industry. The project solved multiple hard problems that no existing technology had tackled at commercial scale.

The core platform was a **superpressure balloon** constructed from multiple layers of polyethylene film, roughly tennis-court-sized when fully inflated [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). Unlike conventional weather balloons that expand as they ascend into lower atmospheric pressure, superpressure balloons limit changes in volume while operating at pressure above the surrounding air, a design choice that solved a critical problem: preventing the balloon from expanding until it burst in the stratosphere and supporting long-duration flight; an onboard parachute enabled controlled descent and landing. The dual-chamber altitude control system, with an internal ballonet, allowed Loon operators to fine-tune altitude in response to wind conditions - descending to catch slower or differently-directed winds and ascending to avoid storms or ride favorable jet streams [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC).

Each balloon carried a **solar power array** to supply electricity, with lithium-ion battery storage allowing operation through night hours [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). This power budget was extremely tight; it supplied the communications equipment, avionics, and other onboard systems. The engineering required to operate a base station in the stratosphere - where temperatures dip to negative 60 degrees Celsius and atmospheric pressure is far below sea-level pressure - was substantial and underestimated in early timeline projections.

The communications payload was a full **LTE base station (eNodeB)** capable of broadcasting 4G connectivity to ground-based users with standard smartphones [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). Loon balloons achieved download speeds of 18.9 Mbps and upload speeds of 4.74 Mbps with latency of 19 milliseconds - not fast by developed-world standards but comparable to early 4G networks and far superior to the satellite alternatives (which offered 500+ millisecond latency) available in remote areas [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). This was accomplished using patch antennas designed for the stratospheric environment, a technical accomplishment requiring years of iteration.

The **inter-balloon network** was one of Loon's important achievements. The team experimented with free-space optical communication, including a laser link between balloons about 100 kilometers apart. That experiment should not be confused with a demonstrated 100 Gbps production network. In 2018, Loon described a nearly 1,000-kilometer connection relayed across seven balloons using custom antennas, and a separate point-to-point link exceeding 600 kilometers. The network extended the reach of a terrestrial backhaul connection while intermediate balloons could also serve users below [[4]](https://x.company/blog/posts/1-connection-7-balloons-1-000-kilometers/).

**Autonomous navigation** was the technical capstone. Each balloon operated independently, equipped with sensors and running machine learning algorithms that analyzed real-time weather data from NOAA, historical wind patterns, and network topology to autonomously maintain position or deliberately navigate to areas where connectivity gaps had formed [[5]](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-project-loon-built-the-navigation-system-that-kept-its-balloons-over-puerto-rico). Sal Candido, Loon's Chief Technology Officer and Principal Engineer, described this aspect simply: wind management was "the hardest problem" [[5]](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-project-loon-built-the-navigation-system-that-kept-its-balloons-over-puerto-rico). The system had to predict wind vectors hours in advance, calculate trajectories through multiple altitude layers, and execute coordinated maneuvers across dozens of balloons simultaneously - all without human intervention once deployed.

The **flight duration** achieved was extraordinary. Loon balloons routinely stayed aloft for 3-6 months; the record was balloon HBAL703, which remained in continuous flight for **312 days** from May 2019 through March 2020 [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). This far exceeded early flight durations and demonstrated technical endurance, not commercial sustainability.

Coverage per balloon reached approximately **11,000 square kilometers**, equivalent to the theoretical footprint of roughly 200 ground-based cell towers [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). This ratio suggested that the sky-based approach could theoretically reduce deployment capital by orders of magnitude compared to terrestrial infrastructure.

By 2019, Loon had accumulated **1 million flight hours** across its entire fleet [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC), a figure that represented not just engineering maturity but extensive operational experience with real-world conditions, equipment failures, regulatory compliance, and logistical complexity.

Yet all of this technical virtuosity - the superpressure design, the laser inter-balloon links, the autonomous navigation, the extended flight durations - became irrelevant the moment the economic question was asked: could this system be built and operated cheaply enough to serve populations with minimal ability to pay? The answer, after a decade, was definitively no.

## People, Organization, and Strategic Pivots

Loon's organizational journey reflected the tension between technological ambition and business viability. The project began under **Rich DeVaul**, an MIT-trained researcher who championed the initial balloon concept and served as project lead through the early announcement period [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC).

**Mike Cassidy**, the project's leader at the 2013 public announcement, brought aerospace credibility to the initiative. Cassidy, a former director of Google search, had pursued aerospace training and private pilot certification, positioning him to bridge the gap between Google's software engineering culture and the hardware-intensive realities of stratospheric systems [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC).

**Tom Moore**, a veteran from WildBlue Communications (a satellite internet company), took a brief interim leadership role from August 2016 through March 2017 before Westgarth took over [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). The leadership change alone does not establish the reasons for that transition.

**Alastair Westgarth** assumed the CEO role in March 2017 and remained in charge through the shutdown announcement in January 2021 [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). Westgarth brought 30+ years of telecommunications experience, having previously served as CEO of Quintel Solutions, VP of Nortel Networks, and director of Bell Canada's wireless division [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). In interviews and public statements, Westgarth would later emphasize the importance of strategic partnerships in bringing Loon's technology to market, forging alliances with major telco leaders and aviation authorities [[6]](https://www.linkedin.com/in/alastair-westgarth/). His LinkedIn profile noted his role in recognizing and working with partners like Nokia, a critical acknowledgment of Loon's dependence on external cooperation for regulatory approval and spectrum access [[7]](https://www.facebook.com/nokia/posts/a-warm-thank-you-to-loons-ceo-alastair-westgarth-for-recognizing-nokias-commitme/10156269092552397/). After Loon's shutdown, Westgarth moved to Starship Technologies as Chief Executive Officer, applying his experience in autonomous systems to autonomous delivery robotics [[8]](https://retailtechinnovationhub.com/home/2021/6/2/alastair-westgarth-former-ceo-of-alphabets-loon-joins-starship-technologies).

**Astro Teller** (Eric Teller), the Captain of Moonshots and CEO of X, served as strategic sponsor and board chair [[2]](https://x.company/projects/loon/) [[9]](https://www.hertzfoundation.org/people/astro-teller/). Teller, a computer scientist with expertise in intelligent systems, held formal authority over Loon's continued funding and strategic direction within Alphabet. His confidence in the project's viability appears to have deteriorated substantially in Loon's final years. At the announcement of shutdown, Teller stated: "Sadly, despite the team's groundbreaking technical achievements over the last nine years, the road to commercial viability has proven much longer and riskier than hoped" [[10]](https://spectrum.ieee.org/why-did-alphabets-loon-fail-to-bring-internet-to-the-world) [[11]](https://www.businessinsider.com/alphabet-google-internet-balloon-startup-loon-2021-1?op=1&amp=&scrolla=5eb6d68b7fedc32c19ef33b4). This framing - emphasizing technical success while acknowledging strategic failure - became the official Alphabet narrative around the project's termination.

**Sal Candido**, Loon's Chief Technology Officer and Principal Engineer, led the navigation systems group and remained a key technical voice through the shutdown [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). Candido's identification of wind management as the "hardest problem" suggested that even as late as the operational phase, the project was still discovering fundamental challenges in moving from theoretical capability to reliable, repeatable field operations [[5]](https://spectrum.ieee.org/how-project-loon-built-the-navigation-system-that-kept-its-balloons-over-puerto-rico).

The organizational journey itself was consequential. Loon spent **seven years (2011-2018) as a project within Google X** [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC), a structure that provided research freedom and access to Google's deep pockets but kept Loon in "advanced research" rather than "business building" mindset. The project's graduation to independent Alphabet subsidiary status on July 11, 2018 - alongside the Wing drone project [[12]](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/07/11/alphabet-projects-wing-and-loon-spin-out-into-separate-companies.html) [[13]](https://techcrunch.com/2018/07/12/project-loon-and-project-wing-graduate-from-google-x/) - represented a formal acknowledgment that Loon had completed its R&D phase and needed to prove commercial viability [[14]](https://www.wired.com/story/alphabet-google-x-innovation-loon-wing-graduation/). This transition was, in retrospect, the point at which Loon's trajectory became unsustainable. As an independent subsidiary, Loon had to justify its existence through revenue and profitability projections. As an internal research project, it could claim progress through technical milestones and partnerships.

The recruitment of **SoftBank's HAPSMobile** as a strategic partner and investor on April 25, 2019, for **\$125 million** was a critical inflection point [[15]](https://techcrunch.com/2019/04/24/alphabet-spinout-loon-grabs-125m-from-softbank/) [[16]](https://apnews.com/Business%20Wire/ff5aa6773eaa4169a6a5abad6e24068b) [[17]](https://www.forbes.com/sites/jilliandonfro/2019/04/25/alphabet-unit-loon-raises-money-from-softbank-subsidiary/). The investment came with a formal partnership agreement to co-develop high-altitude platform systems, jointly standardize technology, and collaborate on regulatory strategy across multiple jurisdictions [[16]](https://apnews.com/Business%20Wire/ff5aa6773eaa4169a6a5abad6e24068b). The investment supported Loon's commercial ambitions, but its public announcement did not disclose an exact remaining cash runway. By 2019, after nearly a decade of development and demonstration, Loon still required external capital to continue operations. The public investment announcement does not establish when that capital was spent or how many months of runway it supplied.

The formation of the **HAPS Alliance** in February 2020 represented the final organizational pivot [[18]](https://techcrunch.com/2020/02/21/loon-and-softbanks-hapsmobile-team-with-airbus-china-telecom-and-more-on-stratospheric-cell-networks/) [[19]](https://www.silicon.co.uk/mobility/5g/expansion-for-haps-alliance-331911). The alliance brought together Loon, HAPSMobile, Airbus Defence & Space, Bharti Airtel, China Telecom, Deutsche Telekom, Ericsson, Intelsat, Nokia, Telefónica, AT&T, and T-Mobile to standardize high-altitude platform systems and coordinate regulatory advocacy [[20]](https://www.aviationtoday.com/2020/02/27/airbus-intelsat-join-tech-companies-form-haps-alliance/) [[21]](https://www.engadget.com/2020-02-22-alphabet-loon-haps-alliance.html) [[18]](https://techcrunch.com/2020/02/21/loon-and-softbanks-hapsmobile-team-with-airbus-china-telecom-and-more-on-stratospheric-cell-networks/) [[22]](https://www.hapsmobile.com/en/news/press/2020/20201008_02/) [[19]](https://www.silicon.co.uk/mobility/5g/expansion-for-haps-alliance-331911). This represented a shift from Loon's earlier position as a would-be monopoly player to a participant in an ecosystem where competing technologies and vendors would coexist. The strategic implication was significant: Loon was hedging its bets by standardizing the technology to increase the total addressable market, even if it meant reducing its relative competitive advantage.

## Emergency Deployments: Technical Success, Strategic Limitations

Between 2017 and 2019, Loon undertook a series of emergency deployments in response to natural disasters. These operations demonstrated the technology's capability and, simultaneously, revealed the structural barriers to sustainable business.

**Peru Flooding (2017)**: Loon's first significant real-world deployment came in response to flooding in Peru, where the company leveraged a pre-existing partnership with Telefónica to rapidly position balloons over affected regions [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC).

**Puerto Rico and Hurricane Maria (September-November 2017)**: Following Hurricane Maria's landfall in Puerto Rico on September 20, 2017 [[23]](https://cimss.ssec.wisc.edu/satellite-blog/archives/25338), Loon requested and received FCC approval on October 7, 2017, to deploy internet balloons as disaster relief infrastructure [[24]](https://www.cnet.com/tech/mobile/hurricane-puerto-rico-balloons-project-loon-google-alphabet/). The deployment was substantial: approximately 30 balloons were positioned over the island, with Loon partnering with AT&T, SES Networks, and Liberty Cablevision to provide ground infrastructure connectivity [[25]](https://www.theverge.com/2017/10/20/16512178/alphabet-project-loon-puerto-rico-lte-balloons-disaster-relief-connectivity) [[26]](https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-41721360). The Puerto Rico deployment demonstrated both the system's utility and its limitations. Loon provided emergency connectivity to approximately **100,000 people** [[27]](https://www.firstpost.com/tech/news-analysis/project-loon-has-delivered-internet-services-to-100000-people-in-hurricane-maria-hit-puerto-rico-4202845.html) through November 2017, supporting people with compatible LTE-enabled phones through AT&T and T-Mobile [[28]](https://x.company/blog/posts/turning-on-project-loon-in-puerto-rico/) [[27]](https://www.firstpost.com/tech/news-analysis/project-loon-has-delivered-internet-services-to-100000-people-in-hurricane-maria-hit-puerto-rico-4202845.html). However, the operation also revealed a constraint that would haunt Loon throughout its existence: the deployment was inherently temporary, with service constrained by the operating conditions of that emergency deployment; the balloon design itself used solar panels to charge batteries for nighttime operation [[2]](https://x.company/projects/loon/), and required pre-positioned ground partnerships to operate. When the emergency ended and normal infrastructure was restored, Loon's temporary balloons became obsolete. The Puerto Rico operation proved that Loon could provide emergency relief - a valuable but non-recurring business case.

**Peru Earthquake (May 26, 2019, magnitude 8.0)**: Loon's most rapid deployment came in response to an 8.0 magnitude earthquake in remote Peru on May 26, 2019. Loon positioned internet balloons over the affected region within **48 hours**, serving approximately **20,000 people** in areas where terrestrial infrastructure had been damaged [[29]](https://9to5google.com/2019/05/29/loon-balloon-peru-earthquake/) [[30]](https://www.news18.com/news/tech/alphabets-loon-balloons-were-beaming-lte-in-peru-within-48-hours-of-the-earthquake-2164765.html) [[31]](https://www.engadget.com/2019-05-29-alphabet-loon-internet-balloons-peru-earthquake.html) [[32]](https://www.popularmechanics.com/technology/infrastructure/a27699512/balloon-emergency-lte-peru-earthquake/) [[33]](https://www.gadgets360.com/internet/news/alphabet-google-loon-internet-access-to-peru-earthquake-victims-2046331) [[34]](https://www.cnet.com/news/alphabets-loon-is-providing-internet-access-to-peru-earthquake-victims/). The partnership with CenturyLink provided critical backhaul connectivity (via E-band spectrum), demonstrating Loon's ability to work with terrestrial carriers for last-mile operations [[35]](https://www.multivu.com/players/English/8524353-centurylink-and-loon-collaborate-to-provide-internet-access-in-peru/) [[36]](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/centurylink-collaborates-with-alphabets-loon-to-provide-internet-access-in-earthquake-affected-areas-in-peru-300884129.html). The speed of deployment - 48 hours from earthquake to service - was operationally impressive and provided a compelling use case: disaster relief connectivity where temporary infrastructure could justify the cost.

These three deployments established a critical pattern: **Loon excelled at emergency response but could not sustain commercial viability in normal circumstances**. In disaster scenarios, expensive temporary infrastructure was justified because the alternative was no connectivity. In normal markets, the same infrastructure competed against established (if limited) cellular networks that had lower operating costs and predictable revenue streams.

The cumulative operational experience from these deployments - over 1 million flight hours by July 2019 - provided extensive proof of concept. The technology worked. Loon could be deployed rapidly, could sustain operations for months, and could provide meaningful connectivity to previously unserved populations. What the deployments failed to demonstrate was any path to sustainable revenue.

## The Commercial Gamble: Kenya Launch and Immediate Collapse

In July 2020, Loon took its first and only step toward sustained commercial operations by launching service in Kenya in partnership with **Telkom Kenya**, the national carrier [[37]](https://techcrunch.com/2020/07/07/alphabets-loon-launches-its-balloon-powered-kenyan-internet-service/) [[38]](https://techcrunch.com/2020/04/21/alphabets-loon-deploys-internet-connectivity-balloons-to-kenya-for-first-commercial-service-launch/). The deployment was strategically positioned in western and central Kenya, covering approximately **50,000 square kilometers** across regions including Iten, Eldoret, Baringo, Nakuru, Kakamega, Kisumu, Kisii, Bomet, Kericho, Narok, and areas near Nairobi [[39]](https://www.techexplorist.com/alphabet-loon-commercial-balloon-based-internet-service-kenya/33592/) [[40]](https://www.theverge.com/2020/4/22/21231205/alphabet-loon-internet-balloons-commercial-launch-kenya).

The commercial service was supported by approximately **35 balloons in rotation**, intended to improve availability as the fleet grew, rather than guaranteeing uninterrupted coverage [[39]](https://www.techexplorist.com/alphabet-loon-commercial-balloon-based-internet-service-kenya/33592/) [[41]](https://www.emergingtechbrew.com/stories/2020/07/08/alphabets-loon-launches-commercial-service-kenya). Performance metrics were competitive by regional standards: 18.9 Mbps download, 4.74 Mbps upload, and 19 millisecond latency in one late-June field test. These were test results, not guaranteed speeds or uptime [[42]](https://x.company/blog/posts/loon-is-live-in-kenya/). Early testing had reached over **35,000 unique users** in early 2020, providing a substantial sample of actual demand [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC).

Kenya represented a deliberate market choice. The country had achieved mobile penetration but lagged in broadband access for rural areas. The Kenyan government prioritized infrastructure development and offered accelerated approval and regulatory support, including **COVID-19 communication needs** as a strategic justification for rapid deployment [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). From a regulatory standpoint, Kenya was far simpler than alternatives like Indonesia (where surveillance concerns delayed approval) or Sri Lanka (where spectrum allocation issues created obstacles) [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC).

Yet the commercial launch, announced with considerable optimism in July 2020, lasted fewer than eight months. Operations were discontinued on **March 1, 2021** [[43]](https://www.agenceecofin.com/internet/0803-85936-alphabet-fait-atterrir-ses-ballons-internet-du-kenya-suite-a-l-arret-du-projet-loon), less than 240 days after launch [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loon_LLC). No public explanation was provided regarding specific revenue figures, churn rates, or customer acquisition costs. The official statement simply noted that operations were not "commercially viable," a characterization that masked a fundamental economic reality.

## Why Loon Failed: The Commercial Economics Gap

The shutdown announcement on **January 21, 2021**, came with statements explaining that the team had not found a sufficiently low-cost, sustainable commercial model:

Loon's leadership said that despite willing partners and technical progress, the team had not brought costs low enough to build a sustainable business. X's retrospective similarly describes the path to commercial viability as longer and riskier than hoped [[2]](https://x.company/projects/loon/).

This explanation identifies the commercial problem the team had not solved; it does not prove that high-altitude connectivity could never become viable under different technologies or market conditions.

**The Cost Problem:**

The cited sources do not provide a sufficiently documented per-balloon manufacturing cost. The superpressure design, while elegant, required precision engineering, specialized materials, and limited production capacity. Manufacturing scale was constrained - Loon could not significantly reduce per-unit costs without massive production volume, which required demand that could not be demonstrated.

Balloons had finite service lives and required replacement, although the cited evidence does not establish a uniform five-month replacement schedule. While the 312-day flight duration record demonstrated technical capability, the system required continuous rotation of balloons to maintain continuous coverage. A network of 35 balloons to serve Kenya meant acquiring replacement balloons on a continuous basis - with manufacturing, launch, recovery and replacement costs to be supported by service revenues.

Solar power systems, battery storage, and communications electronics all had finite lifespans subject to stratospheric degradation. Each component required capital replacement on a predictable schedule.

Ground infrastructure - launch and recovery facilities, trained personnel, storage, quality assurance - represented fixed costs that did not scale with revenue.

**The Revenue Problem:**

Loon targeted underserved populations, including communities least able to pay for connectivity. Affordability of both service and compatible devices was therefore relevant. In Kenya, however, the service connected existing Telkom subscribers and had already reached over 35,000 unique users during testing. The evidence does not support treating all prospective users as lacking compatible phones [[42]](https://x.company/blog/posts/loon-is-live-in-kenya/).

Device affordability, connectivity prices and the costs borne by operators all affected commercial viability. Loon did not publish enough Kenya subscriber, pricing and cost data in the cited sources to establish a defensible break-even ARPU. Specific estimates of wholesale cost or required customer revenue should therefore not be presented as measured results.

The **changing connectivity gap** was consequential. ITU estimated that 4.9 billion people, or 63 percent of the global population, used the internet in 2021, leaving 2.9 billion offline. This was not 93 percent global internet adoption. An estimated 96 percent of those remaining offline lived in developing countries. Internet use, access to network coverage and willingness or ability to pay are different measures, so these figures alone do not quantify Loon's addressable market [[44]](https://www.itu.int/itu-d/reports/statistics/2021/11/15/internet-use/).

**The Capital Constraint:**

By 2019, Loon had spent years raising capital, investing in R&D, and pursuing partnerships. The SoftBank investment of \$125 million represented confidence in the business model, but the company continued to need a sustainable financing and operating model. Loon entered 2021 actively seeking a second funding round; this does not, by itself, establish that all prior capital had been exhausted. No acquirer stepped forward; no investor committed additional capital at a valuation that Alphabet would accept.

Most critically, **Astro Teller's confidence in profitability had collapsed**. According to Teller's post-shutdown commentary, confidence in the project "dropped dramatically in the final six months" before the January 2021 announcement [[45]](https://www.wired.com/story/plaintext-alphabet-pops-loons-balloons/) [[10]](https://spectrum.ieee.org/why-did-alphabets-loon-fail-to-bring-internet-to-the-world). Within Alphabet's leadership, "no one wanted to pick up the mantle" to absorb Loon's ongoing losses and subsidize its operations in pursuit of speculative future returns [[10]](https://spectrum.ieee.org/why-did-alphabets-loon-fail-to-bring-internet-to-the-world).

Alphabet's decision to stop funding Loon is evidence of its assessment of the risks and economics at the time. It is not proof of an unavoidable physical or commercial impossibility. X's own retrospective emphasizes the length and risk of the path to commercial viability, while preserving the technical work for future researchers [[2]](https://x.company/projects/loon/).

## Shutdown, Transition, and Organizational Wind-Down

The shutdown announcement on January 21-22, 2021, was rapid and clean. Westgarth and Teller presented the decision as having been carefully considered, but Loon's employees were given minimal notice. The formal wind-down timeline extended across 2021, with Kenya operations concluding by March 1, 2021 [[43]](https://www.agenceecofin.com/internet/0803-85936-alphabet-fait-atterrir-ses-ballons-internet-du-kenya-suite-a-l-arret-du-projet-loon).

**Employee Transition:**

A layoff notice reported in May 2021 listed **July 2, 2021** as the effective date for layoffs affecting **151 workers** at Loon LLC [[46]](https://www.mercurynews.com/2021/05/28/alphabet-moonshot-loon-jolted-layoff-employee-job-tech-google-edd/) [[47]](https://www.siliconvalley.com/2021/05/28/alphabet-moonshot-loon-jolted-layoff-employee-job-tech-google-edd/). This represented the majority of Loon's workforce; the company had employed fewer than 200 people at its peak, concentrated in Mountain View at **100 Mayfield Avenue** (shared office space with Google X) and **1600 Amphitheatre Parkway** (Google's main campus location) [[48]](https://www.linkedin.com/company/loonforall) [[49]](https://contactout.com/company/loon-7875146) [[50]](https://craft.co/loon/locations) [[51]](https://opengovus.com/colorado-business/20181559175) [[52]](https://craft.co/loon).

However, Alphabet offered redeployment opportunities within the broader organization. According to reports, "many" Loon employees successfully transferred to other Alphabet projects [[46]](https://www.mercurynews.com/2021/05/28/alphabet-moonshot-loon-jolted-layoff-employee-job-tech-google-edd/) [[47]](https://www.siliconvalley.com/2021/05/28/alphabet-moonshot-loon-jolted-layoff-employee-job-tech-google-edd/). Some moved to Project Taara (the successor free-space optical project); others moved to X moonshot initiatives; still others transferred to Google and other Alphabet operating units. The redeployment program mitigated the headline damage of the shutdown, allowing Alphabet to preserve institutional knowledge and demonstrate care for displaced staff.

Formal operations wind-down extended through the second half of 2021. Telkom Kenya completed operations on March 1, 2021, with Loon providing orderly transition support [[43]](https://www.agenceecofin.com/internet/0803-85936-alphabet-fait-atterrir-ses-ballons-internet-du-kenya-suite-a-l-arret-du-projet-loon).

## What Survived: Project Taara and the Optical Legacy

While Loon as a commercial entity ceased to exist, the technical knowledge and innovations developed during its ten-year lifespan were not discarded. The most significant survivor was **Project Taara**, an evolution of the free-space optical communication technology that Loon had pioneered for inter-balloon links.

**The Pivot from Sky to Ground:**

The core insight driving Taara was elegant: if Loon could transmit data via laser beams between balloons 20+ kilometers apart in the stratosphere, could the same technology be adapted to transmit between ground points across shorter distances? This reframing - from vertical "last-mile" connectivity (balloon to ground user) to horizontal "middle-mile" connectivity (ground to ground) - solved several problems simultaneously.

Ground-based optical terminals do not require solar power cycling (they have reliable grid or battery power), do not suffer stratospheric degradation, can be maintained by technicians on the ground, and can be deployed far more cheaply than balloon-based systems. The technology's bandwidth (20+ Gbps) and latency characteristics remained exceptional, making it suitable for backhaul connectivity in regions where fiber infrastructure was absent.

**Leadership and Development:**

The Taara project was led by **Mahesh Krishnaswamy**, who had been a Loon engineer and departed the project in 2016 to pursue optical communication concepts [[53]](https://techblog.comsoc.org/2025/05/06/google-x-spin-out-taara-and-digicomm-international-partner-to-offer-high-speed-wireless-communications/) [[54]](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/telecom/from-project-loon-to-laser-internet-alphabet-spin-off-taara-eyes-deeper-india-telco-partnerships-13851617.html). Krishnaswamy's decision to leave Loon before its formal shutdown reflected his recognition that the balloon platform, while technically impressive, was not the most promising application of the optical communication technology the team had developed [[54]](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/telecom/from-project-loon-to-laser-internet-alphabet-spin-off-taara-eyes-deeper-india-telco-partnerships-13851617.html).

Taara emerged from X as a formal project and was spun out as an independent company on **March 17, 2025** [[54]](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/telecom/from-project-loon-to-laser-internet-alphabet-spin-off-taara-eyes-deeper-india-telco-partnerships-13851617.html). As of September 2026, Taara operates with fewer than 100 employees but has deployed in 13+ countries across Australia, Kenya, Fiji, and regions across Africa, Europe, South Asia, Latin America, and the United States [[55]](https://thegeekinsights.com/laser-internet-by-taara/) [[53]](https://techblog.comsoc.org/2025/05/06/google-x-spin-out-taara-and-digicomm-international-partner-to-offer-high-speed-wireless-communications/) [[54]](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/telecom/from-project-loon-to-laser-internet-alphabet-spin-off-taara-eyes-deeper-india-telco-partnerships-13851617.html).

**Technical Specifications:**

The Taara **Lightbridge** terminal is a compact device weighing approximately 14 kilograms, roughly the size of a traffic light, equipped with precision optics and laser transmitters [[56]](https://www.wired.com/story/plaintext-google-taara-chip-internet-by-light/). The system operates at **20 Gbps bidirectional** over distances up to 20 kilometers in clear-weather conditions, with performance degradation during rain or heavy fog but reliability comparable to microwave backhaul systems [[57]](https://newatlas.com/telecommunications/project-taara-internet-laser/).

Performance testing was extensive. In September 2021, Taara transmitted approximately **700 terabytes of data** across a 4.8-kilometer link between Brazzaville and Kinshasa across the Congo River, achieving 99.9 percent availability over a 20-day test period [[58]](https://www.theverge.com/2021/9/16/22677015/project-taara-fsoc-wireless-internet-kinshasa-congo-fiber) [[57]](https://newatlas.com/telecommunications/project-taara-internet-laser/). This deployment was significant because it demonstrated the technology's capability in challenging equatorial environments with high humidity and tropical weather patterns - precisely the conditions where Loon's balloons would have operated.

**Deployments and Partnerships:**

Taara also deployed in India, working with **Bharti Airtel**, a major Indian telecom operator [[59]](https://www.newsbytesapp.com/news/science/alphabet-airtel-to-use-laser-technology-for-internet/story) [[54]](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/telecom/from-project-loon-to-laser-internet-alphabet-spin-off-taara-eyes-deeper-india-telco-partnerships-13851617.html). The partnership addressed India's "last-mile problem" in rural regions where fiber deployment was economically unviable but where Airtel needed backhaul capacity to extend 4G/5G coverage. Subsequent deployments extended to multiple African countries, demonstrating market viability in the regions where Loon had struggled [[54]](https://www.moneycontrol.com/news/telecom/from-project-loon-to-laser-internet-alphabet-spin-off-taara-eyes-deeper-india-telco-partnerships-13851617.html).

**Next-Generation Innovation:**

Taara is developing a next-generation **silicon photonic chip** prototype, currently in testing, that integrates the optical transmission and reception systems into a fingernail-sized chip [[56]](https://www.wired.com/story/plaintext-google-taara-chip-internet-by-light/). This chip-scale design, achieving 10 Gbps at 1-kilometer range in prototype form, promises to reduce cost and size dramatically. Commercial availability is expected around 2026 [[56]](https://www.wired.com/story/plaintext-google-taara-chip-internet-by-light/).

**Patents and IP:**

The optical communication technology developed by Loon and advanced by Taara is protected through patent filings assigned to **Taara Connect, Inc.** (incorporated in Sunnyvale, California). Recent patent publications include:

- **US Patent 12,541,099** (published February 3, 2026): Optical phased array with grating structure and Fresnel lens [[60]](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12541099)
- **US Patent 12,519,542** (published January 6, 2026): Feedforward motion compensation for FSOC terminals [[61]](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12519542)
- **US Patent 12,470,292** (published November 11, 2025): Feedforward motion compensation for FSOC terminals [[62]](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12470292)

The filing dates and continuations trace back to applications filed in March 2022 (about fourteen months after the January 2021 shutdown announcement), but those filing dates alone do not establish the timing or motivation of a corporate technology transfer [[60]](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12541099) [[61]](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12519542) [[62]](https://patents.google.com/patent/US12470292).

Additionally, **AT&T Intellectual Property II, L.P.** is identified as the assignee of a separate patent on optical and radio local-access network technology (US Patent 10,122,446). Related subject matter alone is not evidence that this patent originated at Loon or was transferred from it [[63]](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10122446).

**Business Model and Viability:**

Taara operates with what appears to be a more sustainable business model than Loon. Taara works with telecommunications operators and infrastructure partners, as Loon also did in Kenya, but supplies ground-based optical connectivity rather than balloon-based coverage, selling or leasing optical terminals to telecom operators and infrastructure companies. The addressable market - telecom operators needing backhaul capacity - is far larger and more sophisticated in terms of ability-to-pay than a model depending solely on retail sales to individual users; Loon itself partnered with mobile-network operators.

Taara became an **independent company** on March 17, 2025, with a funding round led by Series X Capital. It should not be described as remaining an Alphabet subsidiary. The announcement documents deployed links and commercial partnerships, but does not establish audited profitability or loss figures for 2026 [[64]](https://x.company/blog/posts/taara-graduation/).

## The HAPS Alliance: Standardization and Ecosystem Evolution

The **High-Altitude Platform System (HAPS) Alliance**, co-founded by Loon and HAPSMobile in February 2020, survived Loon's shutdown and continues as an industry advocacy and standardization organization [[20]](https://www.aviationtoday.com/2020/02/27/airbus-intelsat-join-tech-companies-form-haps-alliance/) [[21]](https://www.engadget.com/2020-02-22-alphabet-loon-haps-alliance.html) [[18]](https://techcrunch.com/2020/02/21/loon-and-softbanks-hapsmobile-team-with-airbus-china-telecom-and-more-on-stratospheric-cell-networks/) [[22]](https://www.hapsmobile.com/en/news/press/2020/20201008_02/) [[19]](https://www.silicon.co.uk/mobility/5g/expansion-for-haps-alliance-331911) [[65]](https://interactive.satellitetoday.com/haps-a-satellite-operators-big-investment-in-the-stratosphere/).

The alliance's core membership includes **SoftBank/HAPSMobile** (continuing development of stratospheric gliders and autonomous aircraft for high-altitude telecommunications), **Airbus Defence & Space** (designing and manufacturing aircraft platforms), **Bharti Airtel** (Indian operator, supporting spectrum advocacy in South Asia), **China Telecom** (pursuing HAPS strategies in Asia), **Deutsche Telekom** (European regulatory advocacy), **Ericsson** (equipment vendor), **Intelsat** (satellite operator, potentially threatened by HAPS but participating in standardization), **Nokia** (equipment vendor), **Telefónica** (European operator), **AT&T** and **T-Mobile** (U.S. operators, supporting spectrum and regulatory strategy) [[20]](https://www.aviationtoday.com/2020/02/27/airbus-intelsat-join-tech-companies-form-haps-alliance/) [[21]](https://www.engadget.com/2020-02-22-alphabet-loon-haps-alliance.html) [[18]](https://techcrunch.com/2020/02/21/loon-and-softbanks-hapsmobile-team-with-airbus-china-telecom-and-more-on-stratospheric-cell-networks/) [[19]](https://www.silicon.co.uk/mobility/5g/expansion-for-haps-alliance-331911) [[65]](https://interactive.satellitetoday.com/haps-a-satellite-operators-big-investment-in-the-stratosphere/).

The alliance's primary objectives are to:
- Establish common technical standards for HAPS systems
- Coordinate spectrum advocacy across jurisdictions
- Develop interoperability frameworks
- Support regulatory harmonization globally

The alliance's formation in 2020 represented a strategic shift from Loon's original position as a would-be monopoly player to an acknowledgment that multiple HAPS technologies would coexist - stratospheric balloons, high-altitude gliders, fixed-wing autonomous aircraft, potential dirigibles - each with different economics, capabilities, and regulatory requirements.

## Lessons: Technical Success and Business Model Failure

Project Loon's shutdown should be understood not as a technological failure but as a failure of business model assumptions. The technical achievements were genuine and substantial - a decade of sustained innovation in superpressure balloon design, autonomous navigation, free-space optical communications, and stratospheric operations. X's technical archive documents those achievements without establishing a blanket claim that competitors have not solved comparable problems.

The failure was economic and structural: the project's architects believed that technological capability would create a market, but the target market (remote, underserved populations with minimal ability-to-pay) could not sustain the cost structure required to operate the technology. The available evidence does not quantify how a hypothetical 50 percent cost reduction would have changed unit economics, or establish that further optimization could never work. Similarly, the market was shrinking (global internet penetration rising), concentrating the remaining unconnected population in lower-income regions with worse ability-to-pay.

Loon's trajectory from 2011 through 2021 demonstrates the limits of "moonshot" thinking when applied to problems with strong economic constraints. The project succeeded brilliantly within its technical scope but failed absolutely in its commercial mission. Whether a different engineering approach, cost structure or market could support a successor remains a separate question.

What survived - Taara's free-space optical technology, applied to ground infrastructure rather than balloons - suggests that the underlying innovation was sound; it simply required a different application and business model to achieve viability. This distinction - between the failure of Loon and the potential viability of Taara - clarifies that Loon's ending was not the end of the work, but rather a necessary redirection toward problems with more sustainable economics.
