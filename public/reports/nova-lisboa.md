# Nova Lisboa

**Huambo (Nova Lisboa): The Planned Colonial Capital That Never Became Capital**

In the 1920s, during Portugal's late colonial period in Angola, planners designated the city of Huambo—located in the central highlands approximately **600 kilometres southeast of Luanda**—as a planned modern capital. In 1928, the city was formally renamed **Nova Lisboa (New Lisbon)** and given the official status of colonial capital-in-design, with architectural plans modelled on Portuguese urban ideals. [[17]](https://alchetron.com/Huambo) [[18]](https://theafricareport.com/Society-and-Culture/angola-and-the-lost-powerhouse-of-the-planalto.html) [[19]](https://hpip.org/en/Contents/Place/272) [[20]](https://www.theafricareport.com/4772/angola-and-the-lost-powerhouse-of-the-planalto/) [[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huambo)

However, Nova Lisboa never functioned as the primary capital. Luanda remained the seat of colonial government and commercial power. Nova Lisboa developed instead as a secondary economic centre, but infrastructure investment lagged dramatically. Basic amenities, electricity, water systems, and road connections were not fully realised until the 1940s–1950s. [[18]](https://theafricareport.com/Society-and-Culture/angola-and-the-lost-powerhouse-of-the-planalto.html) [[20]](https://www.theafricareport.com/4772/angola-and-the-lost-powerhouse-of-the-planalto/)

Following independence in 1975, Angola entered a 27-year civil war (1975–2002). Huambo, by then renamed back to its indigenous name, was situated in territory contested by opposing military forces. The city was "very nearly obliterated as battle lines swept back and forth," with destruction so severe that by early 2000, significant reconstruction was required. [[21]](https://expatlingo.wordpress.com/2012/02/14/huambo/) By 2024, Huambo had recovered to become Angola's third-largest city with an estimated population of **710,000**, but it never realised the planned-capital vision of the 1920s. [[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huambo)

**Status of Nova Lisboa Vision: PARTIALLY REALISED URBAN DEVELOPMENT, FAILED CAPITAL PLAN**

The Nova Lisboa case illustrates a century-long pattern: ambitious urban visions encounter the friction of geography (distance, infrastructure costs), politics (competing regional and class interests), and violence (war). The planned city was built—but not to the scope or timeline envisioned, and for different purposes than originally intended.

**Master Plan Failures (1940s–1950s, 1973, Post-Independence)**

Academic research on Luanda urban governance identifies multiple master plans that were prepared but never implemented:

1. **1940s–1950s satellite city concepts**: Plans were drafted for satellite cities surrounding Luanda, intended to relieve congestion and enable planned expansion. These were never approved by colonial authorities or, if approved, were never funded. Specific project names and dates were not fully detailed in accessible sources.

2. **1973 French growth-pole plan**: A consultant study commissioned from French urbanists proposed Luanda's restructuring around "growth poles"—nodal points of concentrated development to shape metropolitan expansion. This plan was never formally adopted and had no implementation.

3. **Cuban master plan (post-1975)**: Following independence, Cuban advisers prepared a comprehensive master plan for Luanda incorporating socialist urbanism principles. The plan was implemented partially, but demographic underestimation—the city's population growth exceeded projections by orders of magnitude as rural-to-urban migration intensified during the civil war—rendered the plan's infrastructure projections obsolete within a decade. [[11]](https://www.pasgr.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/Urban-Governance-and-Turning-African-Cities-Around_Luanda-Case-Study_.pdf)

**Status: PROPOSED BUT NOT IMPLEMENTED, OR IMPLEMENTED BUT SUPERSEDED BY DEMOGRAPHIC REALITY**

These historical plans are documented in research literature but lack detailed primary documentation. Their inclusion is important to establish the longer historical arc: Luanda has experienced repeated cycles of master planning and re-planning over more than a century, with each wave of ambition encountering practical and political barriers. The pattern suggests that the 2000s boom-era development (Kilamba, Bay of Luanda, Talatona) was not exceptional but rather the latest iteration of a recurring cycle.
