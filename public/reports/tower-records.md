# Tower Records

## What Was Built and Why It Mattered

Tower Records opened its first retail location in Sacramento, California, in 1960, but by the 1980s it had expanded into a national chain, with New York City as a flagship market. The company's most celebrated location was at the intersection of **Broadway and East Fourth Street** in Greenwich Village, Manhattan, which opened in 1983 [[20]](https://www.newsday.com/news/from-the-archives-tower-records-bankruptcy-expected-q79405). This store was billed as the **"world's largest record store,"** occupying **25,000 square feet** and stocking over 100,000 vinyl records, CDs, cassettes, DVDs, and music merchandise [[18]](https://www.amny.com/news/video-killed-radio-star-ipod-killed-tower-records/) [[20]](https://www.newsday.com/news/from-the-archives-tower-records-bankruptcy-expected-q79405). Tower also operated additional locations at Lafayette and East Fourth Streets and maintained a presence on Manhattan's retail landscape for over two decades [[18]](https://www.amny.com/news/video-killed-radio-star-ipod-killed-tower-records/) [[20]](https://www.newsday.com/news/from-the-archives-tower-records-bankruptcy-expected-q79405).

Tower Records mattered because it served as the primary destination for serious music collectors, casual listeners, and anyone seeking a comprehensive inventory of music. The stores employed knowledgeable staff and created a community gathering place for music enthusiasts. In an era before streaming and downloads, Tower was the retail equivalent of a music library.

## The First Bankruptcy and Emergence (2003–2004)

The chain's troubles began in 2003. The parent company, MTS Inc., filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in May 2003 [[20]](https://www.newsday.com/news/from-the-archives-tower-records-bankruptcy-expected-q79405). Tower Records itself entered bankruptcy in February 2004 [[20]](https://www.newsday.com/news/from-the-archives-tower-records-bankruptcy-expected-q79405). The immediate cause was a combination of factors: declining compact disc sales, the rise of big-box retailers like Best Buy, Walmart, and Virgin Megastore that undercut prices, competition from online sellers (Amazon), and the legal and illegal proliferation of digital music downloads [[22]](https://www.kpbs.org/news/2006/10/24/tower-records-closing-sounds-death-knell-for) [[25]](https://www.the-independent.com/arts-entertainment/music/news/the-fall-of-tower-records-increases-fears-for-fate-of-british-record-shops-68635.html). Between 2000 and 2005, retail music sales had declined by approximately 17% [[22]](https://www.kpbs.org/news/2006/10/24/tower-records-closing-sounds-death-knell-for).

The company attempted a restructuring while in bankruptcy. Bondholders acquired approximately 85% of the company's stock, and Tower emerged from bankruptcy in 2004 with what management hoped would be a sustainable cost structure [[20]](https://www.newsday.com/news/from-the-archives-tower-records-bankruptcy-expected-q79405). For a brief period, it appeared the iconic chain might survive.

## The Second Collapse and Liquidation (2006)

However, the recovery was short-lived. By August 2006, Tower's financial position had deteriorated again. On **August 4, 2006**, the four major music labels—Universal, Warner, EMI, and Sony BMG—ceased shipments to Tower Records after the company notified them it would not pay outstanding invoices [[21]](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2006-aug-04-fi-tower4-story.html). This cutoff of product supply was catastrophic: a record store without new stock cannot operate.

Tower Records filed for bankruptcy a second time in August 2006. The chain was auctioned on **October 5–6, 2006**, with Great American Group acquiring the company for **\$134.3 million**, narrowly defeating Trans World Entertainment's competing bid [[23]](https://www.nytimes.com/2006/08/22/business/22tower.html) [[19]](https://commonwealthtimes.org/2006/10/23/tower-records-closes-its-doors-for-good/).

Great American Group immediately began liquidation operations. Liquidation sales commenced on **October 7, 2006**, with banners proclaiming "Everything Must Go" posted across all remaining stores [[24]](https://sundial.csun.edu/9775/archive/anotheronebitesthedusttowerrecordstocloseitsdoors/). The process was completed by December 2006 [[19]](https://commonwealthtimes.org/2006/10/23/tower-records-closes-its-doors-for-good/).

## Employment and Store Impact

The chain operated **89 to 93 stores** at the time of liquidation (sources vary on the exact count) [[18]](https://www.amny.com/news/video-killed-radio-star-ipod-killed-tower-records/) [[24]](https://sundial.csun.edu/9775/archive/anotheronebitesthedusttowerrecordstocloseitsdoors/). The closure resulted in approximately **3,000 retail employees** losing their jobs [[18]](https://www.amny.com/news/video-killed-radio-star-ipod-killed-tower-records/). The three Manhattan locations—including the iconic Broadway and East Fourth Street flagship store—all closed permanently. The Greenwich Village location, which had served as the symbol of Tower's dominance, was cleared out by late 2006.

## Modernization Attempts and Strategic Missteps

Tower Records did attempt to adapt to the digital era. By 2005–2006, the chain had installed in-store CD burning kiosks that allowed customers to create custom compilations [[21]](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2006-aug-04-fi-tower4-story.html). It also negotiated with digital music services to offer legal 99-cent downloads in its stores [[21]](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2006-aug-04-fi-tower4-story.html). However, these initiatives came far too late. Customers had already shifted their music consumption to iTunes (launched 2003), online retailers, and illegal file-sharing networks (Limewire, BitTorrent). The structural advantage of a physical record store—comprehensive inventory and knowledgeable staff—became a liability as consumers valued convenience and price over browsing experience.

## What Survived and What Did Not

Tower Records as a retail chain did not survive in the United States. All 89–93 stores were liquidated by the end of 2006. The company also operated 14 stores in the United Kingdom, which were sold to a separate buyer in 2005, but the British operation also failed to persist under the Tower Records brand [[20]](https://www.newsday.com/news/from-the-archives-tower-records-bankruptcy-expected-q79405).

The building at Broadway and East Fourth Street still stands in Greenwich Village, but it no longer houses a record store. Today it operates as retail/commercial space under different tenants. The Tower Records brand name and intellectual property were liquidated as part of the bankruptcy. No successor company adopted the Tower Records name for new retail operations. What survived was the building itself, the historical memory of the store as an iconic New York institution, and the lesson that even market-dominant retailers can be displaced by technological disruption when they fail to adapt quickly enough.
