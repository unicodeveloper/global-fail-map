# New Australia in Paraguay: The Ambition, the Schism, and the Lessons from a Failed Cooperative (1893–1909)

In September 1893, the steamship Royal Tar delivered William Lane and 220 Australian settlers to Paraguay with an uncompromising vision: to establish a socialist utopia governed by immutable law. By May 1894, Lane himself had abandoned the colony entirely, taking 58 of his most loyal followers to establish a rival settlement 72 kilometers south. Both settlements would dissolve within fifteen years, yet they left behind approximately 2,000 descendants who today carry surnames like Smith, Kennedy, and Murray in rural Paraguay, plus a documentary record in the hand-written pages of Cosme Monthly that reveals precisely why enforced collectivism fractures when individual behavior resists the rules meant to contain it.

## The Ambition: Constructing the New Australia (1891–1893)

The New Australia movement emerged from economic desperation and radical conviction. In the early 1890s, Australia faced economic crisis, industrial unrest, and what working-class activists perceived as a system rigged permanently in capital's favor. William Lane, a New Zealand-born journalist and organizer who had edited the radical *Boomerang* newspaper in Brisbane, proposed a radical alternative: migrate to Paraguay, acquire land from a government short on settlers and revenue, and establish a functioning cooperative commonwealth where the means of production belonged collectively to those who worked them. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) [[6]](https://labouraustralia.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024)

Paraguay itself offered incentive. The nation had suffered catastrophically in the Triple Alliance War (1865–1870), which killed an estimated 60 percent of its population and left the country with a severe gender imbalance - approximately 150,000 single women to 14,000 men by the 1890s. [[2]](https://www.migrationheritage.nsw.gov.au/exhibition/) The Paraguayan government, seeking repopulation and agricultural development, granted extensive land concessions to settlers willing to establish productive colonies. An Australian settlement promised labor, agriculture, and international capital investment.

Lane structured the colony according to strict principles codified as the "Code of the Medes and Persians" - immutable law that could never be amended. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) Membership required explicit conditions: settlers must be of white British descent, teetotal (no alcohol whatsoever), free from color "by marriage," demonstrating loyalty to the Labour movement, and without contagious disease. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) Labor would be credited at 100 units per adult per week, 25 per child, with all means of production - land, tools, livestock, equipment - held collectively. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) Lane appointed himself Intendant with "sole and full control over colony's finances," and enforcement of the rules proceeded via expulsion. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024)

The venture attracted significant support in Australia. Mary Gilmore, a radical schoolteacher and poet aligned with Labour activism, was "largely responsible for overcoming the financial difficulties that threatened to prevent the departure." [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) On July 16, 1893, the Royal Tar set sail with 220 colonists. The ship arrived in Asunción on September 22, 1893, and the settlement was formally established on September 28, 1893, in the Ajos region southeast of Villarrica, approximately 109 miles southeast of Asunción. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024)

## What Happened: Community, Schism, and Reorganization (1893–1897)

The first months showed tangible progress. Settlers cleared jungle by hand, constructed thatched cottages, a smithy, butcher shop, and a school. [[9]](https://www.australiangeographic.com.au/history-culture/2012/07/the-new-australians-of-south-america/) They planted orange trees and purchased several thousand cattle. [[9]](https://www.australiangeographic.com.au/history-culture/2012/07/the-new-australians-of-south-america/) Food production began with maize, sweet potatoes, beans, bananas, and oranges. A sense of collective purpose animated the earliest accounts, with settlers confident they had escaped Australian capitalism for cooperative dignity.

But tensions emerged almost immediately. Lane's enforced alcohol prohibition met resistance from unmarried men in a landscape containing 150,000 single Paraguayan women. The ban on fraternization with local women and prohibition on what Lane termed color "by marriage" collided with demographic reality and human desire. A second contingent of approximately 195 settlers arrived in early 1894, carrying expectations of democratic governance shaped by Australian labour movement traditions. When Lane refused compromise and continued to expel those who violated his code, the political fracture became irreversible.

The breaking point arrived in May 1894. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) Lane departed the colony with 58 to 63 loyalists and established Cosme, a new settlement 72 kilometers south, on fresh land granted by the Paraguayan government. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) This was not cooperative dissolution but leadership schism. Frederick Kidd, a remaining colonist, was elected to lead the approximately 217 settlers remaining at the original site. Crucially, Kidd "reorganized the colony on a business basis," abandoning communist structure. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) Settlers began operating as private farmers on collectively held land, a fundamental departure from Lane's vision.

The original settlement never recovered. By January 1897, the Paraguayan government, noting that promised settlement targets of 1,000 to 1,200 families had never materialized (only 150 to 220 had arrived), cancelled the colony's founding agreement. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) The government dissolved the cooperative, auctioned land and communal assets, and distributed proceeds to individual remaining settlers. By 1897, only 150 colonists remained at the original site. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024)

Cosme, by contrast, persisted through a more pragmatic structure. It achieved documented productive work: mechanized brick production (documented as Paraguay's first), [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) a school with 22 pupils and 78.9 percent average attendance, [[4]](https://labourhistorycanberra.org/2015/05/an-australian-newspaper-in-paraguay-1894-1904/) postal operations processing 121 letters dispatched and 79 received in the single month of July 1903, and managed livestock herds. [[4]](https://labourhistorycanberra.org/2015/05/an-australian-newspaper-in-paraguay-1894-1904/) Mary Gilmore, who arrived in January 1896 after the original settlement's fracture, worked as editor of the Cosme Evening Notes and participated in teaching and community organization. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) She married William Alexander Gilmore on May 25, 1897, and gave birth to William Dysart Cameron Gilmore on August 21, 1898, at Villarrica. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024)

The colony published Cosme Monthly from November 1894 onward - handwritten and spirit-duplicated at first, then printed in Britain from April 1897, eventually including photographs from March 1901 onward. [[4]](https://labourhistorycanberra.org/2015/05/an-australian-newspaper-in-paraguay-1894-1904/) These publications constitute the most detailed contemporary record of the settlement's practical challenges and ideological tensions.

## Why It Ended: Documented Causes and Structural Contradictions (1894–1909)

The dissolution of New Australia was not inevitable fate but the result of documented, material causes. Lane's expulsion authority proved structurally incompatible with the cooperative principles settlers expected from an Australian labour movement initiative. His "Code of Medes and Persians" assumed that immutable rules, enforced without exception, could sustain collective labor. They could not. Alcohol prohibition, which seemed straightforward as a regulative principle, became unenforceable when violation was widespread - enforcement through expulsion simply reduced labor capacity in an environment where productivity already required maximum effort. The British Embassy's contemporary assessment captured the mechanism: Lane was "remarkably deficient in the tact and human sympathy so necessary in a leader of men." [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024)

The demographic collision was equally documented and equally insoluble. Paraguay contained approximately 150,000 unmarried women to 14,000 men after the Triple Alliance War. Lane's ban on "color by marriage" and his prohibition on fraternization with Paraguayan women meant excluding the only available marriage partners for 43 unmarried men and 7 single women in the first wave. [[2]](https://www.migrationheritage.nsw.gov.au/exhibition/) When the second contingent arrived in 1894 with additional unmarried men, the contradictions multiplied. By Cosme's final period, the Committee Report in the June 1904 Cosme Monthly noted: "Our population has decreased since last May. Of the fifteen adult male full members on the colony last May, one has resigned, one was expelled and one is away on leave of absence." [[4]](https://labourhistorycanberra.org/2015/05/an-australian-newspaper-in-paraguay-1894-1904/)

Political instability in Paraguay accelerated the collapse. The González presidency, which had granted cooperative land concessions, was overthrown in the Cavalcanti Coup on June 9, 1894. [[10]](http://www.pjsymes.com.au/articles/NewAustralia.htm) Government subsidy arrangements lapsed. Paraguay issued a \$100 (approximately £100 sterling) subsidy in May 1895, paid at \$P800 (Paraguayan pesos) monthly, which enabled promissory notes circulation from May to November 1895. [[10]](http://www.pjsymes.com.au/articles/NewAustralia.htm) When the subsidy exhausted in November, so did the currency. Fewer than 30 surviving examples of these notes exist today, making them numismatic rarities. [[10]](http://www.pjsymes.com.au/articles/NewAustralia.htm)

The distinction between Lane's May 1894 departure and the formal January 1897 dissolution is critical. Lane's departure represented a factional split driven by his refusal to accept democratic governance. The 1897 dissolution represented governmental action - the Paraguayan state recognized that the promised population targets would never be met and that the cooperative structure no longer functioned. These were separate, sequential failures: leadership schism followed by institutional collapse.

Cosme persisted longer, but with declining vitality. In June 1904, Cosme Monthly's final issue carried an explicit warning to prospective settlers: "The work here is almost entirely manual...The climate [is] trying for some months in summer...The food [is] limited in variety...We are for example here for ten years and still in debt...A man easily depressed should carefully stay away from here." [[4]](https://labourhistorycanberra.org/2015/05/an-australian-newspaper-in-paraguay-1894-1904/) The Monthly ceased publication after June 1904. The cooperative was formally dissolved in 1908–1909. [[4]](https://labourhistorycanberra.org/2015/05/an-australian-newspaper-in-paraguay-1894-1904/)

## What Survived: Descendants, Villages, and Archives (1909–2026)

Approximately 2,000 descendants of the original colonists remain in Paraguay, primarily in the departments of San Pedro and Concepción. [[3]](https://grokipedia.com/page/New_Australia) They carry surnames including Woods, Burke, Smith, Kennedy, Adams, Murray, Butterworth, McKeen, and Patel. [[8]](https://www.abc.net.au/listen/programs/the-radio-national-hour/how-a-small-town-in-paraguay-became-new-australia-/106849164) The villages of Nueva Australia and Nueva Londres represent the spatial persistence of the settlement - not abandoned archaeological sites but functioning Paraguayan rural townships with approximately 4,500 residents combined. [[7]](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-26/socceroos-paraguay-neuva-australia-world-cup/106840492)

The cultural integration was profound. Spanish and Guaraní are the primary languages; English is spoken rarely, except in bilingual households. [[9]](https://www.australiangeographic.com.au/history-culture/2012/07/the-new-australians-of-south-america/) Fair skin and red hair visible in some descendants testify to genealogical continuity, but assimilation has been nearly complete. When Paraguay faced Australia in the 2026 FIFA World Cup, residents of Nueva Australia identified as Paraguayan nationals, flew Paraguayan flags, and supported the Paraguayan team with evident passion. [[7]](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-26/socceroos-paraguay-neuva-australia-world-cup/106840492)

An octogenarian descendant of Gilbert Casey, who assumed leadership after Lane's departure, told an interviewer in 2012: "We're the marginalised people of Paraguay now. We're more desperate than my grandfather was in Australia." [[9]](https://www.australiangeographic.com.au/history-culture/2012/07/the-new-australians-of-south-america/) The statement captures the arc from utopian ambition to generational integration - the settlers' descendants experienced Paraguay not as liberation but as economic marginalization, their Australian origins a genealogical fact rather than lived identity.

Archival records survive. Cosme Monthly runs are held at the National Library of Australia, Fisher Library at the University of Sydney, Mitchell Library (State Library NSW), and the State Library of South Australia. [[4]](https://labourhistorycanberra.org/2015/05/an-australian-newspaper-in-paraguay-1894-1904/) Mary Gilmore's papers reside at Mitchell Library and the National Library of Australia. [[5]](https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/lane-william-7024) Promissory notes from the 1895 currency circulation are catalogued at the State Library of New South Wales and held in private collections. The New Australia and Cosme Colony Collection at the NSW Migration Heritage Centre contains documents, personal papers, photographs, and objects relating to the colonies. [[1]](https://www.migrationheritage.nsw.gov.au/exhibition/objectsthroughtime/1893-the-new-australia-colony-collection/index.html)

## Lessons for Future Builders: Structural Contradictions and Design Principles

Several substantive lessons emerge from New Australia's documented history, applicable to any collective settlement attempting shared labor and resource governance:

**First, authoritarian leadership and cooperative ownership are structurally incompatible.** Lane's vision of collective work governed by immutable rules discovered an enduring truth: workers who voluntarily enter cooperative arrangements expect voice in governance. When voice is denied and enforcement proceeds through expulsion, the cooperative loses labor capacity precisely when it requires maximum productivity. Democratic governance processes are slower than autocratic decree, but they retain the consent of the governed. A leader who refuses compromise faces factional division, not patient obedience.

**Second, rules cannot substitute for incentive alignment.** Alcohol prohibition, sexual segregation, and racial exclusion were codified as law. Their violation was met with expulsion. Yet unmarried men and women, presented with each other in a sparsely populated region, resisted the rules. No enforcement mechanism can compel desires to disappear. Future efforts must either align rules with human behavior or design settlement selection to admit only those whose goals match the constraints. Cosme's later, more pragmatic structure - permitting private farming and marriage - worked longer precisely because it relaxed these unenforceable constraints.

**Third, external political instability can collapse internal institutions rapidly.** The González government's overthrow, the subsidy's lapse, the currency's failure - these were exogenous shocks to a community already structurally stressed. A settlement entirely dependent on a host government's continued support and goodwill is vulnerable to political upheaval. Cosme survived longer than New Australia not because governance was superior but because the Paraguayan government continued to grant land after the original cooperative failed.

**Fourth, demographic realities cannot be legislated away.** Lane's prohibition on marriage to local women was legally enacted and systematically enforced. It was also impossible. Paraguay's surplus women and shortage of men meant that realistic settlement planning required either accepting intermarriage or restricting membership to married couples exclusively. New Australia recruited unmarried men into an environment that offered no acceptable outlets for desire - a design flaw, not a moral failure on the settlers' part.

**Fifth, transparency and shared knowledge sustain community longer than mythology.** Cosme Monthly, despite its propagandistic intent, documented hardship, debt, climate difficulty, and population decline in its final years. This honest acknowledgment, the historical record shows, did not destroy community so much as prevent false expectations. Settler communities that pretend success sustains morale temporarily but collapses trust catastrophically when reality surfaces. Communities that acknowledge difficulty preserve morale among those who remain.

**Sixth, ambition itself is not the problem - structural design is.** New Australia's settlers were not naive or foolish. They were responding to documented Australian economic crisis with documented hunger for collective dignity. Their failure was not failure of ambition but failure of governance design. Cosme, operating under modified principles (private farming on collective land, marriage permitted, democratic decision-making on some matters), sustained productive work a decade longer. This suggests that the settlement model itself - Australians in Paraguay - was viable; Lane's specific implementation was not.

New Australia proves that utopian aspiration can energize real labor and that collectively owned land can produce food and shelter. It also proves that workers will not sacrifice autonomy permanently to rules they did not author. Future builders must account for this fundamental human inclination toward self-determination, not as weakness to be overcome by stricter discipline, but as a permanent feature of collective work that must be integrated into governance design from the outset.
