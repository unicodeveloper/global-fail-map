# The La Union tuna port

## Project Origins and Construction

The La Unión Tuna Port represents the clearest documented case of infrastructure abandonment in the region. Financed by France at a cost of approximately \$50 million, the facility was constructed as a modern commercial fishing port intended to process and export tuna products. The port was completed in 1981 and represented a substantial foreign investment in El Salvador's eastern maritime development during the early years of the civil war. [[1]](https://fsi9-prod.s3.us-west-1.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/el_salvador_port_case_7-25.pdf) The project also included acquisition of two large tuna seiners (fishing vessels) to generate catch for the processing facility, establishing a vertically integrated fishing operation. The geographic connection is clear: the facility was located directly in the Port of La Unión, the nation's primary eastern maritime gateway. [[5]](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/centam/sv-port-of-la-union.htm)

## Operational Failure and Causes

Despite physical completion, the tuna port never achieved commercial viability. Multiple systemic failures contributed to its abandonment as a functional enterprise:

**Government Management Collapse**: The Salvadoran government proved unable to manage the facility and fishing fleet effectively. The two tuna seiners, once deployed, generated meager catches due to operational and technical difficulties. By 1985, just four years after opening, equipment and infrastructure had begun to languish without proper maintenance or investment. [[1]](https://fsi9-prod.s3.us-west-1.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/el_salvador_port_case_7-25.pdf)

**US Trade Embargoes**: Beginning in 1986, the United States imposed trade embargoes on Salvadoran tuna products, citing violations of the Marine Mammal Protection Act (MMPA). The US embargoes recurred in 1988, effectively closing the primary export market for any tuna caught or processed at the facility. [[1]](https://fsi9-prod.s3.us-west-1.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/el_salvador_port_case_7-25.pdf) These trade restrictions, imposed in response to fishing practices that exceeded US standards for incidental dolphin mortality, eliminated the economic rationale for port operations.

**Capital Flight and Institutional Neglect**: Without revenue generation and facing political pressure from an ongoing civil conflict, government administrators abandoned operational efforts. The modern facility with cranes and processing equipment became a dormant asset, and there is no documentation of subsequent commercial use or repurposing in the 40+ years since operational collapse.

## Current Status and Physical Remnants

As of September 2026, no sources document the current physical condition of the 1981 tuna processing facility or whether the structures still stand. The port area itself has been revitalized as of 2024 under a new concession agreement with Yilport Holding Inc., which focuses on container and general cargo operations rather than fish processing. [[6]](https://www.hunton.com/news/hunton-andrews-kurth-represents-the-salvador-port-authority-in-1-6-billion-port-development) Whether the original tuna port buildings survive as storage, offices, or other uses, or were demolished to make way for modern port infrastructure, cannot be confirmed from available sources. The tuna fleet similarly vanished from operational records, with no subsequent disposition documented.

**Assessment**: This represents a genuine case of operationally abandoned infrastructure—facilities built to specification but never productively used, rendered obsolete by trade policy and governance failure before commercial operation could begin.
