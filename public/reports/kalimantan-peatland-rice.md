# The Central Kalimantan peatland project

## The Mega-Project Vision

In 1995, the Suharto regime launched what became Indonesia's largest single development initiative of the 1990s: the Central Kalimantan Mega Project (often abbreviated as PLG or Proyek Lahan Gambut—literally "Peatland Project"). The undertaking was neither modest nor peripheral to state ideology. [[16]](https://www.multinationalmonitor.org/hyper/issues/1990/10/marr.html) The government committed to draining and converting 1 million hectares of pristine peat wetland forest—an area approximately the size of Jamaica—into irrigated rice paddies, with the explicit goal of achieving national rice self-sufficiency, a politically central objective under the New Order. [[17]](https://www.downtoearth-indonesia.org/old-site/ctrans.htm) [[19]](https://www.conflictresolutionunit.id/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/1987-Indonesia_s-Transmigration-Program-and-Its-Role-in-the-Loss-of-Tropical-Rain-Forests-Anthony-J.-Whitten.pdf) The scheme targeted resettlement of 315,000 transmigrant families across 45 planned settlement units, each typically housing 7,000 families in agricultural colonies. [[15]](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-01-06-mn-500-story.html)

## Ecological and Agronomic Viability—Ignored

The project's fundamental technical premise was catastrophically flawed. Peat soils in their natural state are characterized by high acidity, waterlogging during wet seasons, rock-hard desiccation during dry seasons, and minimal nutrient availability. [[19]](https://www.conflictresolutionunit.id/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/1987-Indonesia_s-Transmigration-Program-and-Its-Role-in-the-Loss-of-Tropical-Rain-Forests-Anthony-J.-Whitten.pdf) Drainage—the project's core operation—rendered peat groundwater highly acidic and undrinkable, while simultaneously triggering subsidence and releasing stored carbon. Rice cultivation on peat soils requires sustained lime applications (estimated at 10 tons per hectare in some sites) to neutralise acidity—an economically unsustainable remediation requirement for smallholder farmers. [[17]](https://www.downtoearth-indonesia.org/old-site/ctrans.htm) Agricultural officers within Indonesia's own Ministry of Transmigration had documented that peat soils possess "limitations that are severe, very severe and extreme" for rice cultivation, yet project planning proceeded regardless. [[19]](https://www.conflictresolutionunit.id/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/1987-Indonesia_s-Transmigration-Program-and-Its-Role-in-the-Loss-of-Tropical-Rain-Forests-Anthony-J.-Whitten.pdf)

## Construction, Settlement, and Immediate Failure (1995–1999)

Settlement commenced in 1995. By 1999, approximately 15,600 families had been settled across 45 units in the peat zone. [[20]](https://voi.id/en/memori/767) The transition period was brief. As families attempted cultivation following conventional rice-farming practices transported from Java, crop failures cascaded. Irrigation infrastructure, designed for mineral soils with conventional drainage, became non-functional in the hyperacidic peat environment. Within the project's first four years, documented returns on capital investment approached zero. Production-oriented propaganda masks the actual outcomes: internal government reports acknowledged that agricultural yields failed to materialise in anticipated quantities, and settler household incomes declined relative to pre-migration baselines.

## The 1997 Fire Cataclysm and Project Termination

The 1997 fire season—the worst of the decade in Southeast Asia—decimated the PLG area with particular severity. [[19]](https://www.conflictresolutionunit.id/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/1987-Indonesia_s-Transmigration-Program-and-Its-Role-in-the-Loss-of-Tropical-Rain-Forests-Anthony-J.-Whitten.pdf) Peat fires, once ignited, burn underground and are nearly impossible to extinguish, releasing massive carbon stores while destroying the soil structure necessary for any future cultivation. The visible ecological catastrophe, coupled with the Asian financial crisis (which triggered Indonesia's deepest recession in decades), created political conditions for formal abandonment. In April 1999, the Habibie government cancelled the project. The decision was reconfirmed in 2000 under the Wahid administration. [[20]](https://voi.id/en/memori/767)

## Financial and Human Costs

At minimum, \$500 million in state resources had been expended on the failed scheme. [[16]](https://www.multinationalmonitor.org/hyper/issues/1990/10/marr.html) The government subsequently allocated Rp 7.5 billion (~\$950,000) in "Presidential Assistance" in 2000 to attempt rehabilitation of resettled communities. [[20]](https://voi.id/en/memori/767) Approximately 63,000 transmigrants faced an uncertain future in soils unsuitable for planned agriculture. Many engaged in illegal logging and artisanal mining for subsistence survival. In February 2001, transmigrant organisations submitted formal requests to the government requesting repatriation to Java. [[15]](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-01-06-mn-500-story.html) [[17]](https://www.downtoearth-indonesia.org/old-site/ctrans.htm)

## Project Repackaging and Broader Extraction Intent

Perhaps most revealing was the government's subsequent reframing of the failed peat project. Rather than accepting defeat, it repackaged the zone as the Central Kalimantan Integrated Economic Development Zone (KAPET—Kawasan Pengembangan Ekonomi Terpadu), nearly tripling the designated area from 1 million to 2.7 million hectares. [[17]](https://www.downtoearth-indonesia.org/old-site/ctrans.htm) The new mandate emphasised "forestry, mining, plantations, and tourism"—a framework widely interpreted by analysts as revealing the "real purpose" had always been access for timber contractors and oil palm investors rather than sustainable agricultural development. [[9]](https://www.rand.org/pubs/working_papers/WR1183.html) [[10]](https://voxdev.org/topic/trade/when-special-economic-zones-fail-lessons-indonesia) The rhetorical shift from "rice self-sufficiency for smallholder welfare" to "extractive economic zone development" exposed the subordinate position of settler interests within state development logic.

## Indigenous Dispossession and Uncompensated Rights

The Dayak indigenous communities, whose customary land rights (hak ulayat) held legal recognition under Indonesian customary law, received no compensation and minimal consultation regarding the PLG initiative. [[17]](https://www.downtoearth-indonesia.org/old-site/ctrans.htm) [[19]](https://www.conflictresolutionunit.id/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/1987-Indonesia_s-Transmigration-Program-and-Its-Role-in-the-Loss-of-Tropical-Rain-Forests-Anthony-J.-Whitten.pdf) By 2001, inter-ethnic violence erupted between transmigrant settlements and indigenous communities, a direct consequence of the unmediated collision between settler agricultural expansion and indigenous resource claims. [[15]](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1990-01-06-mn-500-story.html)
