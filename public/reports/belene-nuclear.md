# Belene Nuclear Power Plant

## 1.1 The Project Framework and Technical Specifications

On **March 20, 1981**, Bulgaria's Council of Ministers formally approved the Belene site for construction of the nation's second nuclear power plant. [[16]](https://www.beleneproject.bg/history?lang=en) [[17]](https://inis.iaea.org/records/nygdt-qhv50) The facility was to consist of two VVER-1000/V-466 pressurized water reactors, each rated at 1,000 megawatts of electrical capacity, for a combined output of 2,000 MW. [[3]](https://www.power-technology.com/projects/belene-nuclear/) [[21]](https://www.reuters.com/article/markets/bulgaria-abandons-belene-nuclear-plant-plans-idUSL6E8ES7A4/) The site occupied approximately 3–4 kilometers from the town of Belene itself, on the Danube River's right bank at approximately 43°37'46"N, 25°11'12"E, roughly 11 kilometers downstream from Svishtov. [[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belene_Nuclear_Power_Plant)

The VVER-1000 technology selected represented Soviet-era reactor design that had achieved operational maturity by the 1980s. Globally, the VVER-1000 family accumulated over 1,000 reactor-years of operational experience without major safety incidents, with more than 30 units in service across Eastern Europe and Russia. [[12]](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/country-profiles/countries-a-f/bulgaria) The geological assessment conducted by the Bulgarian Academy of Sciences in the 1980s and 1990s found no evidence of capable active faults at the site, with peak ground acceleration for a hypothetical 10,000-year safe shutdown earthquake estimated at 0.20–0.24 gravities (approximately Modified Mercalli Scale level VII). [[17]](https://inis.iaea.org/records/nygdt-qhv50) Post-Fukushima (2011) stress testing under European Union protocols found the proposed design compliant with contemporary safety margins. [[12]](https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/country-profiles/countries-a-f/bulgaria) Thus, the technological foundation itself was sound by the standards applied at each stage of evaluation.

## 1.2 Timeline: From Soviet Planning to Post-Cold War Cancellation

The project's lifecycle divided into three distinct phases separated by political and economic shocks.

**Soviet-Era Phase (1981–1991)**

Planning commenced in 1980–1981 with site characterization studies conducted by two organizations: Energoproekt Sofia (Bulgarian) and Atomenergoproekt Kyiv (Ukrainian). [[17]](https://inis.iaea.org/records/nygdt-qhv50) Construction of the primary circuit units and foundation structures began in 1987 following Soviet design protocols. By the time the Soviet Union collapsed in August 1991, approximately **40 percent of Unit 1's site work had been completed**, and approximately **80 percent of major equipment had been procured and remained in long-term storage**. [[2]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Belene_Nuclear_Power_Plant) [[5]](https://babel.ua/en/texts/117262-bulgaria-has-refused-to-sell-russia-made-belene-reactors-to-ukraine-this-nuclear-power-plant-has-been-the-main-long-term-project-and-the-cause-of-scandals-for-40-years-in-bulgaria-and-here-s-why) The project's suspension in August 1991 was immediate and complete. Bulgaria's post-communist transition coincided with economic crisis of extraordinary severity: the national currency became hyperinflated, Bulgaria lost access to Comecon trading advantages (which had subsidized energy costs), and sovereign debt climbed above 100 percent of gross domestic product. The government possessed neither capital reserves nor hard currency to continue investment in a four-year construction project promising no near-term returns. [[2]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Belene_Nuclear_Power_Plant)

**Revival Phase (2002–2012)**

On **December 11, 2002**, the Bulgarian government approved revival of the Belene project with a plan for two VVER-1000 reactors. [[16]](https://www.beleneproject.bg/history?lang=en) The decision reflected both domestic energy shortages and European Union directives urging member states to expand nuclear capacity as a non-emitting power source to meet climate commitments. In May 2005, Atomstroyexport, the Russian state-owned nuclear engineering firm, was selected as the main contractor through what was characterized as an international tender process. [[5]](https://babel.ua/en/texts/117262-bulgaria-has-refused-to-sell-russia-made-belene-reactors-to-ukraine-this-nuclear-power-plant-has-been-the-main-long-term-project-and-the-cause-of-scandals-for-40-years-in-bulgaria-and-here-s-why) [[18]](https://www.powerinfotoday.com/europe/belene-nuclear-power-plant-bulgaria/) Contract signature occurred on **January 18, 2008**, establishing a fixed-price commitment of €3.997 billion, excluding financing, land acquisition, and supporting infrastructure costs. [[3]](https://www.power-technology.com/projects/belene-nuclear/)

On **September 3, 2008**, Bulgarian officials conducted a ceremonial construction recommencement at the site. [[10]](https://www.powermag.com/bulgaria-officially-launches-construction-of-belene-nuke/) However, the 2008 global financial crisis intervened immediately afterward. RWE Power, which had briefly partnered with Bulgaria's state nuclear utility NEK EAD to explore equipment supply, withdrew its involvement in 2009, citing unresolved financing mechanisms and perceived governance risks. [[18]](https://www.powerinfotoday.com/europe/belene-nuclear-power-plant-bulgaria/) In June 2010, the government announced an indefinite construction freeze. [[3]](https://www.power-technology.com/projects/belene-nuclear/)

The national assembly voted on **March 28, 2012**, to terminate the project entirely. [[21]](https://www.reuters.com/article/markets/bulgaria-abandons-belene-nuclear-plant-plans-idUSL6E8ES7A4/) The stated rationale encompassed three factors: (1) cost escalation from the \$4 billion contract estimate to preliminary assessments of \$6–12.5 billion; (2) the absence of any secured strategic investor willing to finance and manage operations; and (3) the lack of long-term power purchase agreements from major customers in Bulgaria or neighboring EU states. [[3]](https://www.power-technology.com/projects/belene-nuclear/) [[6]](https://www.voanews.com/a/russian-newspaper-false-claims-belene-nuclear-power-fact-check/6742217.html)

Critically, the March 2012 termination decision was not the project's final word. In January 2013, Bulgaria held a national referendum on nuclear expansion, in which over 60 percent of voters answered "yes" to continued nuclear development. [[5]](https://babel.ua/en/texts/117262-bulgaria-has-refused-to-sell-russia-made-belene-reactors-to-ukraine-this-nuclear-power-plant-has-been-the-main-long-term-project-and-the-cause-of-scandals-for-40-years-in-bulgaria-and-here-s-why) However, turnout reached only 20 percent (approximately 1.385 million votes), rendering the result non-binding under Bulgarian law. In June 2018, parliament revoked the 2012 moratorium, and the government reopened a tender for strategic investors. [[1]](https://www.neimagazine.com/news/bulgaria-cancels-belene-npp-project-11224401/) Between March and August 2019, the state utility NEK issued a new bid round attracting 13 applications, including proposals from Rosatom (Russia), China National Nuclear Corporation (CNNC), Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP), Framatome (France), and General Electric (USA). [[14]](https://jamestown.org/bulgaria-quits-belene-nuclear-power-plant-open-doors-to-south-stream/) None of these suitors advanced to formal negotiations.

On **October 17, 2023**, Bulgaria's cabinet formally cancelled the Belene Nuclear Power Plant as a declared project of national importance and terminated the investor selection procedure. [[1]](https://www.neimagazine.com/news/bulgaria-cancels-belene-npp-project-11224401/) [[7]](https://www.nucnet.org/news/bulgaria-10-4-2023) [[9]](https://www.euractiv.com/section/politics/short_news/bulgaria-puts-end-to-belene-nuclear-project/) The government simultaneously redirected focus toward expansion of the existing Kozloduy nuclear facility, located 150 kilometers downstream. [[19]](https://balkangreenenergynews.com/bulgaria-abandons-belene-announces-new-reactors-at-kozloduy/)

## 1.3 Financial Accounting and Sunk Costs

Bulgaria invested approximately \$1.2–1.5 billion in capital expenditures over the project's operational phases (1981–2023), though no electricity was ever generated. Of this sum, approximately €600 million was spent before the 1991 suspension, and an additional €400–600 million was expended during the 2002–2012 revival attempt. [[13]](https://www.euractiv.com/short_news/belene-nuclear-plant/) [[14]](https://jamestown.org/bulgaria-quits-belene-nuclear-power-plant-open-doors-to-south-stream/)

A critical financial contingency emerged in June 2016 when an International Court of Arbitration in Geneva awarded Atomstroyexport compensation of €620 million (approximately \$691.5 million) for manufactured equipment that had been procured under the 2008 contract but sat in long-term storage as the project stalled. [[11]](https://www.neimagazine.com/new-build-life-extension/bulgaria-to-repurpose-equipment-from-cancelled-belene-project-for-kozloduy-expansion/) Bulgaria paid this settlement rather than contest further litigation. The arbitration file documented that the contractor had completed approximately 60 percent of manufacturing for two full reactor units: pressure vessels, steam generators, circulation pumps, turbines, instrumentation systems, and auxiliary equipment. [[11]](https://www.neimagazine.com/new-build-life-extension/bulgaria-to-repurpose-equipment-from-cancelled-belene-project-for-kozloduy-expansion/)

Accounting for arbitration payments, direct capital investment, engineering studies, environmental assessments, and land acquisition, the true financial cost to Bulgaria approximated \$2.2–2.5 billion—or roughly 2–3 percent of Bulgaria's annual gross domestic product in 2023—to yield zero productive output.

## 1.4 Governance and Corruption Findings

Multiple investigations and media reports documented governance failures and apparent corruption throughout the project's lifecycle. A 2010 U.S. State Department cable, later disclosed by The Guardian newspaper, reported that Westinghouse officials characterized the first reactor unit as "a lemon" and complained to embassy officials of endemic corruption within Bulgaria's project management. [[6]](https://www.voanews.com/a/russian-newspaper-false-claims-belene-nuclear-power-fact-check/6742217.html) Specific incidents of note include:

- **2007**: Resignation of Energy Minister Rumen Ovcharov amid preliminary corruption probes related to nuclear sector contracts.
- **2010–2013**: Bulgarian State Prosecutor initiated investigations into procurement irregularities, alleging inflated contract terms and non-competitive bidding practices.
- **May 2016**: Former Economy and Energy Minister Petar Dimitrov was indicted in connection with the project, specifically regarding negotiation of the 2008 Atomstroyexport contract. [[8]](https://balkaninsight.com/2016/10/20/bulgarian-ex-minister-indicted-over-failed-belene-nuclear-project-10-19-2016/)
- **2013**: Multiple arrests of NEK executives and Risk Engineering company director on embezzlement allegations related to project cost overruns.

The absence of broad competitive international bidding for the 2008 contract—Atomstroyexport was selected without parallel proposals from Western European contractors—became a focal point of audit criticism in subsequent years. [[5]](https://babel.ua/en/texts/117262-bulgaria-has-refused-to-sell-russia-made-belene-reactors-to-ukraine-this-nuclear-power-plant-has-been-the-main-long-term-project-and-the-cause-of-scandals-for-40-years-in-bulgaria-and-here-s-why)

## 1.5 Geopolitical Realignment and Project Death

Bulgaria's decision to finally abandon Belene in October 2023 occurred within a transformed geopolitical context. The Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 shifted EU energy security concerns sharply away from dependency on Russian technology and financing. Rosatom's positioning as a primary bidder in the 2018–2019 investor round became politically untenable by 2023. Bulgaria, a NATO member state, faced diplomatic pressure from both the United States and EU partners to diminish reliance on Russian nuclear suppliers. [[14]](https://jamestown.org/bulgaria-quits-belene-nuclear-power-plant-open-doors-to-south-stream/) [[19]](https://balkangreenenergynews.com/bulgaria-abandons-belene-announces-new-reactors-at-kozloduy/)

Additionally, Bulgaria's electricity market had evolved. Renewable energy capacity grew substantially during the 2010s, driven by EU climate directives and declining solar panel costs. The urgency for massive new baseload capacity—which had justified Belene in 2008—diminished considerably by 2020. The government's strategic pivot toward expanding Kozloduy (an existing facility with established supply chains and workforce familiarity) offered a lower-political-risk pathway to modestly increased nuclear output. [[19]](https://balkangreenenergynews.com/bulgaria-abandons-belene-announces-new-reactors-at-kozloduy/)

## 1.6 Surviving Assets and Current Status

Although the Belene Nuclear Power Plant facility itself remains uncompleted and formally cancelled, multiple material and institutional assets survived the project's termination:

**1. Manufactured Equipment and Storage Infrastructure:** The two pressure vessels, four steam generators, four circulation pumps, turbine sets, control instrumentation, and auxiliary systems procured under the 2008 contract remain in secure storage facilities at the Belene site under NEK stewardship. [[11]](https://www.neimagazine.com/new-build-life-extension/bulgaria-to-repurpose-equipment-from-cancelled-belene-project-for-kozloduy-expansion/) Estimated equipment value (based on the €620 million arbitration award for manufacturing costs) approaches \$700–750 million. NEK has maintained these systems under climate-controlled preservation protocols, preventing corrosion and material degradation, at estimated annual costs of several million euros (exact figures not disclosed in public sources). [[11]](https://www.neimagazine.com/new-build-life-extension/bulgaria-to-repurpose-equipment-from-cancelled-belene-project-for-kozloduy-expansion/) Bulgaria has declined proposals to sell these reactors to Ukraine for emergency power generation, citing domestic policy constraints and technical compatibility concerns. [[5]](https://babel.ua/en/texts/117262-bulgaria-has-refused-to-sell-russia-made-belene-reactors-to-ukraine-this-nuclear-power-plant-has-been-the-main-long-term-project-and-the-cause-of-scandals-for-40-years-in-bulgaria-and-here-s-why)

**2. Heavy-Lift Port Facility:** The port infrastructure constructed specifically to support nuclear equipment offloading (discussed in Section 3) remains operational and has proven economically valuable for non-nuclear cargo transport.

**3. Institutional Framework and Site Designation:** The Belene site retains its official status as land designated for potential future nuclear development, though this designation now primarily serves archival and legal purposes. The Bulgarian government has explicitly stated no intention to revive the project under any foreseeable political scenario. [[1]](https://www.neimagazine.com/news/bulgaria-cancels-belene-npp-project-11224401/)

**4. Engineering Knowledge and Documentation:** Thousands of engineering studies, site surveys, safety assessments, and design documents from four decades of work remain archived within Bulgaria's nuclear regulatory authority and NEK. These materials have modest value for other energy infrastructure projects but represent a non-recoverable investment.
