# The Lower Churchill proposals

## The Broader Context

Long before Muskrat Falls was sanctioned in 2012, Newfoundland and Labrador pursued a succession of proposals to develop the full hydroelectric potential of the Churchill River. The province spent \$118 million between 1972 and 2003 on Lower Churchill development studies, negotiations, and engineering work—without a single megawatt of capacity constructed. [[10]](https://www.gov.nl.ca/publicat/royalcomm/research/Churchill.pdf) This 30-year corridor of investment yielded no physical infrastructure, only abandoned agreements, suspended negotiations, and political recriminations.

## Early Proposals and the Initial Escalation (1972–1976)

The first serious post-Confederation attempt at Lower Churchill development emerged under Premier Frank Moores in the early 1970s. Initial cost estimates for Gull Island (the larger, upstream site) were set at \$1.842 billion. [[10]](https://www.gov.nl.ca/publicat/royalcomm/research/Churchill.pdf) Within a few years, these estimates had escalated to \$2.318 billion—a 26% increase before a single foundation stone was laid. [[10]](https://www.gov.nl.ca/publicat/royalcomm/research/Churchill.pdf) This early pattern of cost growth would repeat in every subsequent proposal.

By 1976, the Moores government suspended the Gull Island proposal entirely, citing unresolved financing and market access issues. The project reverted to paper status.

## The Lower Churchill Development Corporation and Quebec's Geographic Monopoly (1978–1982)

The Peckford government (1979–1989) established the Lower Churchill Development Corporation to advance the projects with renewed institutional focus. Between 1978 and 1982, the Corporation pursued two parallel strategies: direct development of Gull Island and Muskrat Falls, and negotiation of power purchase agreements with American utilities.

Critical to understanding the abandonment pattern is the geographic reality of power transmission. Labrador's hydroelectric potential is landlocked by Hydro-Québec's control of transmission corridors. Any power generated in Labrador destined for U.S. or Atlantic Canadian markets must traverse Quebec territory or be contracted through Quebec's infrastructure. This geographic monopoly would prove fatal to multiple development proposals. [[10]](https://www.gov.nl.ca/publicat/royalcomm/research/Churchill.pdf)

Between 1980 and 1982, the Lower Churchill Development Corporation negotiated power purchase agreements with the Power Authority of the State of New York (PASNY) and Public Service Company of New Hampshire. These agreements promised firm commitments from U.S. utilities to purchase Labrador hydroelectricity. However, these agreements became, in the technical terminology of the era, "academic"—meaning they were contingent on transmission access through Quebec that Hydro-Québec refused to grant. [[10]](https://www.gov.nl.ca/publicat/royalcomm/research/Churchill.pdf) The Corporation eventually abandoned these efforts as futile.

## The 1984 Quebec Offer and its Rejection (1984)

In 1984, Hydro-Québec itself advanced a proposal. Under the structure of a "Statement of Intent," Quebec offered to provide \$1 billion in financing and infrastructure support for Lower Churchill development, contingent on specific commercial terms. The offer was intended to bring Quebec into partnership with Newfoundland and Labrador in developing the resource.

Premier Brian Peckford reviewed the proposal and rejected it within 72 hours, concluding that Quebec's terms would grant Quebec too much control over the province's hydroelectric future. [[10]](https://www.gov.nl.ca/publicat/royalcomm/research/Churchill.pdf) The offer lapsed, and with it the prospect of a Quebec-led development of the Lower Churchill corridor.

## The Wells Administration Hiatus (1989–1996)

When Clyde Wells became Premier in 1989, his administration inherited two decades of suspended Lower Churchill ambitions. In 1991–1992, the province received a comprehensive \$14 billion development proposal from private sector interests. The Wells government requested substantial clarifications and adjustments to the terms. While deliberations proceeded, an unexpected event shifted priorities: Newfoundland's electrical system faced an operational crisis in 1992–1994, and Hydro's financial position deteriorated sharply. The crisis was resolved through other means (including the Churchill Falls existing contract providing emergency capacity relief), and the provincial government's interest in new development waned. Quebec, observing the delay, withdrew its willingness to support negotiations. [[10]](https://www.gov.nl.ca/publicat/royalcomm/research/Churchill.pdf)

## Final Abandonment and Political Reckoning (1996–1998)

The Tobin administration (1996–2001) made a final attempt to secure Lower Churchill development. Negotiations between Newfoundland and Labrador and potential partners—including Hydro-Québec, U.S. utilities, and private investors—proceeded from 1996 to 1998. The fundamental issue that had defeated three previous administrations persisted: parties could not agree on power pricing, marketing structure, and Quebec's role in transmission and distribution. [[10]](https://www.gov.nl.ca/publicat/royalcomm/research/Churchill.pdf) By 1998, the province formally suspended active development pursuit, though the resource license remained in place.

## The Investment Result

The \$118 million spent on Lower Churchill development from 1972 to 2003 was entirely invested in studies, engineering, negotiation, and organizational overhead. No megawatts were generated. No dams were constructed. The sole survivor of this 30-year investment was institutional knowledge and engineering data that would eventually inform the separate Muskrat Falls project undertaken two decades later.
