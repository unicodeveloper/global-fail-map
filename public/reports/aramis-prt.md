# Aramis

## What Was Attempted

In 1970, France began work on Aramis (Aérien Roulant à Modulation Individuelle de Sillage—aerial rolling vehicle with individual wave modulation), a Personal Rapid Transit (PRT) system developed by Matra, the aerospace and defense firm. The concept was distinct from both the Aérotrain and conventional metro systems: Aramis proposed small, automatically guided vehicles traveling on dedicated guideways, with each vehicle responding to individual passenger demand rather than operating on fixed schedules. Passengers would board at demand-responsive stations, and the vehicle would dynamically couple and decouple with other vehicles to form platoons of optimal size [[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramis_(personal_rapid_transit)).

The system was designed to overcome the inefficiencies of both rail (fixed routes, scheduled stops) and the automobile (congestion, parking demands). It represented a kind of middle ground—public transit that behaved like private transport, without the waste.

Initial development occurred at Orly Airport around 1972, with the idea of creating an internal people-mover [[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramis_(personal_rapid_transit)). But the project's ambition grew beyond a single airport application. By the early 1980s, Aramis was being designed as a citywide system for the Île-de-France region, with partnerships involving RATP (the Paris transit operator), the Île-de-France regional transport authority (IAURIF), and the French transport ministry.

A crucial test track was installed in 1986 on the Petite Ceinture, the abandoned railway that rings Paris in a loop. The specific test site was located at coordinates 48.838817°N, 2.271253°E, in the 15th arrondissement—within Paris proper, allowing engineers and planners to observe the system's behavior in an urban environment [[8]](https://fabricofparis.com/2020/02/25/transport-that-never-was-aramis.html).

## The Design Erosion Problem

What makes Aramis historically fascinating is not its final abandonment but the documented process of design degradation that preceded it. Bruno Latour, the French sociologist of technology, embedded himself in the Aramis project and documented its collapse in a 1996 scholarly monograph, *Aramis, or the Love of Technology*. Latour's analysis reveals how cost constraints and competing institutional interests progressively stripped away the system's defining capabilities.

- **1970–1973**: Original concept promised true point-to-point service—passengers could board at any station and the system would route them directly to any destination without intermediate stops.
- **1973**: To reduce cost, point-to-point capability was removed. Vehicles were now constrained to predefined trunk routes.
- **1977**: Mixed-use stations (allowing both boarding and transit) were eliminated; the system could only serve stations at the beginning and end of routes.
- **1981**: By the early 1980s, as capacity pressure mounted, the vehicle size had grown from 4 persons per vehicle to 10 to 20 persons, transforming Aramis from a small, responsive system into something closer to a conventional metro.

The critical technical problem was platooning—the mechanism by which vehicles were supposed to couple and decouple at speed. In tests on the Petite Ceinture, platooning never functioned reliably. Vehicles would bump and jolt during coupling attempts. Passengers experienced jarring and discomfort. The contactless coupling mechanism, which was supposed to be the technological innovation that made Aramis possible, proved unreliable [[8]](https://fabricofparis.com/2020/02/25/transport-that-never-was-aramis.html).

## Organizational Contradictions

As costs mounted and design capabilities eroded, institutional tensions emerged. RATP, the public transit operator, was skeptical of a system that would require new operations protocols and staff retraining. The regional government wanted a cost-effective solution. Matra wanted a viable product. These interests were not aligned, and the project became a battleground of competing visions, each iteration further diluting the original concept.

## Discontinuation and Geographic Record

Aramis was abandoned in November 1987 [[8]](https://fabricofparis.com/2020/02/25/transport-that-never-was-aramis.html). The Petite Ceinture test track was dismantled. Matra shifted its expertise toward other automated transit systems—most notably the VAL (Véhicule Automatique Léger), which went into service at Orly Airport and subsequently became the basis for fully automated metro lines in Paris and other cities [[8]](https://fabricofparis.com/2020/02/25/transport-that-never-was-aramis.html). Lines 14, 1, and 4 of the Paris Metro are now operated using VAL technology derived from Aramis-era automation research, though without Aramis's problematic platooning mechanism.

The geographic footprint of Aramis is largely erased. The Petite Ceinture test site is now part of the rail corridor's conversion into parks and recreational spaces. But the automation engineering that emerged from Aramis development remains embedded in Paris transit operations. In this sense, while Aramis as a system failed, the technological capabilities it developed survived and were successfully deployed in a different form.
