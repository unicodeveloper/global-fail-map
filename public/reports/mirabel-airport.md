# Mirabel International Airport

In March 1969, the Canadian federal government expropriated 97,000 acres (39,250 hectares) of land approximately 50 kilometres northwest of Montreal [[1]](https://sencanada.ca/Content/Sen/Committee/451/NFFN/briefs/NFFN_C-15_Brief_KristinMuller_e.pdf). The expropriation affected approximately 3,200 property owners [[1]](https://sencanada.ca/Content/Sen/Committee/451/NFFN/briefs/NFFN_C-15_Brief_KristinMuller_e.pdf). The land was intended for a new international airport to replace the congested facilities then serving Montreal.

Mirabel International Airport opened in 1975 at an estimated cost of approximately \$500 million (equivalent to approximately \$3 billion in 2026 dollars [[3]](https://driving.ca/features/feature-story/not-so-civil-engineers-4-of-the-biggest-canadian-infrastructure-boondoggles/wcm/b8be51b1-34c6-40c9-a39f-be7e8ce46436/amp/)). The airport was located at a significant distance from the city centre—a decision made to acquire sufficient land and avoid residential congestion. This distance proved to be a strategic liability. Planners anticipated that expanded highway systems and a light rail connection would mitigate the travel time penalty. Neither infrastructure materialized at the scale required.

Without the planned surface transit connections, Mirabel was effectively inaccessible to Montreal residents except by automobile. The distance and lack of transit options gradually shifted passenger preference toward the older, closer Trudeau Airport (formerly Dorval), located only 22 kilometres from downtown Montreal. As airlines and passengers increasingly chose Trudeau, Mirabel's passenger volume declined [[3]](https://driving.ca/features/feature-story/not-so-civil-engineers-4-of-the-biggest-canadian-infrastructure-boondoggles/wcm/b8be51b1-34c6-40c9-a39f-be7e8ce46436/amp/).

Commercial passenger service at Mirabel ended in 2004, approximately 29 years after opening [[3]](https://driving.ca/features/feature-story/not-so-civil-engineers-4-of-the-biggest-canadian-infrastructure-boondoggles/wcm/b8be51b1-34c6-40c9-a39f-be7e8ce46436/amp/). The airport transitioned to cargo operations and served as a manufacturing site for Bombardier. The terminal building was demolished between 2014 and 2015 [[3]](https://driving.ca/features/feature-story/not-so-civil-engineers-4-of-the-biggest-canadian-infrastructure-boondoggles/wcm/b8be51b1-34c6-40c9-a39f-be7e8ce46436/amp/). Land return processes occurred in phases: 1985, 2006, and 2019 [[1]](https://sencanada.ca/Content/Sen/Committee/451/NFFN/briefs/NFFN_C-15_Brief_KristinMuller_e.pdf). The site now operates as a cargo facility and manufacturing centre rather than the passenger airport originally intended.

Mirabel represents a failure of site selection and integrated planning. The geographic isolation was known at the time of construction but was justified by the assumption of robust ground transit. The failure of that transit infrastructure to materialize left the airport permanently disadvantaged relative to the older, closer alternative. The massive federal expropriation—displacing thousands of property owners and acquiring land at significant public cost—ultimately created an asset that served a different purpose (cargo and manufacturing) than its original intended use (international passenger service). The decision to exile the airport to the far periphery of the metropolitan area, without parallel investment in rapid transit, proved unsustainable for the passenger function.
