# Ofo

Ofo represents the most emblematic failure of Beijing's startup culture during the 2010s venture capital boom. Founded in **December 2014** by **Dai Wei** and members of the Peking University cycling club, Ofo began as a simple response to dormitory commuting challenges but rapidly scaled into a global phenomenon. [[3]](https://www.rolandberger.com/en/Insights/Publications/The-rise-and-fall-of-Chinese-bike-sharing-startups.html) [[27]](https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1001480)

## Rapid Growth and Capital Influx

The company raised **\$2.2 billion** across multiple funding rounds, reaching a peak valuation of **\$3 billion**. [[3]](https://www.rolandberger.com/en/Insights/Publications/The-rise-and-fall-of-Chinese-bike-sharing-startups.html) This extraordinary capital accumulation enabled aggressive international expansion: Ofo deployed bikes not only across major Chinese cities but into **Shanghai, Singapore, Paris**, and other international markets. The ambition was palpable—the company aimed to become the world's largest bike-sharing operator. However, this expansion masked a fundamental business reality: Ofo could not monetize its operations at scale.

## Unsustainable Economics and Strategic Missteps

The company's operational model proved unsustainable. Despite massive funding, Ofo could not generate sufficient revenue from bike rentals to offset the operational costs of deployment, maintenance, customer service, and the inevitable loss of inventory to theft and abandonment. The company pursued an aggressive merger strategy, seeking to consolidate with competitors like **Mobike** and negotiate acquisition by **DiDi Chuxing** (ride-hailing giant), but these deals faltered. [[27]](https://www.sixthtone.com/news/1001480) [[28]](https://jefftowson.com/2017/03/how-hype-and-greed-are-ruining-chinese-bike-sharing-i-e-mobike-and-ofo/) The merger with DiDi, which would have provided access to an existing customer base and alternative revenue streams, was never completed—a critical strategic failure for a company burning cash.

## Organizational Collapse and Founder Responsibility

By **summer 2018**, Ofo approached insolvency and by **2019** had laid off most of its staff. [[3]](https://www.rolandberger.com/en/Insights/Publications/The-rise-and-fall-of-Chinese-bike-sharing-startups.html) The company's failure exposed fundamental gaps in founder experience. **Dai Wei**, despite being effective at marketing and securing venture capital, lacked the operational expertise necessary to build a sustainable business model or navigate the complex logistics of managing a dockless bike fleet at scale. [[28]](https://jefftowson.com/2017/03/how-hype-and-greed-are-ruining-chinese-bike-sharing-i-e-mobike-and-ofo/)

## What Survived and What Was Lost

Ofo itself dissolved, but the bike-sharing concept did not die with it. **Mobike** achieved commercial sustainability where Ofo failed, partnering with Meituan (a major e-commerce and services platform) and consolidating market position. What Beijing lost was not the technology but a specific company's vision: Ofo's aggressive, venture-capital-driven expansion model became a cautionary tale for Chinese startups and international investors about the dangers of prioritizing growth over unit economics.

The human cost was substantial. Hundreds of thousands of users lost access to their deposited rental fees—funds held by Ofo that were never recovered. [[30]](https://www.pymnts.com/news/international/south-east-asia/2017/mobike-ofo-bike-sharing) Employees faced sudden unemployment. And the streets of Beijing, Shanghai, and dozens of international cities became littered with abandoned bikes—a visible monument to venture capital excess.
