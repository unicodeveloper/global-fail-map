# The Red Sea to Dead Sea Conveyance

## What Was Proposed

The Red Sea–Dead Sea Water Conveyance (RSDSC), occasionally referred to as the "Two Seas Canal," was conceived as a comprehensive pipeline and desalination infrastructure system intended to address two simultaneous crises: the Dead Sea's catastrophic shrinkage and chronic water scarcity across Israel, Jordan, and the Palestinian territories [[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea%E2%80%93Dead_Sea_Water_Conveyance). The project involved constructing a pipeline from the coastal city of Aqaba on the Red Sea northward through Jordanian territory to the Dead Sea's Lisan basin, paired with a desalination plant to convert seawater into potable water for regional distribution and a hydroelectric power generation facility to offset operational costs [[16]](https://deadsea.com/jordan/attraction/jordan-dead-sea-shrinking/).

The underlying logic was ecological and hydro-political: the Dead Sea was declining at an alarming rate—approximately **one meter per year**—due to upstream water diversions on the Jordan River (Israel's 1960s National Water Carrier project diverted approximately **95% of the Jordan River's water** before it reached the Dead Sea) [[14]](https://www.22-med.com/en/can-we-still-save-the-dead-sea/) [[15]](https://www.timesofisrael.com/experts-want-pols-to-pipe-down-over-red-seadead-sea-link/). Discharging desalinated seawater's brine (hypersaline discharge) into the Dead Sea could, in principle, stabilize the lake's level while simultaneously providing the region with freshwater for drinking and agriculture.

## Dates and Formal Structure

The project emerged from a **May 9, 2005** formal trilateral agreement between Israel, Jordan, and the Palestinian Authority [[16]](https://deadsea.com/jordan/attraction/jordan-dead-sea-shrinking/). Formal feasibility studies were conducted between **2005 and 2012** by the World Bank and engineering firm Coyne & Bellier [[13]](https://arava.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/Dead-Sea-Conduits-Study-English.pdf). International bidding for construction contracts opened in **2016**, with Jordan's government announcing **five shortlisted consortia**. The Ministry announced that a **\$100 million first phase would commence in Q1 2018**, with completion scheduled for **2021** [[17]](https://www.timesofisrael.com/after-years-of-delays-jordan-said-to-nix-red-sea-dead-sea-canal-with-israel-pa/).

No construction ever commenced. By **June 2021**, Jordan's government formally abandoned the project [[17]](https://www.timesofisrael.com/after-years-of-delays-jordan-said-to-nix-red-sea-dead-sea-canal-with-israel-pa/).

## Geographic Connection to the Dead Sea

The pipeline's intake was located at **Aqaba** on the Red Sea coast [[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea%E2%80%93Dead_Sea_Water_Conveyance); the discharge point was specified as the **Lisan area** in the Dead Sea basin [[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Sea%E2%80%93Dead_Sea_Water_Conveyance), positioned to discharge brine directly into the lake. The routing ran through the **Arava Valley**, the arid lowland depression separating Israel from Jordan. The project's entire geographic rationale depended on leveraging the **approximately 400-meter elevation difference between the Red Sea and the Dead Sea** to enable gravity-fed conveyance and hydroelectric generation [[11]](https://www.jpost.com/health-science/what-is-the-red-sea-dead-sea-canal-that-jordan-renounced-explainer-671294).

## Why the Project Mattered

For Israel and the Palestinian territories, the project promised a solution to chronic water scarcity; for Jordan, it offered both freshwater and economic opportunity through regional cooperation. The Dead Sea's decline was not abstract—it threatened tourism infrastructure, geological stability (sinkholes were proliferating), mineral extraction operations, and the lake's ecosystem. A World Bank feasibility study affirmed that the project was **technically feasible** [[16]](https://deadsea.com/jordan/attraction/jordan-dead-sea-shrinking/).

## Why It Was Abandoned

The project faced a constellation of interconnected obstacles:

**Cost and Maintenance Burden**: The total estimated cost was approximately **\$10 billion**, with operational and maintenance costs reaching **\$400 million annually** [[16]](https://deadsea.com/jordan/attraction/jordan-dead-sea-shrinking/). The economic burden of perpetual maintenance, given the region's limited revenue bases, made the project unsustainable in the long term.

**Fundamental Environmental Uncertainty**: Despite the World Bank's technical approval, the feasibility study identified significant environmental risks [[16]](https://deadsea.com/jordan/attraction/jordan-dead-sea-shrinking/). The critical question—whether brine discharge would meaningfully stabilize the Dead Sea or merely accelerate its salinization and ecological collapse—remained unresolved. Brine discharge of this magnitude would alter the Dead Sea's ionic composition, potentially damaging remaining biological systems and mineral extraction operations dependent on specific salt concentrations.

**Israeli Funding Resistance**: Israel was a formal signatory to the **2013 trilateral agreement** extending the original 2005 framework [[17]](https://www.timesofisrael.com/after-years-of-delays-jordan-said-to-nix-red-sea-dead-sea-canal-with-israel-pa/), yet the country never approved the required funding contributions, which were expected to exceed **\$1 billion over 25 years**. Political commitment appeared strong; financial commitment proved absent.

**Geopolitical Deterioration**: The 2015–2021 period witnessed escalating Israel-Jordan tensions, particularly following the 2015 killing of Jordanian pilots by Israeli forces and subsequent security incidents. The political momentum necessary to sustain international negotiations and coordinate multinational financing evaporated.

**Technological Alternatives**: By the late 2010s, advances in desalination technology made the brine-discharge component unnecessary. A simpler, cheaper desalination-only approach became feasible—leading to the **Aqaba-Amman Water Desalination and Conveyance Project**, formally agreed to in **January 2025**, which bypassed the problematic brine-discharge component entirely [[16]](https://deadsea.com/jordan/attraction/jordan-dead-sea-shrinking/).

## Status and What Survived

**Status**: Abandoned (officially discontinued by Jordanian government in June 2021).

**What survived**: The institutional framework for trilateral water cooperation persists, embodied in the 2005 and 2013 agreements and the successor Aqaba-Amman Project framework. Engineering and feasibility studies by the World Bank and Coyne & Bellier remain archived resources for future planners. However, the project itself—the integrated conveyance-desalination-hydroelectric system—was permanently shelved.

**What did not survive**: The assumption that brine discharge could serve as a solution. The 2025 Aqaba-Amman framework explicitly abandoned brine-discharge engineering in favor of straightforward seawater desalination with no discharge to the Dead Sea, acknowledging that the hydrological risk was unacceptable.
