# The Libyan Railway Network

## The Modern Railway Ambition (1998–2011 and Beyond)

The most expensive infrastructure suspension in Libyan history began with a vision announced in **1998**: a unified **3,170-kilometer national railway network** operating at standard gauge (1,435 mm), connecting the country's fragmented coastal cities and southern inland routes [[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Libya). The envisioned system comprised three principal lines: a **170-kilometer stretch linking Tripoli to Ras Ajdir** along the coastal road; a **472-kilometer route from Tripoli to Sirte**; and an **810-kilometer southern freight corridor connecting Misrata to Sabha** [[27]](https://libyaobserver.ly/inbrief/libya-china-sign-mou-resume-national-railway-project). The geographic hub was Tripoli—all major lines converged there, positioning the city as Libya's rail gateway to the Mediterranean and southern trading partners.

By 2008–2009, Libya had moved from planning to contracts. The government signed major construction agreements with China Railway Construction Company (CRCC), Russian Railways (RZD), and Ansaldo STS for signaling systems. Work commenced on ground surveys, workforce training, and construction logistics. Equipment arrived: **17 General Electric diesel locomotives** were delivered to Libya and initially stored in warehouses in Khoms [[5]](https://www.dw.com/en/libyas-train-project-remains-off-the-rails/a-19565824), approximately 150 kilometers east of Tripoli. The Libyan Railway and Maritime Transport Board (LREMB), headquartered in southwest Tripoli, employed over **800 people**, maintained facilities including Chinese-built barracks, a mosque, theater, and sporting complex, and held monthly payroll even as construction stalled [[5]](https://www.dw.com/en/libyas-train-project-remains-off-the-rails/a-19565824).

Then the uprising of **February 2011** halted everything. Within weeks, all contracts were suspended. The scale was staggering: **\$4.24 billion in signed contracts** were suspended, with **\$3.55 billion remaining unexecuted** as of mid-2016 [[5]](https://www.dw.com/en/libyas-train-project-remains-off-the-rails/a-19565824). No locomotives have run on Libyan rails since. The **3 kilometers of track already completed** before suspension sat unmaintained. As of **October 2016**, the LREMB headquarters compound remained staffed but derelict—a suspended institution with no trains to operate [[5]](https://www.dw.com/en/libyas-train-project-remains-off-the-rails/a-19565824).

## Attempted Revivals and Fragmentation

Between 2011 and 2026, the railway project experienced multiple attempted revivals, none resulting in resumed construction. In **2024**, Libyan and Russian officials met in Tripoli to discuss resuming the **stalled 2009 railway construction contract for the Sirte-to-Benghazi segment** [[28]](https://libyaherald.com/2024/07/libya-and-russia-discuss-reactivating-the-construction-of-the-railway-project/). By **November 2023**, a partnership agreement was signed between the Chinese consortium BFI and Tripoli's railway operators to oversee the **Eastern railway project** [[10]](https://libyareview.com/44105/libya-to-launch-6-infrastructure-projects-by-2024/). Yet these discussions produced no financing, no mobilization schedules, no equipment procurement.

The obstacles remained structural: political fragmentation (by 2016, three competing governments claimed legitimacy), collapsing oil revenue (production fell from 1.5 million barrels per day in 2010 to 200,000–400,000 bbl/day by 2013–2016), and currency depreciation that evaporated project budgets [[5]](https://www.dw.com/en/libyas-train-project-remains-off-the-rails/a-19565824). As one Deutsche Welle investigator noted in 2016, Libya represented a broader pattern: "a great number of ambitious rail projects in the Maghreb and Middle East, few of which have ever really come to anything" [[5]](https://www.dw.com/en/libyas-train-project-remains-off-the-rails/a-19565824).

## The Forgotten Colonial Railway (1912–1965)

Tripoli's infrastructure history includes a brief operational railway. During the **Italian colonial period (1911–1943)**, Italy invested heavily in transportation infrastructure, building approximately **1,500 kilometers of railway** across Libya [[13]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282572721_Libyan_Railway_A_Gateway_to_Europe). Tripoli's colonial-era rail network connected the city to inland towns (Garian, Tagiura, Zuara), and a unified Tripoli–Benghazi railway was initiated in **August 1941**, advancing approximately **160 kilometers (10 percent of the planned 1,040-kilometer route)** before World War II military defeat ended construction in **1942** [[13]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282572721_Libyan_Railway_A_Gateway_to_Europe).

The colonial network survived until **1965**, when Libya's post-independence government closed the system as a political decision—not abandonment due to failure, but a deliberate phase-out [[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_transport_in_Libya). The Italian railway workshops, stations, and signaling infrastructure fell into disuse. Fiat Railmotors (known as "Littorina") once operated at Tripoli Railway Station near the El Mina waterfront [[13]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282572721_Libyan_Railway_A_Gateway_to_Europe), but by the late 20th century, only traces remained—rusted rails, abandoned stations, archival photos. Contemporary Tripoli saw the railway presence reduced to institutional memory and administrative overhead (the LREMB payroll) without operational service.
