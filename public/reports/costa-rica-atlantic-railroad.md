# The Atlantic Railroad

After three failed contracts, a fourth American entrepreneur, Henry Meiggs, negotiated a revised concession in 1871. When Meiggs died in 1877, a young railroad employee named Minor Keith inherited the nearly bankrupt line and secured additional government land grants and contract extensions. Through persistent effort and access to banana plantation profits, Keith and his successors completed the Atlantic Railroad—also called the Limón-San José railroad—on December 7, 1890, after 19 years of intermittent construction [[5]](https://ticotimes.net/2026/04/19/the-story-of-costa-ricas-famous-railroad-to-limon) [[8]](https://www.visitcostarica.com/things-to-do/culture/historical-walks/limon).

The project was born out of economic necessity. In the 1860s and 1870s, Costa Rica's coffee industry, the lifeblood of its economy, depended on an agonizingly slow export route: coffee traveled from San José across difficult mountain terrain by mule to the Pacific coast, then shipped around Cape Horn or across Central America. A direct Caribbean outlet could dramatically reduce shipping time and cost [[5]](https://ticotimes.net/2026/04/19/the-story-of-costa-ricas-famous-railroad-to-limon). The completed Atlantic Railroad achieved this goal. Running from San José through mountainous terrain to Limón, the line transformed Costa Rica's export capabilities and facilitated the growth of the banana industry along the Atlantic corridor.

## A Successful Century

The Atlantic Railroad operated continuously for 101 years, serving as a critical economic lifeline. Passengers and freight moved across the line with regularity. The railway created employment, enabled settlement inland, and became woven into Costa Rican national identity [[13]](https://www.crcdaily.com/p/the-lost-legacy-of-costa-ricas-railroads). Mining, agricultural export, and passenger services depended on the line's operation. By the late twentieth century, however, road transport competition and shifting freight patterns had reduced its profitability, and the Instituto Costarricense de Ferrocarriles (INCOFER), the government agency responsible for the national rail system, struggled with chronic underfunding.

## Destruction and Permanent Abandonment

On April 22, 1991, a 7.6-magnitude earthquake centered 34 kilometers west of Ciudad de Limón struck the province with devastating force [[19]](https://earthquaketrack.com/quakes/1991-04-22-21-56-51-utc-7-6-10) [[21]](https://www.volcanodiscovery.com/es/sismos/informe-sismo/3070472/mag7quake-Apr-22-1991-Costa-Rica.html). The earthquake killed two adults and a child when the International Hotel in downtown Puerto de Limón collapsed [[20]](https://www.upi.com/Archives/1991/04/22/Earthquake-rocks-Costa-Rica-Panama/9351672292800/). More broadly, the temblor caused widespread infrastructure damage. The Atlantic Railroad's tracks, particularly the vulnerable section spanning the Pacuare River, suffered severe damage that rendered the line unusable [[5]](https://ticotimes.net/2026/04/19/the-story-of-costa-ricas-famous-railroad-to-limon).

The Costa Rican government never undertook repairs. As of 2026, the extensive miles of iron tracks remain in disrepair, rusted, and abandoned. No service has been restored on this historic line [[12]](https://www.arcr.net/elresidente/fa-9-10-2001.htm). The railroad's operational death was permanent—not a temporary closure pending reconstruction, but a definitive end [[5]](https://ticotimes.net/2026/04/19/the-story-of-costa-ricas-famous-railroad-to-limon). The physical infrastructure, once the pride of Costa Rican engineering, became a lost legacy. Some sections are impassable; others have been scavenged for materials. Historical documentation exists, but no restoration projects have been initiated, and the line has not operated for 35 years as of 2026 [[13]](https://www.crcdaily.com/p/the-lost-legacy-of-costa-ricas-railroads) [[5]](https://ticotimes.net/2026/04/19/the-story-of-costa-ricas-famous-railroad-to-limon).

The destruction of the Atlantic Railroad represented the loss of not just physical infrastructure but also a century-long institutional practice. The earthquake severed a connection between the Central Valley and the Caribbean that had defined Costa Rican economic geography for longer than most of the nation's citizens had been alive.
