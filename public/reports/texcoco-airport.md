# Texcoco Airport: The Ambition, the Reversal, and What Remains of a Cancelled Megaproject

## Introduction: A Project Unfinished

In October 2018, Mexico abandoned one of the world's most ambitious airport projects, a \$13.5 billion facility designed by renowned British architect Norman Foster that was meant to triple Mexico City's aviation capacity. The cancellation came via a hastily organized public referendum and the political will of a newly elected president who had made opposition to the project a centerpiece of his campaign. Four years of work following the 2014 project announcement - with thousands of workers and billions of dollars invested - ended in a few days of voting that saw roughly 1.2 percent of Mexico's registered voters participate. [[1]](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2018/10/29/mexico-referendum-rejects-part-built-14bn-airport-project) [[2]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport-idUSKCN1N30EE/)

What followed was neither simple victory nor straightforward failure. The airport site was repurposed into a vast ecological restoration project; an alternative airport opened in its stead; and the question of whether Mexico's aviation crisis was actually solved remained unresolved even into 2026. The Texcoco project reveals not a clear narrative of corruption defeated or engineering triumph, but a complex intersection of geological realities, political transformation, indigenous resistance, and disputed evidence about what went wrong and whether it needed to go wrong at all.

## Part One: Why Texcoco? The Deeper Past and the Choice

To understand why a former lake bed seemed like Mexico's best option for a new international airport, one must first grasp how the site came to be available and why an earlier attempt to build there had failed violently.

### The Draining of Lake Texcoco

Lake Texcoco was once the enduring feature of the Valley of Mexico. For roughly two centuries before the Spanish conquest, it supported the Aztec city of Tenochtitlan, built on an island with sophisticated hydraulic engineering - chinampas (raised agricultural plots), dikes, and aqueducts that sustained a metropolis of perhaps 200,000 people. [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Texcoco) [[4]](https://www.swallowsnotes.com/blog/mexico-city-and-tenochtitlan-an-ancient-island-city-in-lake-texcoco-revealing-extraordinary-aztec-engineering) The Spanish conquest in 1521 destroyed that system. Colonial rebuilding and changes to water management disrupted the earlier hydraulic system, and major floods recurred.

Colonial authorities began major drainage works in 1607 under Enrico Martínez. Those works were part of a succession of drainage schemes, not a single Gran Canal project lasting more than three centuries. Later projects included the Gran Canal and, in the twentieth century, the deep-drainage system. The original text conflated the different works and their construction periods [[5]](https://www.willylogan.com/?p=2074) [[6]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/draining-lake-texcoco).

By 2014, when President Enrique Peña Nieto announced the Texcoco airport, more than 95 percent of the original Lake Texcoco surface area had been drained. What remained was largely salt marshes and alkali flats - land that appeared available without residential expropriations. This was crucial. Mexico had tried to build an airport in the Texcoco area in 2002 during Vicente Fox's presidency, and it had ended in violent confrontation.

### The Failed 2002 Airport and Indigenous Resistance

In October 2001, President Fox announced plans to build an airport on expropriated communal farmland. The government issued an expropriation decree on October 22, 2001, targeting 3,866 to 5,391 hectares across 13 communities, including San Salvador Atenco, Nexquipayac, and Santa Isabel Ixtapan. [[7]](https://www.nytimes.com/2002/08/03/world/mexico-drops-planned-airport-after-protests-from-peasants.html) [[8]](https://www.nacion321.com/gobierno/en-mexico-ya-se-cancelo-un-aeropuerto-en-la-zona-de-texcoco-esta-es-la-historia/)

Local peasants refused to sell. In July 2002, when state police (FARI) moved to enforce the expropriation, violence erupted. On July 11-12, 2002, police repressed demonstrators, detaining 15 and injuring more than 30. Peasants responded by taking 7 to 12 state officials hostage and blocking the Texcoco-Lechería highway for three days. [[9]](https://www.proceso.com.mx/nacional/2002/8/2/caso-atenco-el-fracaso-de-las-maniobras-politicas-68214.html) [[10]](https://www.proceso.com.mx/nacional/2002/7/25/la-leccion-de-la-tierra-las-mujeres-de-san-salvador-atenco-5087.html) [[11]](https://elpais.com/internacional/2002/07/14/actualidad/1026597603_850215.html)

The Frente de Pueblos en Defensa de la Tierra (FPDT) - Front of Peoples in Defense of the Land - formed to organize the resistance. By August 2, 2002, Fox's government capitulated and officially cancelled the airport project. [[12]](https://www.jornada.com.mx/2002/08/02/016n1pol.php?origen=index.html) But the FPDT did not disband. It would remain the organizational core of anti-airport sentiment for the next 16 years.

When Peña Nieto's team revived the airport idea in 2014, they located it on drained lake bottom. This avoided residential expropriations but not environmental destruction - and it did not eliminate the memory of 2002.

## Part Two: The Ambition Unveiled

### The 2014 Announcement

On September 2, 2014, President Enrique Peña Nieto announced the new Nuevo Aeropuerto Internacional de la Ciudad de México (NAICM). [[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City_Texcoco_Airport) [[14]](https://harvardpolitics.com/mexico-city-airports/) The formal justification was urgent: Mexico City's Benito Juárez International Airport (AICM), designed to handle 32 million passengers annually, was handling roughly 44 to 47 million passengers annually in the later 2010s - operating at 40 to 50 percent overcapacity. [[15]](https://www.routesonline.com/news/29/breaking-news/278076/mexico-city-bursting-at-the-seams-amid-capacity-constraints-/) Demand was growing at 6 percent annually, and forecasts predicted 50 million passengers by 2021. The airport had been formally declared saturated on September 29, 2014. Only two runways served the facility, with 99 boarding positions, and authorized operations of 61 flights per hour were regularly exceeded, placing pressure on scheduling and terminal capacity. The cited sources do not establish 180 actual flights per hour. [[16]](https://airport-technology.com/analysis/mexico-ambitious-airport-plans-hang-on-a-knife-edge) [[15]](https://www.routesonline.com/news/29/breaking-news/278076/mexico-city-bursting-at-the-seams-amid-capacity-constraints-/)

The current airport was hemmed in by thousands of urban buildings and could not be expanded. A new facility was not a luxury; it was presented as an economic necessity. IATA estimated that without it, Mexico would lose \$20 billion annually in economic activity by the mid-2030s. [[17]](https://www.as-coa.org/articles/update-battle-over-mexico-citys-new-airport)

### The Architects and the Vision

To design the new airport, the Mexican government held an international competition. The winning team was announced in 2014: Norman Foster, the renowned British architect and Pritzker Prize winner (1999), in collaboration with Fernando Romero, a Mexican architect and son-in-law of billionaire Carlos Slim. [[18]](https://www.tureforma.org/british-architect-lord-norman-foster-designs-mexico-citys-new-airport/) [[19]](https://www.archpaper.com/2014/09/unveiled-norman-foster-fernando-romero-team-up-to-design-mexico-citys-new-9-2-billion-airport/) [[20]](https://www.archdaily.com/544659/new-details-released-of-norman-foster-and-fernando-romero-s-designs-for-mexico-city-s-new-airport) [[21]](https://www.archdaily.com/544296/foster-romero-alliance-wins-competition-to-design-the-mexico-city-airport-expansion)

Foster's design was audacious. The terminal would be a single, continuous X-shaped structure with a lightweight gridshell spanning up to 170 meters - roughly three times the span of conventional airport terminals. [[19]](https://www.archpaper.com/2014/09/unveiled-norman-foster-fernando-romero-team-up-to-design-mexico-citys-new-9-2-billion-airport/) [[22]](https://www.dezeen.com/2018/10/30/foster-partners-fernando-romero-mexico-city-airport-nixed-public-vote/) The terminal would encompass 555,000 to 743,000 square meters (exact figures vary by source) and would feature natural ventilation, displacement cooling, and natural daylight throughout, with a target LEED Platinum certification. [[18]](https://www.tureforma.org/british-architect-lord-norman-foster-designs-mexico-citys-new-airport/) [[23]](https://www.dezeen.com/2018/08/22/foster-partners-fernando-romero-mexico-city-new-airport-referendum/)

The design was marketed as the world's most sustainable airport. The X shape was meant to evoke the letter in Mexico's name, while the form drew on traditional Mexican architecture. There would be no internal trains or tunnels; passengers would navigate the open floor plan. The entire terminal would be one vast, continuous enclosure - a design principle Foster called revolutionary. [[19]](https://www.archpaper.com/2014/09/unveiled-norman-foster-fernando-romero-team-up-to-design-mexico-citys-new-9-2-billion-airport/) [[23]](https://www.dezeen.com/2018/08/22/foster-partners-fernando-romero-mexico-city-new-airport-referendum/) [[22]](https://www.dezeen.com/2018/10/30/foster-partners-fernando-romero-mexico-city-airport-nixed-public-vote/)

### Scope and Cost

The airport would sit on 4,431 hectares (approximately 10,950 acres) of former lake bed, approximately 14 kilometers northeast of Mexico City's center. Phase 1 would include three runways with capacity for 50 to 70 million passengers annually, with an opening target of 2020 (later slipped to 2022). Full expansion would eventually include six runways and capacity for 120 to 125 million passengers per year - potentially making it the world's third-largest commercial airport. [[24]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Mexico_City_Texcoco_Airport) [[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City_Texcoco_Airport) [[18]](https://www.tureforma.org/british-architect-lord-norman-foster-designs-mexico-citys-new-airport/)

The initial cost estimate in 2014 was \$8.7 to \$10 billion, or 120 billion Mexican pesos. By 2018, as construction proceeded, the figure had escalated to \$13 to \$14.5 billion, or 300 billion pesos. Reported estimates varied in date, currency and scope. The stated 120-to-300-billion-peso change would be a 150% increase, not 70%, and should not be equated directly with a dollar-denominated change. [[25]](https://www.voanews.com/a/mexico-referendum-cancels-partly-built-13-billion-airport/4634726.html) [[26]](https://www.businessinsider.com/ap-mexico-referendum-nixes-partly-built-13-billion-airport-2018-10) [[27]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport/what-is-next-for-mexico-city-airport-after-mega-project-axed-idUSKCN1N51BQ/) [[28]](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/the-mexican-airport-conflict-thats-amlos-first-test/2018/10/26/7bd2caca-d8d6-11e8-8384-bcc5492fef49_story.html)

### The Organizations and Financing

The project was administered by Grupo Aeroportuario de la Ciudad de México (GACM), a state entity holding the airport concession. Carlos Slim, Mexico's richest billionaire with a net worth near \$64 billion and extensive business interests through Grupo Carso, emerged as the lead private contractor, with his company directing approximately \$3.9 billion worth of terminal and runway construction. [[29]](https://www.theguardian.com/cities/2017/jun/28/billionaire-airport-last-act-mexico-city-ruin-carlos-slim) Slim, then in his 70s, described the airport as likely his "last great act" in Mexico City. [[29]](https://www.theguardian.com/cities/2017/jun/28/billionaire-airport-last-act-mexico-city-ruin-carlos-slim)

Program and construction management fell to Parsons Corporation (prime contractor, with a 2.36 billion-peso contract for six years) in partnership with CH2M HILL, a major global engineering firm, and FOA Consultores, a Mexican local firm. [[30]](https://www.theconstructionindex.co.uk/news/view/parsons-and-ch2m-hill-join-mexico-city-airport-project) [[31]](https://expatnetwork.com/parsons-ch2m-hill-airport-project/) [[32]](https://www.globalconstructionreview.com/parsons-ch2m-hill-win-role-mexicos-massive-new-air/) Foster + Partners handled the architectural design in coordination with Netherlands Airport Consultants (NACO), Royal HaskoningDHV, Grupo TADCO, and Grupo SACMAG on infrastructure design. [[19]](https://www.archpaper.com/2014/09/unveiled-norman-foster-fernando-romero-team-up-to-design-mexico-citys-new-9-2-billion-airport/) [[33]](https://arquitecturaviva.com/works/new-international-airport-in-mexico-city)

Financing was structured through the Mexico City Airport Trust, which issued approximately \$6 billion in green bonds between 2016 and 2017, with a 60-40 split between government and private investment. [[34]](https://centreforaviation.com/analysis/reports/attractive-airport-infrastructure-deals-in-latin-america-part-2---mexico-city-airport-at-texcoco-211109)

## Part Three: Building on Difficult Ground

### The Geological Challenge

The site seemed available, but geologically it was extraordinarily difficult. Lake Texcoco's bed consists of "up to 500 meters of soft clays with void ratios around 6 and water content around 250 percent." [[35]](https://www.britishgeotech.org/event/new-mexico-city-airport-some-challenges-of-building-on-virgin-lake-texcoco-clays/) [[36]](https://vulcanhammer.net/2021/05/29/mexico-citys-surprising-crisis-the-city-is-sinking/) These are not firm foundations.

Mexico City and the Valley of Mexico have been subsiding - sinking - since the 1930s. The city has dropped more than 8 to 10 meters in less than a century as groundwater was extracted for the growing metropolitan population. [[37]](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2020JB020648) The airport-site engineering presentation described settlement near 20 centimetres per year in 2017, while regional satellite observations show that rates vary substantially across the city. NASA's 2026 map identified areas sinking more than 2 centimetres per month; it is not a uniform citywide rate. [[38]](https://www.wired.com/story/mexico-city-is-sinking-a-powerful-nasa-satellite-just-exposed-how-fast/) [[39]](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/us-indian-spacecraft-captures-mexico-city-subsidence/)

This subsidence is irreversible. As groundwater pressure drops, the clay grains in the lakebed compact and repack. There is no elastic rebound. Peer-reviewed research projected 150 or more additional years of settlement, with the possibility of 30 additional meters of subsidence in the former lake zone alone. [[37]](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2020JB020648)

Adding to this are fractures. By 2013, researchers had documented 868 fracture sites in the former lake zone - abrupt transitions where soft soils adjoin firmer ground, creating brittle failure zones that crack as differential subsidence occurs. [[40]](https://www.cfms-sols.org/sites/default/files/Actes/2921-2924.pdf) The 1985 earthquake exposed the severe seismic vulnerability associated with the city's soft-ground conditions. Amplification of shaking in lakebed deposits should not be simplistically described as liquefaction of the entire lakebed.

Groundwater quality was another concern, but the site's saline surface water and the deep aquifer are not interchangeable. The engineering presentation describes public-supply wells below 120 metres and complex clay, silt and sand layers. It does not demonstrate that polluted lake water simply drains into the aquifer supplying most of the city [[35]](https://www.britishgeotech.org/event/new-mexico-city-airport-some-challenges-of-building-on-virgin-lake-texcoco-clays/).

### The Engineering Response

The international engineering team was not ignorant of these challenges. On April 5, 2017, the British Geotechnical Association hosted a seminar explicitly titled "New Mexico City Airport: Some Challenges of Building on Virgin Lake Texcoco Clays." [[35]](https://www.britishgeotech.org/event/new-mexico-city-airport-some-challenges-of-building-on-virgin-lake-texcoco-clays/) Engineering firms proposed compensated foundation design for the 1.6-kilometer by 0.5-kilometer terminal - balancing the weight of the proposed structure against soil removed by excavation, alongside other measures to manage settlement. For the 89-meter control tower, a shallow piled mat foundation was considered. [[35]](https://www.britishgeotech.org/event/new-mexico-city-airport-some-challenges-of-building-on-virgin-lake-texcoco-clays/)

These were sophisticated engineering responses to a known challenge, not projects proceeding in ignorance of geological hazards. The team understood what they faced and proceeded with engineered solutions.

### Construction Progress

Work formally began in 2015. By 2016-2017, site preparation was well underway: a 33-kilometer perimeter fence, access roads, leveling of the 4,431-hectare site. The first concrete for the terminal was poured on August 25, 2017. [[14]](https://harvardpolitics.com/mexico-city-airports/)

By October 2018, when López Obrador announced the referendum, the project was approximately 30 to 40 percent complete. The airport had 21 foundation "funnels" (massive support structures) under construction. Terminal steelwork was partially erected. Runway bases were being prepared on two runways. The control tower had been excavated. Millions of tons of rock and fill had been quarried and transported to the site. [[25]](https://www.voanews.com/a/mexico-referendum-cancels-partly-built-13-billion-airport/4634726.html) [[41]](https://www.foxnews.com/travel/mexico-cancels-partially-built-13-billion-airport-after-referendum) [[42]](https://www.enr.com/articles/45812-13b-mexico-city-airport-project-apparently-axed-by-president-elect)

About \$5 billion had already been spent. All this work, all this material, all this engineering - and it was about to be abandoned.

## Part Four: The Rising Opposition

### The Frente de Pueblos and the Return of the Land Question

The same organization that had resisted the Fox-era airport proposal in 2002, the Frente de Pueblos en Defensa de la Tierra (FPDT), reactivated in 2014 as Peña Nieto's project moved forward. [[43]](https://planamayor.com.mx/resurge-frente-de-pueblos-en-defensa-de-la-tierra-de-atenco-exige-a-amlo-cancelar-aeropuerto-en-texcoco/) The FPDT's core grievance did not change: the airport represented theft of communal farmland (ejidos) expropriated without adequate compensation or consultation. [[44]](https://planamayor.com.mx/agreden-a-ejidatarios-de-atenco-que-se-oponen-al-aeropuerto-hay-15-lesionados/)

In the summer of 2018, as the presidential election approached, the FPDT mobilized. On July 12, 2018, the organization held a demonstration at López Obrador's transition team offices, delivering a dialogue invitation. Eleven days later, on July 23, 2018, they met with the transition team, headed by Javier Jiménez Espriú, who would become Secretary of Transportation. [[45]](https://lopezobrador.org.mx/temas/frente-de-pueblos-en-defensa-de-la-tierra-fpdt/)

In September 2018, as construction continued and the referendum neared, the campaign heated up. On September 11, 2018, approximately 100 armed persons attacked FPDT members who were blocking access to construction vehicles on the Pirámides-Texcoco highway. Around 300 campesinos responded by blocking the federal highway in protest. [[44]](https://planamayor.com.mx/agreden-a-ejidatarios-de-atenco-que-se-oponen-al-aeropuerto-hay-15-lesionados/)

On September 24, 2018, the FPDT launched a formal campaign: "#YoPrefieroElLago" - "I Prefer the Lake." [[46]](https://www.somoselmedio.com/atenco-lanza-campana-yo-prefiero-el-lago/) [[47]](https://forbes.com.mx/yoprefieroellago-la-campana-que-busca-frenar-el-aeropuerto-en-texcoco/) [[48]](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/metropoli/edomex/lanzan-campana-contra-construccion-de-nuevo-aeropuerto-yo-prefiero-el-lago/) The slogan expressed a preference for ecological restoration over airport construction. The campaign emphasized that the Texcoco airport would constitute an "ecocide", meaning severe ecological destruction, that would destroy Mexico's last significant wetland refuge in the Valley of Mexico. [[47]](https://forbes.com.mx/yoprefieroellago-la-campana-que-busca-frenar-el-aeropuerto-en-texcoco/) The lake provided habitat for migratory birds (Lake Nabor Carrillo nearby presented hazards like bird strikes that had damaged aircraft), and its restoration was framed as both cultural patrimony (honoring the Aztec past) and ecological necessity.

The campaign drew unlikely supporters. Artists including Rubén Albarrán of the band Café Tacvba, Dr. Shenka of Panteón Rococó, and Roco Pachucote of Maldita Vecindad lent their names and voices, along with more than 500 other intellectuals and activists. [[46]](https://www.somoselmedio.com/atenco-lanza-campana-yo-prefiero-el-lago/) [[47]](https://forbes.com.mx/yoprefieroellago-la-campana-que-busca-frenar-el-aeropuerto-en-texcoco/)

The opposition framed the choice starkly: continue draining the wetland and building an unsustainable airport on impossible geology, or restore an ecosystem and acknowledge indigenous land claims.

## Part Five: The Political Reversal

### López Obrador's Landslide and the Campaign Promise

On July 1, 2018, Andrés Manuel López Obrador (AMLO) won Mexico's presidential election with 53 percent of the vote - a landslide margin of 31 points and the first presidential majority since 1988. [[49]](https://www.latimes.com/world/mexico-americas/la-fg-mexico-election-20180630-story.html) López Obrador, a longtime leftist politician then aged 64, had run on a platform of radical change and the end of corruption. The airport had become a symbol of the old, corrupt order under Peña Nieto.

During his campaign, López Obrador had attacked the Texcoco project repeatedly, calling it a "bottomless pit of corruption" and declaring it "tainted by graft." [[50]](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-latin-america-46026860) [[27]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport/what-is-next-for-mexico-city-airport-after-mega-project-axed-idUSKCN1N51BQ/) [[51]](https://www.reuters.com/article/mexico-airport-idUSL2N1X906E/) He had promised that before taking office, he would hold a public referendum to let Mexicans decide the airport's fate. Though López Obrador had won the election, he would not take office until December 1, 2018. The referendum would occur before his inauguration, in what he framed as respecting public opinion before formalizing his position.

### The Referendum: Legitimate Consultation or Political Theater?

The referendum took place over four days, October 25-28, 2018. [[1]](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2018/10/29/mexico-referendum-rejects-part-built-14bn-airport-project) It was organized not by the National Electoral Institute (INE), Mexico's official electoral authority, but by López Obrador's Morena party, funded by party legislators' contributions totaling approximately \$78,000. [[2]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport-idUSKCN1N30EE/) [[52]](https://paxex.aero/2018/10/mexico-city-new-airport-canceled/)

The voting took place across 1,073 polling places in 538 of Mexico's 2,458 municipalities, with roughly 1 million ballots available. The final participation was approximately 1.06 million votes - less than 1 percent of Mexico's population of 129 to 130 million and fewer than 1.2 percent of the roughly 90 million eligible voters. For context: roughly 1 in 85 registered voters participated.

Reports documented problems with repeat voting and participant identification. These were concerns about the consultation's safeguards, not proof that every ballot or the overall count was fraudulent. The Arturo Rosenblueth Foundation helped organize and tally the consultation rather than the official electoral authority [[52]](https://paxex.aero/2018/10/mexico-city-new-airport-canceled/).

The ballot question itself may have been biased. Pre-referendum polling by El Financiero newspaper showed that when asked generically whether Mexico should build a new airport, voters favored it. But the actual referendum ballot presented a choice between continuing Texcoco construction or choosing the Santa Lucía military base alternative - a framing that appears to have nudged respondents toward the Santa Lucía option. [[17]](https://www.as-coa.org/articles/update-battle-over-mexico-citys-new-airport)

The result: roughly 70 percent favored the alternative to Texcoco, while roughly 30 percent favored continuing construction. [[53]](https://www.foxbusiness.com/markets/mexico-referendum-cancels-partly-built-13-billion-airport) [[52]](https://paxex.aero/2018/10/mexico-city-new-airport-canceled/)

### The October 29 Announcement and Political Commitment

On Monday, October 29, 2018 - the day after the consultation closed - López Obrador announced that the airport would be cancelled. [[1]](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2018/10/29/mexico-referendum-rejects-part-built-14bn-airport-project) [[2]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport-idUSKCN1N30EE/) He framed it as a triumph of democracy: "The decision taken by the citizens is democratic, rational and efficient," he stated. He added, "Corruption has ended." [[50]](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-latin-america-46026860) [[27]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport/what-is-next-for-mexico-city-airport-after-mega-project-axed-idUSKCN1N51BQ/)

The announcement shocked Mexico's financial markets. The Mexican peso dropped more than 3 percent against the US dollar, approaching the psychological barrier of 20 pesos per dollar. The Mexico City stock exchange's IPC index fell 4.2 percent - its largest single-day drop since the U.S. presidential election in November 2016. [[2]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport-idUSKCN1N30EE/) [[50]](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-latin-america-46026860)

Mexico's business community objected. The Confederation of Chambers of Commerce stated the decision should have been "technical and financial, not political." [[51]](https://www.reuters.com/article/mexico-airport-idUSL2N1X906E/) Airlines including Avianca and Copa expressed concerns about operating from multiple airports. [[54]](https://airinsight.com/aeromexico-takes-to-court-mexs-claimed-saturation/) IATA, the International Air Transport Association, explicitly opposed the cancellation. IATA's regional vice president Peter Cerda warned it would cost Mexico \$20 billion annually in economic losses. IATA CEO Alexandre de Juniac called the decision "extremely disappointing." [[17]](https://www.as-coa.org/articles/update-battle-over-mexico-citys-new-airport) IATA projected that the airport would have handled 20 million new passengers by 2035, contributing \$20 billion to future GDP. [[17]](https://www.as-coa.org/articles/update-battle-over-mexico-citys-new-airport)

Yet López Obrador held firm. The referendum, whatever its procedural limitations, gave him a mandate he chose to honor. On December 1, 2018, he took office as Mexico's president.

## Part Six: The Corruption Narrative

### The Claims Without Evidence

López Obrador's repeated characterization of Texcoco as a "bottomless pit of corruption" and "tainted by graft" became central to his political narrative. Reuters reported, however, that López Obrador made these allegations "without providing evidence" of specific fraud. [[27]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport/what-is-next-for-mexico-city-airport-after-mega-project-axed-idUSKCN1N51BQ/)

Cost escalation was one element of the political criticism, but differing budget baselines and currencies complicate the comparison. An increase in a budget does not itself demonstrate contractor kickbacks; that allegation would require contract-level evidence. But escalation in major infrastructure projects is not unusual, especially on challenging geology.

### Broader Context: Peña Nieto and Documented Corruption

The Peña Nieto administration (2012-2018) did face serious corruption allegations and some documented evidence. Emilio Lozoya, former head of Mexico's state oil company Pemex, testified in 2020 that Peña Nieto and Finance Minister Luis Videgaray had created a "corruption scheme" involving bribes from the Brazilian construction firm Odebrecht - payments to Lozoya and associates totaling over \$4 million - as well as schemes to buy votes in congress and finance future campaigns. [[55]](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/latino/former-mexico-president-enrique-pe-nieto-directed-corruption-says-ex-n1237443) [[56]](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-latin-america-53746715)

However, none of Lozoya's testimony specifically named Texcoco contracts or identified fraud mechanisms specific to the airport. [[55]](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/latino/former-mexico-president-enrique-pe-nieto-directed-corruption-says-ex-n1237443)

In July 2025, Mexico's Attorney General opened an investigation into alleged \$25 million bribes paid by the Israeli cybersecurity firm NSO Group (makers of the Pegasus spyware) to Peña Nieto to secure government contracts. Peña Nieto denied the allegations, the cited 2025 article does not establish a current charging status. [[57]](https://therecord.media/former-mexican-president-investigated-spyware-bribes)

### The Distinction: Allegations vs. Documentary Evidence

The research reveals a critical distinction: López Obrador's corruption allegations regarding Texcoco are well-documented rhetorically - he stated them repeatedly and publicly - but specific mechanisms of fraud, indictments, or detailed forensic audits of Texcoco-specific corruption are not present in accessible English-language sources. The cited material documents allegations concerning the Peña Nieto administration, but allegations and witness statements are not equivalent to final judicial findings, and they do not establish fraud in specific Texcoco contracts.

This does not mean no corruption occurred. It means the evidence for it - the specific fraud mechanisms, the indictments, the audit findings - remains either in Spanish-language sources not included in this research, in Mexican court records not yet public, or genuinely absent. López Obrador's claims may be accurate, or they may be political positioning. The available evidence does not conclusively resolve the question.

## Part Seven: The Alternative Airport

### The Pivot to Santa Lucía

Within days of announcing the Texcoco cancellation, López Obrador's team announced an alternative: an airport at the Santa Lucía military base in Zumpango, State of Mexico, approximately 45 kilometers north of the current Mexico City airport. This had been the second option in the referendum ballot. [[58]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport/mexican-president-says-new-airport-construction-to-start-next-week-idUSKCN1S02FT/) [[59]](https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/mexican-president-open-new-airport-three-years-after-scrapping-alternative-2022-03-21/)

Ground-breaking for the Felipe Ángeles International Airport (AIFA, named in honor of a Mexican Revolution military hero) occurred on October 17, 2019, after more than 100 legal appeals were cleared on October 16. [[58]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport/mexican-president-says-new-airport-construction-to-start-next-week-idUSKCN1S02FT/) [[60]](https://apnews.com/1e79d7df5efe436ca2ff5c7df3de6c72)

The cost was estimated at \$4.1 to \$4.15 billion - less than one-third of Texcoco's final cost. The design was not revolutionary; it involved converting an existing military base, adding two civilian runways to one existing military runway, and creating shared civil-military operations. [[58]](https://www.reuters.com/article/us-mexico-airport/mexican-president-says-new-airport-construction-to-start-next-week-idUSKCN1S02FT/) [[61]](https://english.elpais.com/international/2022-02-15/mexicos-new-international-airport-gets-ready-for-takeoff.html)

Some materials and equipment from Texcoco were designated for reuse at Santa Lucía, reducing the amount left without a purpose. That does not mean every material was recovered, every embedded cost was recouped, or transport and reuse were cost-free. The report has no comprehensive inventory supporting its original claim that nothing was wasted.

### AIFA Performance, 2022-2026

Felipe Ángeles International Airport opened on March 21, 2022. The opening date was not the centenary of General Felipe Ángeles' death; he died in November 1919. [[59]](https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/mexican-president-open-new-airport-three-years-after-scrapping-alternative-2022-03-21/) [[62]](https://www.nlarenas.com/en/2023/03/primer-aniversario-aifa/)

Initial performance fell short of government expectations. In its first year (March 2022 to March 2023), AIFA transported 1.46 million passengers - significantly below the government's projection of 2.5 million. This represented only 3.1 percent of Mexico City International Airport's (AICM's) traffic. [[63]](https://mexicobusiness.news/aerospace/news/aifa-has-fewer-passengers-government-expected) [[64]](https://mexicobusiness.news/aerospace/news/aifa-transported-105-million-passengers-opening)

Traffic subsequently increased, but totals accumulated since opening must be distinguished from calendar-year figures. The cited reports describe growing traffic and a three-million-passenger target for 2023; they do not establish that AIFA handled 2.6 million passengers during January-August 2023 alone [[65]](https://mexicobusiness.news/aerospace/news/aifa-aims-serve-3-million-passengers-2023) [[66]](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/aeromexico-increases-operations-aifa-40-002500151.html).

Routes expanded from opening offerings of 6 national and 1 international route to 14 national and 5 international routes by 2023. The airline roster grew: Aeromexico, VivaAerobus, and Volaris anchored domestic service; Conviasa (Venezuela), Arajet, and Copa Airlines provided regional and international options. In late 2023, Mexico's state-owned airline Mexicana de Aviación began operations. Earlier proposals for ten Boeing 737s and lower fares should not be reported as the actual delivered launch fleet. [[67]](https://www.britishaviationgroup.co.uk/knowledge/mexico-city-s-aeropuerto-internacional-felipe-ngeles-opened-one-year-ago/)

In May 2023, Aeromexico launched the first direct international service from AIFA to the United States, connecting to Houston via George Bush Intercontinental Airport. [[68]](https://aerocrewnews.com/2023/02/03/aeromexico-will-operate-the-first-route-between-the-aifa-and-the-united-states/) By mid-2023, Aeromexico had increased operations 40 percent, with more than 1,000 monthly flights planned; this should not automatically be interpreted as 1,000 departures. [[66]](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/aeromexico-increases-operations-aifa-40-002500151.html)

Government accounts reported improving operating performance as passenger and cargo activity grew. Operating surplus, treatment of public subsidies and recovery of construction costs are different measures, so these reports do not establish that AIFA had repaid its capital cost or achieved unsubsidized whole-project profitability. Later operating allocations and cargo-investment announcements likewise need their own dates and accounting scope [[69]](https://mexico-now.com/government-report-highlights-operation-of-felipe-angeles-airport/) [[70]](https://thebusinessyear.com/article/felipe-angeles-international-airpxort/).

### Persistent Challenges

Early reporting highlighted lengthy ground journeys and unfinished or limited public-transport connections. Those 2022 observations describe the opening period, not a verified inventory of transport services in September 2026. Access quality remained an important factor in the airport's appeal, but journey time varies with origin, mode and traffic [[71]](https://airlinegeeks.com/2022/03/09/mexico-city-s-new-felipe-angeles-airport-to-open-without-fixed-ground-transportation-services/) [[72]](https://pulsenewsmexico.com/2022/06/02/mexicos-new-aifa-airport-continues-with-challenges/).

The multi-airport system required coordinated airspace design, and pilots raised safety concerns in the early operating period. The airports' geographic separation alone does not prove their operations inherently incompatible, and a claimed increase in go-arounds does not by itself identify its cause. Mexico's FAA Category 2 rating concerned national safety oversight, not a determination that these two airports were too close together. The FAA restored Category 1 on September 14, 2023 [[73]](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/federal-aviation-administration-returns-mexico-highest-aviation-safety-status) [[74]](https://ops.group/blog/mexico-city-safety-concerns/).

Most critically, AICM saturation remained unresolved. The original airport continued to operate at or near capacity through 2023, even as AIFA grew. The three-airport metropolitan system (AICM, AIFA, and the smaller Toluca airport) was less efficient than the single-hub system Texcoco would have provided. Mexico City's aviation capacity problem was distributed rather than solved.

## Part Eight: The Site Transformed

### The Ecological Park

On August 30, 2024, President López Obrador inaugurated the Parque Ecológico Lago de Texcoco (PELT) - Lake Texcoco Ecological Park - on the site of the abandoned airport. The 14,000-hectare park (35,000 acres, roughly 21 times the size of Mexico City's famous Bosque de Chapultepec and more than twice Manhattan's land area) represented a radical repurposing of the airport site. [[75]](https://simpleflying.com/mexico-city-texcoco-airport-national-park/) [[76]](https://simpleflying.com/one-good-thing-mexico-city-abandoned-13-billion-texcoco-airport-project/) [[77]](https://www.casadechiles.com/texcoco-lake-park-challenges-progress/) [[78]](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/02/13/1067854/lake-texcoco-ancient-lake-unfinished-airport-mexico-city/)

The project was led by landscape architect Iñaki Echeverría, a 20-year advocate for Texcoco restoration who had studied the site's ecological and historical dimensions extensively. [[78]](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/02/13/1067854/lake-texcoco-ancient-lake-unfinished-airport-mexico-city/)

The park received formal protection: in March 2022, the Mexican government declared it a Protected Natural Area; in June 2022, it gained Ramsar Wetland designation (reference 2469), recognizing it as a wetland of international importance; and in March 2025, UNESCO designated it an Ecohydrology Demonstration Site. [[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Texcoco_Ecological_Park)

### Restoration Features

The restoration strategy involved returning the site to water. Nine rivers - Papalotla, Coxcacoaco, and Texcoco among them - that had been diverted during airport construction were rerouted back to the site, along with flows returned from the Dren General del Valle (Main Drainage Canal). [[77]](https://www.casadechiles.com/texcoco-lake-park-challenges-progress/)

Seasonal shallow pools covering 723 hectares were created, fed by rainwater and natural flows. Levees of volcanic rock bounded the water. Echeverría noted that the massive concrete terminal footprints left by airport excavation unexpectedly became retention basins, collecting and holding water naturally. [[77]](https://www.casadechiles.com/texcoco-lake-park-challenges-progress/)

The ecological targets were ambitious: 9,000 hectares of restored wetlands; 250 or more flora species; 370 fauna species; 276 bird species; 14 reptiles; 5 amphibians; and 29 mammals. [[80]](https://www.wired.com/story/rainfall-buries-a-mega-airport-in-mexico/) [[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Texcoco_Ecological_Park)

Early results exceeded expectations. Within two weeks of the 2025 rainy season - the heaviest rainfall in more than 80 years - nine bird nests were documented in a seven-hectare test area. Herons, shorebirds, and migratory waterfowl colonized Lake Nabor Carrillo adjacent to the park, with one report noting the lake "grew blue again." [[80]](https://www.wired.com/story/rainfall-buries-a-mega-airport-in-mexico/) [[81]](https://spacedaily.com/d-mexico-city-is-slowly-sinking-into-the-lake-it-tried-to-erase-the-ancient-bed-of-lake-texcoco-where-tenochtitlan-once-stood-and-where-the-modern-capital-now-collapses-centimetre-by-centimet/)

The restoration was intended to improve wetland habitat, water management and local employment. The cited reporting does not provide an auditable method for the asserted 1.5 million tonnes of annual carbon sequestration, so that figure should not be treated as an established outcome.

### Ongoing Tensions

However, restoration came with unresolved contradictions. The site hosted Sosa Texcoco S.A., a historical salt-industry operation associated with the spiral evaporation structure known as El Caracol. Historical industrial output must not be presented as verified current annual production. [[77]](https://www.casadechiles.com/texcoco-lake-park-challenges-progress/) Squatter settlements persisted in the El Caracol area. Land disputes continued with farmers from Texcoco, Atenco, and Chimalhuacán who claimed expropriated ejidos (communal lands) and demanded compensation. [[77]](https://www.casadechiles.com/texcoco-lake-park-challenges-progress/)

Protected-area designation governs land use; it does not by itself settle ownership, compensation or every outstanding agrarian claim. Proposals for agricultural activity within the wider landscape should be distinguished from a legal determination that land can never be returned.

Political vulnerability remains. Anthropologist Gabriela González, director of the Natural Resources Protection Area, cautioned that legal declarations alone are insufficient for long-term survival. Claudia Sheinbaum was elected on June 2, 2024. A future administration might seek changes, but could not be described as automatically free of the protected area's legal constraints. [[77]](https://www.casadechiles.com/texcoco-lake-park-challenges-progress/)

### Architectural and Historical Legacy

Norman Foster and Fernando Romero's Texcoco airport design, despite being cancelled before completion, received the Rethinking the Future Awards in 2021, honored for pioneering "a new concept for large-span, single airport enclosure." [[82]](https://www.constructionbriefing.com/news/scrapped-mexico-airport-project-awarded-international-prize/8013274.article) The unbuilt terminal design did not become AIFA's terminal design. AIFA involved substantial new civilian facilities at an existing military base, not merely the reuse of an unchanged military terminal.

The restoration of Lake Texcoco invoked historical layering. Historians and activists noted the site represents "laminated histories": the pre-Columbian hydraulic system engineered by the Aztecs, the colonial-era drainage undertaken by Spanish engineers like Enrico Martínez, the 20th-century subsidence caused by aquifer extraction and urban sprawl, and the 21st-century restoration attempting to undo centuries of erasure. [[78]](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/02/13/1067854/lake-texcoco-ancient-lake-unfinished-airport-mexico-city/) [[4]](https://www.swallowsnotes.com/blog/mexico-city-and-tenochtitlan-an-ancient-island-city-in-lake-texcoco-revealing-extraordinary-aztec-engineering)

The Frente de Pueblos en Defensa de la Tierra connected the airport dispute with longer struggles over land, water and community participation. The cited archive does not verify the report's attributed 2020 quotation naming historical figures and designers, so it should not be presented as a verbatim statement [[45]](https://lopezobrador.org.mx/temas/frente-de-pueblos-en-defensa-de-la-tierra-fpdt/).

## Part Nine: Unresolved Questions

The cancellation left several questions open: whether the multi-airport system would provide adequate capacity, how easy it would be for passengers to use, and whether its combined costs and benefits would compare favorably with the abandoned hub. Early utilization figures alone cannot establish the answers as of September 2026.

The corruption narrative, despite its political power, remains partially unresolved. López Obrador's claims about graft are documented; the specific mechanisms and indictments are not - at least not in publicly available English-language sources. Allegations about Odebrecht payments and political vote-buying form part of the wider political context but do not prove specific Texcoco fraud.

The geological challenges that made Texcoco difficult - subsidence, groundwater concerns and unusually challenging soil conditions - were real and well-understood by engineers. Whether they made the project genuinely impossible or merely expensive and complex remains contested. Foster's design included compensated foundations and other sophisticated engineering responses.

The land claims of indigenous and peasant communities remain unresolved. The FPDT's mobilization helped stop successive airport proposals, but cancellation, ecological designation and resolution of individual land claims are separate processes. The report's sources do not establish that no property or rights were restored in any affected community.

And the ecological restoration, while impressive and unprecedented in scale, is politically vulnerable. A future government could reverse course and repurpose the restored wetlands. Domestic protected-area rules and international ecological recognition support conservation in different ways. Their legal effects should not be conflated, nor should future policy changes be treated as unconstrained by law.

## Conclusion: The Ambition Remains Unfinished

The Texcoco airport represents a particular kind of ending - not a simple failure, but an interruption. A technical vision by renowned architects; a geological challenge met with engineering sophistication; a political moment when a newly elected president made opposition to a megaproject the centerpiece of his mandate; decades of indigenous and environmental resistance finally achieving political voice; and an alternative hastily assembled that neither fully solves the capacity crisis nor meets the vision of the original.

What remains is not a clear lesson but a set of tensions: between ambitious engineering and geological reality; between political legitimacy and procedural irregularity; between corruption allegations and documentary evidence; between capacity relief and sustained bottlenecks; between ecological restoration and unresolved land claims.

Parts of a lake system managed by the Mexica and extensively drained through colonial and modern engineering are being restored. But the question of who owns it, whether it can be sustained, and whether Mexico's aviation crisis has truly been addressed all remain open. The Texcoco airport is cancelled, but its problems have not disappeared. They have only been redistributed - across three airports, across damaged ecosystems, and across communities still waiting for resolution.
