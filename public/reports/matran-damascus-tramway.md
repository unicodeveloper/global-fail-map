# The Matran Tramway Concession

The earliest documented failed transport project in Damascus involves what should have been the city's first modern tramway system. On October 21, 1887, a local merchant named Yusuf Matran petitioned Ottoman authorities for permission to build a horse-drawn tramway network that would connect central Damascus to suburban neighborhoods. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Damascus) His vision extended from Marjeh Square—the traditional commercial center—through the Al-Midan quarter to the south, with branches reaching into Al-Salihiyeh and Douma to the north and northeast. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Damascus)

The Ottoman administration granted Matran's concession in 1889, providing exclusive rights to construct and operate the tramway system within Damascus. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Damascus) This appeared to be a straightforward modernization project in line with Ottoman infrastructure ambitions of the period. But the concession proved controversial. The Ottoman authorities, as a condition of the monopoly grant, required that Matran widen all streets along the proposed tramway route to a minimum of nine meters to accommodate the tracks and ensure public safety. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Damascus)

This width requirement became the fatal constraint. Matran's company refused to comply with the street-widening mandate, which would have required extensive property acquisition and compensation to hundreds of property owners in the dense urban quarters affected by the project. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Damascus) In July 1903—sixteen years after the original petition and fourteen years after the concession grant—the Ottoman administration formally revoked Matran's monopoly. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Damascus) The company had never laid a single rail.

## What Survived and What Followed

Though Matran's venture failed entirely, the concept of a Damascus tramway did not die with his revoked concession. Ottoman officials recognized that the transportation need was real, and the regulatory framework could be made workable with different ownership. Within four years, a new actor emerged: a Belgian investment consortium. Where Matran had stumbled over the modernization requirements, the Belgian company accepted them. The first operational tram lines opened in February 1907, running initially along Al-Midan and Al-Jisr Al-Abyad routes. [[2]](https://www.newarab.com/features/damascus-tramway-golden-age-syrias-modern-history) [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trams_in_Damascus) The Belgian-owned Damascus Electricity and Tramway Company would eventually expand to operate an extensive network across the city, operating successfully until 1962.

The Matran case demonstrates how regulatory conditions, rather than technical infeasibility or fundamental lack of demand, can derail infrastructure projects. The streets were narrow by modern standards, and widening them was expensive—but not impossible. The Ottoman administration's insistence on modernization standards was itself forward-thinking; the failure lay in Matran's refusal to accept the cost and complexity of compliance.
